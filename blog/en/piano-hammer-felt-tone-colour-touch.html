Piano Hammers and Felt: How Touch Becomes Tone

Music theory · 2026-11-14

When a pianist presses a key, a small hammer covered in felt flies toward a string, strikes it and falls back. That moment of contact decides much of what the listener hears. The felt on the hammer acts like a cushion and a filter at the same time. How firm or soft it is changes the brightness of the tone, and the way the pianist moves the key changes how fast the hammer arrives.

What the hammer does

The hammer does not stay on the string. It strikes and rebounds so that the string can vibrate freely. If the hammer stayed in contact, it would damp the sound.

The felt shapes the strike

A soft surface touches the string gently and gives a rounder tone. A harder surface makes a sharper contact and emphasises the higher overtones. Between the two lies the typical range of a well-prepared piano.

Speed controls loudness

A faster hammer produces a louder note. The pianist cannot control the hammer after it leaves the mechanism, so the speed at release is what counts.

Why touch changes tone colour

Pianists often speak about colour, even though each note is only a struck string. The reason is that speed and the way the hammer meets the string alter the balance of overtones.

  • A quick, firm attack tends to sound brighter.
  • A slow, gentle attack tends to sound rounder.
  • Layering notes with different weights in a chord changes which voice is heard.
  • Sustained notes ring differently depending on how many strings are free to vibrate.

What technicians pay attention to

Instrument technicians work with the hammers as a normal part of care. Over time felt compresses where it strikes the string, and the tone can become brighter or harsher. Technicians can adjust the surface, a process called voicing, to bring the tone back toward the intended character. It is careful work and best left to a trained person.

  • Hammers wear with playing, so the sound changes slowly.
  • Humidity can affect felt and, with it, the tone.
  • Voicing is different from tuning, which concerns pitch.

What a learner can listen for

You can train the ear to notice the link between touch and tone without any technical knowledge.

One note, many speeds

Play a single key several times, from very soft to moderately firm. Listen for how the colour changes as well as the volume.

A chord with one voice highlighted

Play a chord, then repeat it with the top note slightly louder. Hear how the melody seems to appear.

Quiet playing with control

Soft playing needs precise control, not timid pressing. Try a slow, even descent into the key.

Three small questions before you play a phrase

Before you start a phrase, ask what character it has. Is it singing, dancing or speaking quietly? Then ask which finger speed and arm weight would produce that character, and which note in the chord should lead. Finally, listen to the last note of the phrase and let it die away before you move on. These questions take only a few seconds, and they turn a technical fact about hammers and felt into a musical decision.

FAQ

Q: Why does one piano sound brighter than another?

A: Hammers, strings, the room and the technician's voicing all contribute.

Q: Does a digital piano have hammers?

A: Many have a keyboard mechanism that imitates the feel, but the sound comes from samples or models, not felt hitting strings.

Q: Can I change the tone of my piano myself?

A: It is safer to ask a qualified technician, because felt can be damaged.

Q: Does touch really change tone on a piano?

A: It does, through the speed and manner of the strike, although the effect is subtler than on some other instruments.
