A Simple Practice Log: What to Write After Each Session

Practice tips · 2026-11-20

A practice log is a small notebook or file where you write a few short notes after each piano session. Five items are enough: the date, what you practised, what went well, what needs work and the first thing to do next time. It takes about two minutes, yet it gives your next session a clear start and lets you see progress over weeks. If you ever feel that practice is going nowhere, a log often shows that it is not.

Why a log helps

Memory is unreliable. After a busy day, it is easy to forget which bar was tricky or which fingering you decided on. A log holds that information for you. It also turns vague feelings into specific facts, such as "left hand slips in bar nine", which are far easier to fix.

The five lines

Keep the format the same every time so that writing is quick.

  • Date and time spent: just a rough note.
  • What I played: pieces, scales or exercises.
  • What went well: one small success.
  • What to fix: one or two precise spots.
  • First task next time: the very first thing to try.

Aim for short phrases. A page full of writing is more likely to be abandoned than a few lines.

Paper or phone?

Both work. Paper is calm and hard to ignore on the music stand. A phone note or a shared document is easy to search later. Choose whichever you will actually use. Some learners keep a paper notebook at the piano and photograph a page now and then.

Use it to plan the week

Read back over your log on one day each week. Notice patterns: perhaps the same passage appears in "what to fix" three times. That tells you to change the method, for example by practising hands separately or at half speed. Notice also what keeps appearing under "what went well", and let that give you confidence.

Make small wins visible

When you tick off something that once felt hard, write it down in a different colour. Seeing those marks over a month can be more motivating than any outside praise. The log is a private record, so you can be honest and specific.

Sharing with a teacher

If you have a teacher, bring the log to the lesson. It shows what happened between lessons and saves time. A teacher can see at a glance where your practice is working and where it needs another approach. If you learn on your own, the log still helps you ask clearer questions in a forum or with an app.

When practice goes badly

Every learner has sessions where nothing seems to work. Write those down too, calmly and without blame: "tired, only ten minutes, scales only". A log that includes ordinary and difficult days is more truthful, and it helps you see that bad days pass. It can also show you that a pattern, such as late-evening practice, is not working, so that you can try a different time of day.

Keeping the habit

Put the notebook where you can see it, and write before you stand up from the bench. If you miss a day, simply carry on. A log is a helper, not a score, so there is no need to feel guilty about gaps.

A sample entry

To show how short a good entry can be, imagine this made-up example: "Tuesday, 20 minutes. Played C major scale and the first page of the new piece. Right hand was even. Left hand rushes in bar six. Next time: left hand alone at half speed, then join." That is only a few lines, yet it answers every question tomorrow's session will have. Notice that it names a bar and a method, so there is no need to guess what to do first.

FAQ

Q: How long should each entry be?

A: Two minutes or less. A few lines are enough.

Q: Should I record mistakes?

A: Yes, but be specific, for example the bar number, so that the note leads to an action.

Q: Is a log useful for beginners?

A: Very much. Beginners often forget small instructions, and the log keeps them.

Q: Can I use a spreadsheet?

A: Yes. A simple table with the five headings works well if you like digital tools.
