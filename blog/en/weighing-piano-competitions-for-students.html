Should Your Student Enter a Piano Competition? Weighing It Up

Practice tips · 2026-11-11

Piano competitions attract many students and families. They offer a goal, a chance to perform and feedback from adjudicators. For some students, preparing for a competition brings focus and growth. For others, the pressure and comparison can take the joy out of music. Deciding whether a student should enter is a decision for the teacher, student and family together, based on the student's personality, readiness and goals rather than on the prestige of a result.

What competitions can offer

When chosen well, competitions can provide:

  • a clear deadline that motivates careful preparation
  • performance experience in front of unfamiliar listeners
  • written or spoken feedback from experienced adjudicators
  • exposure to other players and repertoire at a similar level
  • a sense of achievement when a demanding piece is completed

These benefits do not depend on winning. Students often remember the friendships made backstage, the pieces they heard other players perform and the moment they walked onto an unfamiliar stage, long after they have forgotten the scores. Many students gain the most from the preparation process itself.

Possible downsides

Competitions also have costs:

  • intense focus on one or two pieces can narrow a student's repertoire for months
  • the comparison with other players can feel discouraging, especially for sensitive students
  • entry fees, travel and accompanying costs add up
  • disappointing results can affect motivation if expectations were not managed

Recognising these risks in advance helps families decide with open eyes, and it allows the teacher to plan support, such as extra performance practice or a lighter workload in other areas, before the pressure builds.

Questions to ask before entering

Why does the student want to enter?

Interest from the student is essential. Entering only because others do, or to please adults, rarely leads to a positive experience.

Is the student ready?

The student should be able to perform the required pieces securely well before the event, with time left for polishing.

How does the student handle performing?

A student who enjoys recitals may find competitions exciting. One who finds performing very difficult may benefit from smaller steps first, such as informal concerts.

What are the rules and costs?

Read the requirements carefully: repertoire lists, time limits, age categories, fees and travel needs.

Choosing the right competition

Competitions vary widely, from local festivals with friendly feedback to formal events with strict requirements. For a first experience, a festival-style event that emphasises comments over rankings is often a gentle starting point. Look for events with clear rules, transparent judging criteria and a reputation for respectful care of young performers.

Preparing without losing balance

Plan a timeline that allows the piece to be learned securely, polished and performed several times in lower-pressure settings before the event. Keep some other music in lessons, such as sight-reading, technique and a fun piece, so the student's development stays broad. Practise the stage routine: walking on, adjusting the bench, pausing before starting and bowing.

Managing expectations

Parents play a large part here. Their reactions before and after the event shape how the student remembers the experience, so it helps when the whole family agrees on what a good day looks like. Before the event, discuss what success means. Goals such as playing with confidence, managing nerves or performing a passage well are within the student's control, while results are not. After the event, focus first on what went well, then review the adjudicator's comments calmly and choose one or two points to work on.

Alternatives to competitions

Students who want goals without competitive pressure can choose from many options: graded exams, studio recitals, community performances, recording projects or ensemble playing. Each offers motivation and performance experience in a different form. A balanced musical path may include competitions at some stages and other goals at others.

FAQ

Q: At what age can students enter piano competitions?

A: Many events have categories for young children, but readiness and interest matter more than age.

Q: Do competitions help students improve?

A: The preparation and feedback can help, especially when the student is motivated and well supported.

Q: What if a student is disappointed with the result?

A: Focus on personal goals achieved, review feedback calmly and remember that results reflect one day.

Q: Are there gentler alternatives?

A: Yes. Festivals with feedback, exams, studio concerts and recordings offer goals with less comparison.
