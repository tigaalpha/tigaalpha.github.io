Marking Phrase Ends in a Piano Score Before You Play

Practice tips · 2026-11-12

A piano phrase has a place where the line arrives, even if the next phrase begins at once. If those arrivals are not marked, the hands often push through and the ear never gets a close. Marking phrase ends in the score before you play is a small study job: you decide where a thought finishes, you write a clear sign, and you practise arriving there with tone and time, not only with the last correct pitch. The work belongs at the keyboard and in the copy, not as a vague idea of "playing musically."

Find the close by singing and by looking at the harmony

Read a few bars and sing or hum the melody without playing. Notice where you would take a breath if this were a sung line. Then check the bass and the harmony: a cadence, a long note, or a rest often confirms the same place. If melody and harmony disagree by a beat, mark the later of the two arrivals and test both at a slow tempo. Write the mark in pencil, close to the bar, so it remains visible when the page is on the stand.

Use a small set of marks and keep them consistent

A tick, a comma, or a small square bracket at the end of the phrase is enough. Use the same sign through the piece so you do not invent a new code on every page. If one phrase is a question and the next is an answer, you can add a light Q or A beside the mark, but only if that helps you play the close, not as decoration. Avoid crowding the staff with extra words. The mark should tell you "arrive here" in a single glance.

Practise arriving at the mark, then leaving it

Play only as far as the first marked close and stop, holding the last sound for its full written value. Listen to whether the tone finishes or is cut off by a hurried lift. Then play from the same start, arrive, and continue into the next phrase without a bump. If the join is rough, the arrival may be too short, or the next phrase may start too loudly. Loop the close and the first bar of the next phrase until both jobs are clear. A phrase mark that is never practised as an arrival will not change the playing.

When to keep the marks and when to take them out

Keep the marks while the piece is still being learnt, and during any sitting where the line has begun to run on. Once the arrivals are part of the playing at a fluent tempo, some marks can come out so the page is cleaner for a run or a lesson. Leave a mark at a close that still fails, or at a page that is rarely practised. The aim is not a permanently decorated score, but a line that knows where it finishes.

  • Sing or hum the line and mark the place you would breathe.
  • Confirm the close against the bass and the cadence in those bars.
  • Use one pencil sign consistently through the copy.
  • Practise arriving at the mark with a planned length, then go on.
  • Erase a mark only after the arrival is secure at a moving tempo.

FAQ

Q: What if the score already has phrase marks printed?

A: Use those as a starting point, and add a pencil sign only where the printed curve is easy to miss or where a close is unclear.

Q: Should every phrase end with a silence?

A: No. Some closes are joined to the next thought. The mark still means "arrive," even if the sound continues into the next bar.

Q: Can I mark phrases only in the right hand?

A: Mark the melody first, then check whether the left hand also needs a close, especially if the bass has its own cadence.

Q: How large should the sign be?

A: Large enough to see on the stand, small enough not to hide pitch or rhythm. A short tick is often enough.

Q: What if two editions mark phrases in different places?

A: Choose one copy for practice, mark the closes you have tested at the piano, and stay with that plan until you have a reason to revise it.
