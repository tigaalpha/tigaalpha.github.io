Choosing Piano Exam Pieces Early Enough to Practise Them Properly

News · 2026-11-12

Choosing piano exam pieces is musical work, not only an administrative box to tick. The writing has to suit the learner's present hands, and there has to be enough calendar time for slow practice, for memory if it is required, and for run-throughs that include the endings. A late choice leaves no room for a fingering that fails, or for a movement that turns out to be heavier than it looked on the page. This article is about choosing early enough to practise properly. It does not name exam boards, and it does not treat a syllabus as a race.

Read the current list and test the writing at the piano

Start from the official list that applies to this year and this grade or level, and look at more than one option in each required group if there is a choice. Then sit at the piano and play the openings, the hardest-looking page, and the last cadence. A piece that looks short on paper may have a dense texture or an awkward leap. A piece that looks long may be built from patterns the learner already knows. Testing at the keyboard is the only reliable check. Do not choose from a title or a famous recording alone.

Test the hard page at the piano before you settle the set

Leave calendar time for slow work, not only for later run-throughs

Count back from the exam date, or from the date a teacher wants the pieces secure, and ask whether there are enough weeks for hands-separate work, for joins, and for a period in which the tempo can rise in small steps. If there is also a technical test or a quick-study task, those need time of their own. A choice made only a few weeks before a date often forces the later sittings to skip the slow stage. Write the plan in a diary so the family can see why an early decision matters.

Match the set to this learner, not to a neighbour's programme

A set that worked for one learner may not fit another, even at the same level. Consider hand span, reading, and how much contrast the three or four pieces give as a group. A programme in which every piece is loud and fast is tiring; a programme in which every piece is slow may not show enough of the learner's playing. Teachers can help by offering two or three tested options rather than a single "must do" title.

Once a set is chosen, short takes in TIGA ALPHA of the hardest page of each piece can become a weekly check, so the early weeks are spent on real writing rather than on deciding again.

  • Use the current official list, and test openings, hard pages, and endings at the piano.
  • Count back from the date so slow work and later run-throughs both have weeks.
  • Choose a set that fits this learner, with contrast between the pieces.
  • Do not choose from a title or a recording alone.
  • Allow a short trial period, and change a piece only while time remains for the replacement.

Review the choice after a trial period, while there is still time

After two or three weeks of honest work, ask whether a piece is still a reasonable fit. If a movement is clearly beyond the time available, it is better to change it while weeks remain than to keep it out of habit. Any change should still leave enough time for the replacement. Families should not treat the first week of looking at titles as the end of the decision; the trial at the piano is part of choosing. The aim is a set that can be practised properly, which is the only useful meaning of "early enough."

FAQ

Q: How early is early enough?

A: Early enough that slow practice, joins, and a later period of running the pieces can all fit before the date. The exact number of weeks depends on the level and the learner.

Q: Should a learner always pick the "easier" option in a list?

A: Choose a piece that can be practised well in the time available, and that still suits the hands. Difficulty on the page is only one factor; texture and length matter too.

Q: What if the official list changes?

A: Check the list that applies to the date of the exam, and test again if a chosen piece is no longer allowed.

Q: Can a teacher choose all the pieces?

A: A teacher can propose options, but the learner still needs to try them at the piano. A piece that has not been tested is not yet chosen.

Q: Is it useful to copy last year's popular set?

A: Only if those pieces still fit this learner and this year's list. Another person's successful set is not a plan by itself.
