Dust Covers and Keyboard Care in a Piano Studio

News · 2026-11-12

A teaching studio may have several pianos in rooms that also see bags, water bottles, and a stream of different hands. Dust covers and a short care routine are not decoration. They keep grit off the keys, they reduce the chance of a spill reaching the action, and they make the next lesson start on an instrument that feels cared for. The routine should be simple enough that teachers and learners will actually follow it between lessons, and it should leave deeper work — regulation, voicing, repairs — to a technician.

Cover the keys when a room will stand empty

If a piano has a fallboard, close it gently at the end of a lesson or at the end of the day. If the instrument is a grand that stays on a short stick, a fitted cover or a key cover can protect the keys when the room will be empty for some hours. Do not use a rough cloth that sheds, and do not drag a cover across the finish. A cover is not a table: nothing should be stacked on it except, if the design allows, a closed score that will be removed before playing.

Keep a no-liquid rule that is easy to see

Place a small sign or a spoken reminder at the start of term: bottles stay on a side table, not on the keybed, the lid, or the music stand. A closed bottle still spills. If a spill happens, lift any liquid at once, keep it from running into the keys, and call a technician if anything has gone inside the instrument. Studio staff should know where cloths are kept. This is ordinary instrument care, not a chemistry lesson.

Wipe the keys in a way that does not harm the surface

Dust the keys with a soft, dry cloth, following the direction of the keys, when the room is tidied. Do not use household cleaners, oils, or wet wipes on ivory, plastic, or wood unless a technician has recommended a specific method for this instrument. Hands should be reasonably clean before a lesson; a learner coming from a snack can wash first. Between those simple steps and a regular tuning visit, most studio pianos stay playable.

Give one person a short end-of-day check

Assign someone — a teacher finishing the last lesson, or a staff member — to walk the rooms and confirm that lids or covers are in place, benches are pushed in, and no cups have been left behind. A written Friday check can include a note if a key has begun to stick, so a technician can be booked before the next busy day. Care then becomes a studio habit, which is more reliable than hoping each visiting family will think of it.

FAQ

Q: Can I use a baby wipe on plastic keys?

A: Do not improvise with household wipes. Ask a technician what is safe for this instrument, and use a dry cloth for ordinary dust.

Q: Should every piano have a fitted cover?

A: A fallboard may be enough on an upright that is closed after use. A grand in a busy room often benefits from a key cover or a fitted cover when idle.

Q: What if a learner always brings a water bottle?

A: Provide a place for it away from the instrument, and repeat the rule calmly at the start of term. The rule is about the piano, not about forbidding a drink in the building.

Q: How often should a studio piano be tuned?

A: Follow the advice of the technician and the room's climate. Dust covers do not replace tuning or regulation.

Q: Who is responsible if a spill reaches the action?

A: Follow the studio's written room rules. Immediate wiping and a prompt call to a technician matter more than blame in the first minutes.
