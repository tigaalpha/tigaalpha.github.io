Guide-Tone Lines on Piano: Thirds and Sevenths That Connect

Music theory · 2026-11-11

In any seventh chord, two notes do most of the work of telling the ear what kind of chord it is: the third and the seventh. These are called guide tones. The root names the chord and the fifth adds firmness, but the third shows major or minor and the seventh shows the chord's colour. When you trace the guide tones from one chord to the next, you often find that they move by only a step or stay the same. This creates smooth lines that can be used for voicing, accompaniment and improvisation.

Finding the guide tones

Take a simple ii-V-I progression in C major: D minor 7, G7, C major 7.

  • D minor 7: third is F, seventh is C
  • G7: third is B, seventh is F
  • C major 7: third is E, seventh is B

Now look at how they connect. The seventh of D minor 7 (C) moves down a semitone to the third of G7 (B). The third of D minor 7 (F) stays the same and becomes the seventh of G7. From G7 to C major 7, the seventh (F) falls a semitone to the third of C (E), and the third (B) stays as the seventh of C major 7.

This pattern, where thirds and sevenths swap roles and move by small steps, is the heart of guide-tone movement.

Two lines from one progression

Guide tones form two parallel melodic lines. In the example above:

Upper line: C, B, B. One step down, then a held note.

Lower line: F, F, E. A held note, then one step down.

Played together over the roots in the left hand, these lines outline the harmony with only two notes per chord. The sound is clean and modern, and it leaves plenty of room for a melody or a bass player.

Using guide tones in accompaniment

When accompanying a singer or another instrument, a simple left-hand root plus right-hand guide tones gives a light, clear texture. Because the guide tones move so little, the right hand barely needs to shift, and the harmony flows smoothly. Later, you can add a colour note, such as a ninth or a thirteenth, above the guide tones to enrich the sound without losing clarity.

Guide tones and improvisation

Improvisers use guide-tone lines as a skeleton for solos. Try this exercise:

  • play the progression slowly with roots in the left hand
  • in the right hand, play one guide tone per chord on beat one, choosing the one closest to the previous note
  • then add a few notes before each guide tone, approaching it from a step above or below

The result is a melody that clearly outlines the chord changes, even without accompaniment. Many experienced improvisers aim for guide tones on strong beats because they make the harmony audible within the line itself.

Extending to longer progressions

Guide-tone lines work across longer chord sequences, such as a circle of fifths or a twelve-bar blues. In a blues in F, for example, the guide tones of F7 (A and E-flat) move to those of B-flat 7 (D and A-flat) by semitone steps. Writing out the guide tones for a whole song is a useful exercise: it reveals how the harmony moves and gives you a ready-made voicing plan.

Practice routine for guide tones

Name them aloud

For each chord in a progression, say the third and seventh before playing them.

Play in all keys

Move the ii-V-I through several keys. Notice that the pattern of half steps and common tones stays the same.

Sing the lines

Sing the upper guide-tone line while playing the roots. This trains your ear to hear the harmonic movement.

Use a lead sheet

Pick a standard song and write the guide tones above each chord symbol, then play them with the melody.

FAQ

Q: What are guide tones?

A: They are the third and seventh of a chord, the notes that define its quality.

Q: Why do guide tones move so smoothly?

A: In many common progressions, they stay the same or move by a semitone or whole tone to the next chord.

Q: Can I use guide tones without jazz experience?

A: Yes. They are a simple way to voice seventh chords in any style that uses them.

Q: How do guide tones help improvisation?

A: Landing on them at chord changes makes the harmony audible in the melody line.
