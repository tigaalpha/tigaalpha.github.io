Practising Keyboard Leaps by Aiming at a Landing Spot

Practice tips · 2026-11-12

Wide leaps on the keyboard often fail because the hand leaves late and hopes to find the key in mid-air. A more reliable method is to treat the arrival as a landing spot: a specific key, a planned finger, and a moment in the bar when the hand should already be there. You then practise the leave, the travel, and the landing as a short unit, and only afterwards put the leap back into the phrase. The ear checks the tone of the arrival, not only whether the right key went down.

Name the landing key, the finger, and the leave time

In the score, mark the target note and the finger that will play it. Look at the previous note and decide when the hand may leave. If a rest or a held note in the other hand covers the travel, write that in as well. The landing should be early enough that the new tone can speak on the beat. A last-moment lunge is the opposite of this plan.

Practise a silent landing before the sounding one

Arrive silently on the target key before you play it

From the previous note, move to the target key and touch it silently, on time, then play it on a second pass. The silent landing teaches the distance without the extra tension of sound-production. Once the silent arrival is regular, add the tone and listen: a jab, a thin note, or a late swell all tell you the travel is still rushed. Keep the loop short, usually the previous note, the leap, and the next note.

  • Mark the landing pitch, the finger, and the beat when the hand should leave.
  • Loop a silent arrival first, then a sounding one, at a slow pulse.
  • Listen for a jab or a late tone at the landing, not only for the correct key.
  • Use a held sound or a rest, when the score allows, to cover the travel.
  • Return the leap to a longer phrase only after the short unit is calm.

Hear the landing as part of the line

When the unit is secure, start a little earlier in the phrase and continue a little after the leap. The arrival should have the tone the line needs, whether it is a melody note, a bass root, or a quiet inner tone. If the phrase dips or waits at the leap, the landing is still the problem; do not solve it by simply playing the whole phrase louder. Pedal may join the landing once the finger is true, but it should not hide a miss.

When leaps occur in both hands or in a fast sequence

If both hands leap at once, give each hand its own landing mark and, if possible, slightly different leave times. If several leaps follow one another, practise them as a chain of landings, two at a time, rather than as one long hop. The same rule applies: each target is named, each travel has a moment, and speed comes after the chain is accurate at a moderate pulse.

If the landing is a bass note that must then stay as a pedal point in the ear, practise the arrival and the held length together, so the leap does not end in a short bump before the sound should continue.

FAQ

Q: Should I watch the landing key as I travel?

A: A glance is useful in the first loops. Later, try to land from the marked leave time, with the eyes returning to the score.

Q: What if I cannot reach the interval comfortably?

A: Check the fingering, the bench place, and whether a redistribution would still keep the voicing. A landing plan cannot replace a span that the hand cannot make.

Q: Do staccato leaps need a silent landing too?

A: Yes, at first. The silent arrival still teaches distance; the short touch is added once the key is found in time.

Q: How slow should the first loops be?

A: Slow enough that the silent landing is early and quiet. If the sounding arrival is late, the loop is still too fast.

Q: The leap is downward and I overshoot. What should I change?

A: Aim at the specific key, not past it, and arrive silently first. A smaller, earlier motion is usually clearer than a larger one begun late.
