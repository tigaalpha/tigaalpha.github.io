Locrian Mode on Piano: The Scale That Never Feels Home

Music theory · 2026-10-31

The Locrian mode is the seven-note scale you get by playing the white keys of the piano from B to B. It differs from the natural minor scale in two places: the second note is lowered and, crucially, the fifth note is lowered too. That flat fifth means the chord built on the home note is diminished rather than major or minor, so the music never quite settles. Among the seven traditional modes, Locrian is the one composers use least as a home base, yet it is a valuable tool for creating tension, mystery and unease, and studying it sharpens your ear for every other mode.

This article explains why Locrian sounds unstable, how to find it in any key, and several short exercises for exploring its colour at the keyboard.

The formula and its two fingerprints

Written as steps, Locrian is half, whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole. Starting on B, that gives B, C, D, E, F, G, A and B.

Flat second

The C sits just a semitone above the home note. This creates a dark, pressing sound, similar to the Phrygian mode.

Flat fifth

The F is a tritone above B, rather than a perfect fifth. The perfect fifth is the interval that most strongly anchors a key, so removing it pulls the ground away. This is the single reason Locrian feels like a floor that tilts.

Why the home chord wobbles

Stack every other note from B and you get B, D and F, a diminished triad. Play it with the sustain pedal and listen. It sounds like a question, not an answer. In most tonal music, the diminished chord appears as a passing chord that pushes toward a more stable one. When a mode makes that unstable chord its centre, the whole piece takes on a restless character.

Try this comparison at the piano:

  • Play A, C and E, the home chord of A natural minor. It sounds sad but settled.
  • Play B, D and F, the home chord of B Locrian. It sounds suspended and uneasy.
  • Play B, D and F sharp, which restores the perfect fifth. The tension eases at once, and the sound slides into B minor.

That third example shows how one semitone defines the mode.

Finding Locrian in any key

Start from a major scale

Locrian begins on the seventh note of a major scale. To find E Locrian, ask which major scale has E as its seventh note. The answer is F major, so E Locrian uses one flat: E, F, G, A, B flat, C, D, E.

Alter natural minor

Take any natural minor scale and lower the second and fifth notes. D natural minor becomes D Locrian with E flat and A flat.

Use the half-diminished chord

In jazz, Locrian is the scale that matches a half-diminished seventh chord, written as m7♭5. If you see Bm7♭5 in a lead sheet, B Locrian fits naturally over it.

Exercises for hearing the colour

The drone experiment

Hold a low B in the left hand. With the right hand, play the B Locrian scale slowly, pausing on each note. Notice the bite of C and the hollow sound of F over the drone.

Swap one note

Play a short phrase in B natural minor, then repeat it with F replacing F sharp. Listen for the moment the mood darkens.

Write a four-bar riff

Using only the notes of E Locrian, compose a riff that returns to E often. Keep the rhythm simple. Many players find that the mode suits film-style tension cues and game music.

Locrian over a ii–V–i

In a minor key jazz progression, play the Locrian scale over the first chord. This is where the mode is most at home in practical music.

Where Locrian appears

Pure Locrian pieces are uncommon, but the mode turns up in a few places worth listening for. Heavy rock and metal riffs sometimes exploit its flat fifth for an aggressive sound. Jazz improvisers rely on it constantly over half-diminished chords. Film and game composers use fragments of it to signal danger or something otherworldly. Learning to hear it helps you recognise these colours when they appear.

FAQ

Q: Why is Locrian used so rarely as a home key?

A: Because its home chord is diminished, the music struggles to sound finished. Composers usually prefer it for short passages.

Q: Is Locrian the same as the diminished scale?

A: No. The diminished scale has eight notes in a repeating pattern of steps. Locrian has seven notes and comes from the major scale.

Q: Should beginners learn Locrian?

A: Learning all seven modes on the white keys is a useful ear exercise at any level, and Locrian completes the set.

Q: Which mode sounds closest to Locrian?

A: Phrygian, which shares the flat second but keeps a perfect fifth.
