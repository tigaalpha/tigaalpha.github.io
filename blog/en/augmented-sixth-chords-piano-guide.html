Augmented Sixth Chords on Piano: Three Common Chromatic Shapes

Music theory · 2026-11-11

An augmented sixth chord on the piano is a chromatic approach to the dominant whose outer notes form an augmented sixth (or, in inversion, a diminished third) and then move outward to the octave of the dominant scale degree. In C major or C minor, those outer notes are A-flat and F-sharp, both leaning toward G. Three common inner fillings are used often enough to have familiar labels, and the labels are only a shorthand for which extra notes sit between that outer interval.

The outer interval comes first

Find the lowered sixth degree and the raised fourth degree of the key. In C, A-flat in the bass and F-sharp above it (or the reverse in a rare inversion) create the characteristic stretch. Play those two notes alone and resolve them: A-flat down to G, F-sharp up to G. That outward motion is the fingerprint. Only after it feels natural should the inner notes be added.

The same outer interval appears in minor and, as a borrowed colour, in major. In major, the lowered sixth is the note that often surprises the ear, because the rest of the phrase may have been using A natural a moment earlier.

The two outer notes first

Play the lowered sixth and the raised fourth alone until the outward resolution is easy.

Three familiar inner shapes

The leanest inner filling adds only the tonic, C, between A-flat and F-sharp. A richer filling adds the tonic and the second degree, C and D. A still richer filling adds the tonic and the lowered third, C and E-flat (in a C context). These are the three shapes teachers often introduce first. Play them in the same key, one after another, resolving each time to a G major or G dominant chord, and listen for how much inner movement appears before the octave on G arrives.

Resolving to the dominant, with or without a delay

The standard landing is a dominant triad or a dominant seventh, with the outer notes becoming an octave on the dominant degree. Sometimes a cadential six-four stands in between: the bass already arrives on the dominant note, while the upper voices pass through a tonic chord in second inversion before the dominant triad proper. That delay is optional. Practise both the direct resolution and the delayed one so a score cannot surprise you.

A short checklist helps when a passage looks crowded with accidentals:

  • Locate the two notes that form the augmented sixth.
  • Confirm they will expand to an octave on the dominant degree.
  • Name the inner notes so the spelling matches the key.
  • Decide whether a six-four will sit between the chord and the dominant.
  • Play the outer voices alone, then rebuild the inner parts.

Spelling, not keyboard guessing

Because A-flat and G-sharp are the same key, it is tempting to write whatever accidental is nearest. Resist that. The lowered sixth should look like a lowered sixth, and the raised fourth should look like a raised fourth, so the resolution is visible on the page. In E-flat major, the outer notes are C-flat and A natural, heading toward B-flat. In A minor, they are F and D-sharp, heading toward E.

If a student writes G-sharp instead of A-flat in C, the interval will still sound, but the analysis and the resolution arrows will be harder to follow. Take a moment to respell, even if the fingers already know the keys.

Inner notes should follow the same honesty. A lowered third in the richest of the three shapes is written as a lowered third, not as a sharp second, when the destination is a regular dominant in that key.

Using the chord in a short phrase

A practical composition drill is to write four bars: tonic, a brief pre-dominant, the augmented sixth, dominant, tonic. Keep the rhythm slow. The chromatic chord should occupy a strong enough beat to be heard, then move onward without extra ornaments on the resolution. After the phrase works in C minor, transpose the whole four-bar model to G minor and to F minor, reading the new accidentals rather than sliding the hand by feel alone.

Avoid stacking this colour on every cadence. The sound is vivid because it is unusual. One well-placed approach to a final dominant is usually easier to hear than a chain of chromatic sixths that never settles.

FAQ

Q: Do I need the historical labels to play the chord?

A: No. Learn the outer interval and the three common inner fillings first. The labels are a convenient nickname once the shapes are already in the hands.

Q: Does the chord belong only in minor keys?

A: It is at home in minor, and it is also borrowed in major, where the lowered sixth gives an especially strong colour.

Q: What if the bass is not on the lowered sixth?

A: Inversions exist, but they are less common in teaching examples. Find the augmented sixth interval first, then see which of those two notes is in the bass.

Q: Why does my resolution sound bare?

A: The outer notes may be resolving correctly while the inner voices drop out too soon. Keep the inner tones until the dominant chord is complete, then release.

Q: Can this replace a regular subdominant?

A: It can stand in the pre-dominant position, but it is a chromatic substitute, not a daily replacement for a plain IV or ii chord.
