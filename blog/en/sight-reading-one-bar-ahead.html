Sight-Reading at the Piano by Looking One Bar Ahead

Practice tips · 2026-11-12

Sight-reading at the piano often stalls because the eyes stay on the notes the fingers are already playing. By the time a new bar arrives, there is no plan for the next harmony or the next leap. Looking one bar ahead is a practical habit: the hands play the present bar while the eyes take in the next, and the pulse continues even if a finger is approximate. The skill can be practised on music well within reach, not only on difficult new scores.

Choose music simple enough that the eyes have spare attention

Use a slow piece, a hymn-like texture, or a short study whose rhythm you can count. If the present bar already consumes all your attention, there is no room to look ahead. Set a modest tempo with a click and decide that the pulse will not stop. Wrong notes may occur; a stopped pulse is the larger fault in this exercise.

Practise a deliberate look into the next bar

At the start of each bar, play what is already in the hands, then move the eyes to the next barline and take in the new bass, the new melody note, or a leap. You can even say the next bass note while still playing the present bar. Do this for a few lines, then rest. The habit is tiring at first. Mark a reminder at a few barlines, such as a small arrow, and remove the marks as the look becomes regular.

Recover on the next beat, not by restarting the line

When a chord is wrong, the useful response is to arrive in time for the next beat, even if the wrong notes are abandoned. Restarting the line every time trains the opposite of sight-reading. Practise this recovery on purpose: play a line, allow a miss, and require the next downbeat to be on time. Looking ahead makes that recovery possible, because the next bar is already partly known.

When the texture thickens or the page turns

In a busier texture, look ahead for the next bass and the next right-hand landmark, not for every inner tone. For a page turn, the look-ahead must include the first notes of the new page, so the turn is prepared like any other shift. Practise the turn on the same simple piece until the pulse survives it. The aim is still a flowing first reading, not a polished performance.

On a first reading of a new line, you can also decide in advance which voice you will protect if the texture becomes busy: usually the bass and the melody. That choice makes the look-ahead smaller, and it keeps the pulse from stalling while the inner parts wait for a later pass.

  • Choose a simple texture and a tempo you can keep without stopping.
  • Keep a click running, and do not halt for a missed note.
  • At each bar, glance at the next bass or the next leap while still playing.
  • After a slip, rejoin on the next beat rather than restarting the line.
  • End the sitting while the look-ahead is still possible, not after the eyes have tired.

FAQ

Q: Should I always look a whole bar ahead, even in very short bars?

A: In a very short bar, a beat or two may be enough. The idea is that the eyes are not stuck on the notes already under the fingers.

Q: What if I lose my place when I look ahead?

A: The music may still be too hard, or the tempo too fast. Simplify, and point to the next barline as a landmark until the return to the present bar is easy.

Q: Is it all right to skip an inner note in order to keep the pulse?

A: In a first reading, yes. Keep the bass and the melody if you can, and accept a thinner middle, so the line continues.

Q: Should I practise sight-reading at performance tempo?

A: Start under the tempo at which the look-ahead is possible. A slower, continuous reading teaches more than a fast reading that stops every bar.

Q: How long should a sight-reading sitting last?

A: A few minutes of fresh reading is often enough. Tired eyes stop looking ahead, and the habit does not improve after that point.
