The Neapolitan Sixth on Piano: A Flattened Second in First Inversion

Music theory · 2026-11-12

A Neapolitan sixth takes a major triad on the lowered second degree, usually in first inversion, so the bass is the fourth degree of the key. In minor keys it is especially common as a colourful approach to the dominant. At the piano the chord can sound like a mistake if the flattened second is late or omitted, or if the inversion is not clear in the bass. It can also be banged, as if the unusual accidental were a special effect. The practical job is to locate this harmony, prepare the accidental, voice it, and move to the next chord as the score directs, often toward the dominant.

Find the flattened second and confirm the first inversion

Look for a chord whose root is the lowered second degree, with the bass on the fourth degree. Mark the accidental and the bass note, and name the chord in the margin. Play the chord slowly, then play the same roots in root position as a check, then return to the written inversion. If the flattened second is in an inner voice, circle it. In a major key the chord also needs a flattened sixth; check every accidental before you practise at speed.

  • Mark the flattened second, the bass on the fourth degree, and the next chord.
  • Confirm the first inversion by playing the bass alone, then the full spacing.
  • Practise the Neapolitan and its resolution as a slow pair.
  • Keep the unusual accidental audible inside the texture, without banging it.
  • Check every accidental again if the passage is in a major key.

Prepare the accidental and the spacing as a pair with the next chord

The Neapolitan often sits just before the dominant or a cadential six-four. Practise the Neapolitan and the next chord as a pair, with the finger already above the flattened key, and with the bass ready for its step. Listen for the unusual colour, then for a clean resolution. If two voices move in similar motion into a clash, slow the pair and check the written parts. Do not let a busy right-hand figure hide the flattened second.

Place the chord inside the phrase, not as an isolated event

A Neapolitan sixth belongs to the line that approaches it. Practise a few bars earlier, arrive, and continue to the cadence or the next phrase. The dynamic should follow the score; a sudden lurch into the chord often spoils the colour. If the same harmony is decorated with passing notes, learn the plain chord first, then add the decoration. In a longer minor movement, mark every Neapolitan so you are not surprised on a later page.

Teaching the chord at the keyboard

A learner can be asked to build a major chord on the lowered second degree, put it in first inversion, and then play the written progression. Naming the chord matters less, at first, than finding the accidental and the bass. Teachers may also ask for the resolution alone, then for the whole phrase. The label "Neapolitan sixth" becomes useful once the sound is known. The aim is a prepared colour that belongs to the cadence, which is how the chord usually works in piano music.

FAQ

Q: Why is it called a sixth?

A: Because the common position is first inversion, in which a sixth above the bass is a prominent part of the spacing. The bass is usually the fourth degree of the key.

Q: Is the Neapolitan only used in minor keys?

A: It is especially common in minor, but it can appear in major with the extra accidentals the chord requires. Always check the written spelling.

Q: What usually follows the chord?

A: Often the dominant, or a cadential six-four and then the dominant. Practise the written next chord as part of the same pair.

Q: Should I emphasise the flattened second?

A: Make it audible and in time. A heavy accent on that note can make the chord sound like a wrong note rather than a colour.

Q: How do I stop the chord from sounding like a mistake?

A: Prepare the accidental, keep the inversion clear in the bass, and resolve it in time. Slow pair practice is the usual remedy.
