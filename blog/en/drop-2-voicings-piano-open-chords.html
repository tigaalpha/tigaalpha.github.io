Drop-2 Voicings on Piano: Opening Up Close Chords

Music theory · 2026-11-11

When chords are played in close position, all notes sit within one octave. This sounds compact and sometimes crowded, especially in the middle register. Drop-2 voicings open up the sound by taking the second note from the top and moving it down an octave. The result is a wider, warmer chord that is easy to spread between two hands. These voicings are common in jazz piano, big-band arranging and guitar, and they can enrich ballads, pop and film-style accompaniments.

Building a drop-2 voicing

Start with a four-note chord in close position. Take C major 7 with the seventh on top: C, E, G, B, from bottom to top.

  • the top note is B
  • the second note from the top is G
  • move G down an octave

The new voicing, from bottom to top, is G, C, E, B. The chord now spans more than an octave, with a wider interval at the bottom.

You can apply the same process to every inversion of the chord. Each close position produces a different drop-2 shape, giving four voicings for each seventh chord.

Playing with two hands

Drop-2 voicings fit naturally between the hands. A common approach is to play the lowest note with the left hand and the upper three notes with the right hand. Another is to play two notes in each hand. This distribution makes the voicing easy to reach and allows the right hand to keep the melody on top.

Melody on top

Drop-2 voicings are often used in chord-melody playing, where the melody note is the top of each chord. To harmonise a melody:

Find the chord

Identify the chord for each melody note from the lead sheet.

Build in close position

Arrange the chord so the melody note is on top in close position.

Drop the second note

Move the second-highest note down an octave.

Distribute

Play the lowest note in the left hand and the remaining notes in the right, or split two and two.

The melody remains clear on top, while the harmony underneath sounds full and open.

Voice leading through a progression

In a ii-V-I progression in C, choose top notes that move smoothly: C, then B, then B. The drop-2 voicings, from bottom to top, are:

  • D minor 7 with C on top: close position D, F, A, C becomes A, D, F, C
  • G7 with B on top: close position D, F, G, B becomes G, D, F, B
  • C major 7 with B on top: close position C, E, G, B becomes G, C, E, B

Look at the movement. From D minor 7 to G7, the bottom note falls from A to G, the top note falls from C to B, and the middle notes D and F stay put. From G7 to C major 7, D falls to C and F falls to E, while G and B stay. Every voice moves by a step or not at all, which creates the polished sound associated with arrangers.

Rather than memorising fixed shapes, practise finding the drop-2 voicing for each chord with a given top note, then choose the one that moves most smoothly from the previous chord.

Where drop-2 voicings sound good

They work particularly well in the middle register, roughly from the G below middle C to the C above it. Too low and the wide intervals become muddy; too high and they can sound thin. They suit ballads, slow jazz standards, gospel-flavoured pop and lush accompaniments for singers.

Drills for the four shapes

Take one seventh chord and play all four drop-2 inversions ascending and descending. Then do the same for minor 7, dominant 7 and half-diminished chords. Next, harmonise a simple melody, such as a folk song, with drop-2 voicings on each strong beat. Record yourself and listen for smooth movement between chords.

FAQ

Q: What does drop-2 mean?

A: It means taking the second-highest note of a close-position chord and lowering it by an octave.

Q: Why use drop-2 voicings?

A: They create a more open, warm sound and are easy to distribute between two hands.

Q: Are drop-2 voicings only for jazz?

A: They are common in jazz but also useful in pop, gospel, ballads and film-style arrangements.

Q: How many drop-2 shapes does one chord have?

A: Four, one from each close-position inversion of a four-note chord.
