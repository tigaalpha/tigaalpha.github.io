Hemiola in Piano Repertoire: Three Beats in the Time of Two

Music theory · 2026-11-12

A hemiola is a grouping in which three beats occupy the time usually taken by two, or in which a barline still printed in one metre is heard, for a moment, in another. In piano music it often appears near a cadence in triple metre, where two bars of three are heard as three groups of two, or in a passage that briefly writes against the prevailing pulse. The device only works if the player knows where the grouping starts and ends, and if the new accents are audible without turning the bar into a scramble. Practising a hemiola is a counting and grouping job at the keyboard, not a vague idea of "syncopation somewhere."

Find the bars and mark the temporary grouping

Look for a place where the written metre stays the same, but ties, beams, or harmony suggest a different grouping. In two bars of 3/4, for example, a hemiola may be heard as three dotted-crotchet pulses. Circle the start, draw light ticks on the new beats, and say the grouping aloud before you play. If the score already prints accents or beams that show the hemiola, use those marks and add only what you still miss on the stand. Do not guess; a wrong grouping practised for a week is hard to undo.

Count the hemiola, then play it at a modest tempo

Count the new grouping while you play slowly, with a click on the original written beats if you need a check, or with a click on the hemiola beats once the bars are understood. The hands should make the new pulse audible through tone and slight length, not through a stamp on every tick. Practise the bar before the hemiola, the hemiola itself, and the bar after, as one group. The return to the original metre is as important as the temporary grouping.

When one hand stays in the old metre

Some passages write a hemiola in the melody against a bass that continues the original beat, or the other way around. Practise each hand with its own count, then put the hands together slowly and listen for that crossing. The point of the writing is that crossing, not a compromise in which both hands abandon their grouping. If the join is confused, separate the hands again and name the beats out loud. Only then return to a single pulse word, such as a larger bar count, for a run.

Cadential hemiolas and performance choice

Near a cadence, a hemiola often broadens the last bars. Follow any written ritardando, but do not let slowing replace the grouping; the three-in-the-time-of-two should still be countable. If two editions beam the bars differently, choose one plan, write it in your copy, and stay with it. Teachers can ask a learner to clap the two groupings, then to play the bars, so the ear is prepared before the fingers are. The hemiola then becomes a clear event in the phrase, which is its purpose in the repertoire.

FAQ

Q: Is a hemiola the same as a triplet?

A: No. A triplet divides one beat into three. A hemiola regroups a longer span, often two bars, so that three beats occupy the time of two.

Q: What if I cannot hear it at first?

A: Mark the ticks, count aloud, and play very slowly. If needed, play only the notes that fall on the hemiola beats, then fill in the others.

Q: Should both hands always follow the hemiola?

A: Only if both parts are written that way. When one hand stays in the old metre, keep that independence on purpose.

Q: Can I ignore a hemiola and just play the printed metre?

A: If the writing clearly groups against that metre, ignoring it flattens the phrase. Use the score's beams, ties, and harmony as your guide.

Q: How do I return to the original metre after the hemiola?

A: Practise the last hemiola beat and the first beat of the next bar as a join, with a count that names both groupings, until the return is clean.
