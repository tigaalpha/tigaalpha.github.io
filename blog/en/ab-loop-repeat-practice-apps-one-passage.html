A-B Loop Repeat in Practice Apps: Repeating One Passage Well

Practice tips · 2026-11-17

An A-B loop is a practice feature that repeats a chosen section of a recording, backing track or lesson over and over. You mark a start point, called A, and an end point, called B, and the player keeps returning to A whenever it reaches B. For piano students, this simple tool makes it easy to focus on a tricky passage without hunting for the right place each time, and it pairs naturally with slowing the tempo.

Why looping helps practice

Much practice time disappears into finding the place to start again. Looping removes that friction. You can listen to the same few bars many times, play along, and notice details that are easy to miss when a piece rushes past.

  • Repetition builds familiarity with notes and rhythm.
  • Listening closely to one passage sharpens the ear.
  • Focused work on a small section is less tiring than playing everything.

Setting good loop points

The length of the loop matters. A loop that is too long becomes another full run-through, while a loop that is too short can cut a phrase in an awkward place.

  • Choose a section of one to four bars for difficult spots.
  • Start the loop a beat or two before the tricky moment.
  • End the loop just after it, so you practise landing safely.
  • Follow phrase shapes when possible, so the music still makes sense.

Leave space to breathe

Some players add a small gap at the end of the loop. This short silence gives time to reset the hands and prepare before the passage starts again.

Combining loops with slower tempo

Many apps allow the playback speed to slow down without changing the pitch. Pairing a loop with a slower tempo is a calm and reliable way to learn a passage.

Practising along with the loop

Looping is not only for listening. Try different ways of engaging with the passage while it repeats.

  • Listen once without playing and hum the melody.
  • Tap the rhythm on your knee.
  • Play the right hand alone, then the left hand alone.
  • Play both hands together quietly.
  • Play with full expression once the notes feel secure.

Avoiding the traps of looping

Watch for autopilot

Loops are powerful, but they can create habits worth watching. Playing the same bars for a long time can lead to mindless repetition, where the fingers move but the mind drifts. Short, focused rounds work better than endless cycles.

  • Set a small goal for each round, such as smooth fingering or even rhythm.
  • Stop after a few repeats and check whether the goal was met.
  • Reconnect the passage to the bars before and after it.
  • Take short breaks to keep attention fresh.

Connecting the passage back to the piece

A passage learned in isolation sometimes stumbles when you play the whole piece. Bridge the gap by gradually widening the loop. Extend it by a bar on each side, then play the full phrase, then the full section. This helps the new passage join the music around it smoothly.

Looping with a teacher's guidance

Teachers often know which passages deserve a loop and which simply need more listening. Share your loop points with your teacher and ask whether the chosen sections make musical sense. A teacher may suggest a different starting point or a fingering that turns a stubborn passage into an easy one.

FAQ

Q: How many times should I repeat a loop?

A: Repeat until the passage feels a little easier, then take a break. Quality matters more than a set number.

Q: Does slowing the tempo change the pitch?

A: Many modern apps keep the pitch the same while slowing playback.

Q: Can I use loops for the whole piece?

A: Loops work best for short, difficult sections. Play the whole piece regularly as well.

Q: Is looping useful for beginners?

A: Yes. It helps beginners focus on small sections without feeling overwhelmed.
