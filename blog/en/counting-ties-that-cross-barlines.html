Counting Ties That Cross the Barline at the Piano

Practice tips · 2026-11-12

Ties that cross the barline are easy to under-count or to cut short. The held sound belongs to both the old bar and the new one, but the downbeat of the new bar still has a pulse, even when no new attack occurs. If you lose that pulse, the next note after the tie arrives early or late, and ensemble with another player or with a click becomes shaky. Counting through the tie, marking the hidden downbeat, and practising the release or the continuation make the barline a place of control rather than a blur.

Mark the hidden downbeat under the tie

Count the new bar under the tie before you add tone

In the score, draw a small tick or write the beat number at the barline, even though the key is already down. Say the count of the new bar while the note continues, including any rests that follow the tie. If another voice attacks on that downbeat, circle it; that attack is your audible proof of the pulse. If no other voice sounds, a click or a foot tap on the barline can stand in until the count is secure.

Hold the full value, then practise the release or the next attack

Play the start of the tie, keep the key down through the barline, and only then release or play the next written note, according to the score. Loop this slowly with a click on every beat, including the silent downbeat. If you tend to add a second attack at the barline, practise a calm continuation of the sound. If you tend to let the tone die and then poke the next note, stay on the key until the written end.

  • Tick the downbeat under the tie in the score and count it aloud at first.
  • Use a click on every beat, including the beat that has no new attack.
  • Loop from the start of the tie through the next sounding note.
  • Check other voices for an attack that can confirm the barline.
  • Only later play a wider span of music that includes this barline.

Put the tied crossing back into the phrase

Once the short loop is secure, begin a bar earlier and continue a bar later so the tie is not an isolated event. Listen for a swell or a dip at the barline that you did not plan. Pedal should not be used to fake a tie that the finger has already left, unless the score and the style really ask for a connected sonority produced that way. The finger's written length comes first.

When several parts tie across the same barline

In more than one voice, one part may tie while another articulates the downbeat. Practise the articulating part alone so the pulse is obvious, then add the tied part and keep the held key quiet at the barline. If both hands have ties, give each hand its own count in turn, then combine them. The goal is a shared pulse, not a general wash of sound that hides the barline.

FAQ

Q: Should I make a small accent on the tied downbeat?

A: Only if the music asks for one. Usually the downbeat is felt, not re-attacked. A tick in the score is enough to keep it honest.

Q: What if the tie is very long, across two barlines?

A: Count every barline in between. You may add a light physical pulse, such as a finger remaining in place but a wrist that marks the beat, without a new attack.

Q: The next note after the tie is in the other hand. How should I practise it?

A: Loop the held note through the barline and the other hand's entry, so the entry is on time even though the first hand has not released yet.

Q: Can I use the pedal instead of holding the key?

A: Not as a substitute for a written tie, unless you are solving a reach and the style accepts it. Practise the held key first.

Q: Why does my next entry keep arriving early?

A: The hidden downbeat is probably being skipped in the count. Slow the click, speak the new bar, and do not leave the key until the written value is finished.
