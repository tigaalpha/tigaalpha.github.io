Burgmüller Op. 100: A Skill Map for the 25 Progressive Studies

Songs · 2026-10-31

The 25 Easy and Progressive Studies, Op. 100, by Friedrich Burgmüller are short piano pieces that each train one skill while also telling a small musical story. They suit players who have finished a beginner method and want real repertoire. The smartest way to use the book is not to play it in strict order, but to choose studies by the skill you need next and to learn each one character first, notes second.

Below you will find a quick profile of the composer, a skill map that groups the studies by what they teach, a three-week routine for learning any one of them, and the mistakes teachers hear most often.

Who was Burgmüller?

Friedrich Burgmüller (full name Johann Friedrich Franz) came from Regensburg, Germany, where he was born in 1806, and moved to Paris in the early 1830s. There he taught piano and wrote light, popular music for amateur players. His younger brother Norbert was also a composer. Today Friedrich is remembered mainly for his teaching pieces, above all Op. 100, published in the mid-nineteenth century and still widely used by piano teachers.

The book's secret is simple: every study has a French title and a clear mood. "La candeur" (innocence) is calm and plain; "La chasse" (the hunt) gallops with horn calls; "Inquiétude" (restlessness) captures nervous energy in about a minute of music. A title gives you a picture to aim for, which makes technical work feel musical.

A skill map of Op. 100

Instead of starting on page one and grinding forward, look for the skill you want to build. Here are useful groupings:

  • Singing melody over a quiet accompaniment: La candeur (1), Tendre fleur (10), Consolation (13).
  • Light, quick fingers: Arabesque (2), La babillarde (17), L'hirondelle (24).
  • Scales and running passages: Progrès (6), Le courant limpide (7).
  • Ornaments and grace notes: La gracieuse (8), La bergeronnette (11).
  • Chords and big sound: Ballade (15), La chevaleresque (25).
  • Rhythm and dance character: La styrienne (14), Tarentelle (20), Barcarolle (22).
  • Phrasing and expression in slow music: L'adieu (12), Douce plainte (16).

Many teachers still follow the printed order for early students, because the difficulty rises gently. Once you have a few studies behind you, the skill map lets you target weaknesses directly.

Learning one study in three weeks

Week 1: hear the story

Read the title and look up what it means. Listen to a recording once or twice and describe the mood in a single word. Then play hands separately at a slow tempo, focusing on fingering. Mark any tricky bars with a pencil.

Week 2: build the technique

Work on the marked bars as short loops of two or three beats. For quick passages, practise in dotted rhythms, long-short and then short-long, before returning to even notes. For melody studies, play the accompaniment alone very softly until it sounds like a background murmur. Put hands together at about half the final tempo.

Week 3: perform the character

Raise the tempo in small steps. Now concentrate on dynamics, articulation and the shape of each phrase. Record a full run-through every second day and compare it with your Week 1 description. Does Arabesque sound playful? Does Consolation sound warm? If not, the notes are ready but the story is not.

Short studies are perfect for this cycle, because most of them fit on one or two pages. Finishing a complete piece every few weeks keeps motivation high.

Mistakes to watch for

Treating it as finger gymnastics

The studies are music first. A technically clean Arabesque that sounds angry has missed the point.

Ignoring the first dynamic marking

Many studies begin softly. Starting forte changes the whole character before the first phrase is over.

An accompaniment that is too loud

In melody studies, the lower notes often have more notes than the tune, so they easily drown it. Practise the accompaniment alone, lighter than you think it should be.

Rushing quick passages

Speed comes from evenness. Keep the metronome slower until every note is the same size.

Skipping the slow studies

L'adieu and Douce plainte look easy, but they train the patience and listening that faster pieces depend on.

After Op. 100, what next?

Players who enjoy the set often continue with Burgmüller's 18 Characteristic Studies, Op. 109, which are longer and more demanding. Others move into short classical pieces of similar length, such as easy sonatina movements or simple Romantic character pieces. Either way, the habits from Op. 100 carry over: read the title, find the mood and let technique serve expression.

These studies also make a good bridge for adult learners who want to learn piano with real repertoire, and they fit neatly into a structured piano course or longer piano learning course. Because each piece is short, they are easy to practise with an online piano lessons app that records each attempt, so you can hear whether the melody truly sings above the accompaniment.

Frequently asked questions

Q: What level are Burgmüller's Op. 100 studies?

A: They are usually taught after a beginner method, at an early-intermediate level. The first studies are gentler than the last ones.

Q: Do I have to play them in order?

A: No. The order rises in difficulty, which helps early students, but choosing studies by skill is also a common approach.

Q: How long does one study take to learn?

A: It depends on the player and the piece. A three-week cycle of hearing, technique and performance is a practical starting point.

Q: Are the titles important?

A: Yes. Each title suggests the character of the piece, and playing that character is a large part of what the study teaches.

Q: Can I learn these studies when I learn piano online?

A: Yes. They are short and well structured, which makes them easy to practise and review in sections at home.
