Contrary Motion Scales: Mirror Practice for Both Hands

Practice tips · 2026-11-11

Most pianists learn scales in parallel motion, with both hands moving in the same direction an octave apart. Contrary motion offers a different view. The hands begin on the same note, often with thumbs together, and then move outward in opposite directions before returning to the centre. Because the hands mirror each other, contrary motion can feel surprisingly natural, and it builds coordination and fingering awareness that later transfer to parallel scales and real pieces.

Why contrary motion feels easier

In the C major scale played in contrary motion from middle C, both thumbs play C, both third fingers cross or tuck at the same time, and both hands use matching fingers on each step. The fingering is symmetrical. Instead of keeping track of two different patterns, you only need to remember one, mirrored. This makes contrary motion a useful early step for beginners, and a reset for intermediate players whose parallel scales have become uneven.

Getting started in C major

  • place both thumbs on middle C, or the right thumb on middle C and the left thumb on the C below
  • move outward: right hand up, left hand down, one octave
  • turn back and meet at the starting C
  • keep the fingering mirrored: 1-2-3, thumb under to 1-2-3-4-5 in each hand

Play slowly, listening for the moment when both thumbs pass under. If one hand arrives early, slow down until they align.

Keys that need attention

Not every key is perfectly symmetrical. Scales whose starting notes sit on black keys, such as B-flat or E-flat major, have different finger patterns in each hand, so the mirror is broken. These keys are excellent for training independence, because the hands must keep their own patterns while still moving outward together. Try them after C, G, D and E major, which are close to symmetrical.

A neat exception is D major starting on D, and similarly A-flat major, where the black and white keys mirror around the starting note. Exploring which keys are symmetrical is a small puzzle that also teaches the layout of the keyboard.

Making it musical

Scales become much more useful when they are treated as music rather than gymnastics. Small changes of touch and rhythm turn one exercise into many.

Shape the line

Crescendo as the hands move apart and diminuendo as they return. This mirrors the physical shape of the motion and turns an exercise into a phrase.

Vary articulation

Play legato in one octave and staccato in the next. Then try one hand legato while the other plays staccato.

Change rhythms

Use dotted rhythms, triplets and groups of four. Changing the rhythm reveals weak fingers that steady eighth notes can hide.

Extending the range

A useful in-between step is to practise one and a half octaves, which forces the hands to turn around at an unusual point and keeps attention on the fingering rather than on habit. Once one octave is comfortable, extend to two, then to four. In the wider range the hands travel far apart, and posture matters: lean slightly in the direction of each hand, and keep the shoulders level. Avoid twisting the body to reach the ends of the keyboard; move the arms from the shoulder instead.

Where contrary motion appears in music

Composers have long valued contrary motion between outer voices because it keeps the parts independent and gives harmony a sense of opening and closing. In four-part writing, students learn to let the bass and soprano move in opposite directions where possible, which avoids hidden parallel octaves and fifths. At the piano, this principle shows up whenever the left hand descends while the right hand climbs toward a climax.

Mirror patterns appear in many pieces, from classical cadences where the bass falls while the melody rises, to modern film cues that open outward from a single note. Practising contrary motion helps you recognise these shapes when reading and makes them easier to play.

FAQ

Q: Should beginners learn contrary motion first?

A: Many teachers introduce it early because mirrored fingering is easy to remember, but both forms are useful.

Q: Which keys are symmetrical in contrary motion?

A: C, G, D, A and E major from their tonic are close to symmetrical, and D and A-flat have pleasing mirror layouts.

Q: How many octaves should I practise?

A: Start with one, then extend to two and later four once the hands stay together.

Q: Does contrary motion help with pieces?

A: Yes, mirror patterns appear in cadences and accompaniments, and the coordination carries over to parallel playing.
