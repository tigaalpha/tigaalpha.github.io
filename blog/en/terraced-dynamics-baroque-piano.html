Terraced Dynamics: Playing Baroque Contrast on a Modern Piano

Music theory · 2026-11-11

Terraced dynamics are sudden changes between distinct volume levels, such as loud then soft, rather than gradual crescendos and diminuendos. The term is often used to describe Baroque keyboard and ensemble music, where contrasts between sections or repeated phrases create a step-like pattern of loudness, like terraces on a hillside. Understanding the idea helps pianists make thoughtful choices when playing music written before the piano became common.

Instruments that shaped the style

Many Baroque keyboard works were written for the harpsichord or organ. A harpsichord plucks strings mechanically, so pressing a key harder does not make the note much louder. To change volume or colour, players used different keyboards, called manuals, or engaged additional sets of strings with stops. Organs similarly change sound by switching between stops and manuals.

These instruments therefore encouraged contrast by blocks: a phrase on one manual, then the next on another. Gradual swells were difficult, so composers and performers thought in clear levels. A two-manual harpsichord, for instance, might have a brighter, fuller upper keyboard and a gentler lower one, and a player could move a hand from one to the other at the start of a new phrase. Listening to a harpsichord recording with this in mind makes the stepped contrasts easy to hear.

Echo effects

A common use of terraced dynamics is the echo: a phrase is played loudly, then repeated softly as if heard from a distance. Some Baroque pieces even include the word echo in their titles or mark repetitions with forte and piano.

  • First statement: full and confident.
  • Repeat: softer, lighter, sometimes with a different touch.
  • Return: back to the full sound for the next new idea.

Echoes are a simple, effective way to add variety to repeated material.

Contrast between sections

In larger works, such as concertos and suites, sections often alternate between full ensemble passages and smaller groups or solo lines. Keyboard arrangements of such music reflect these contrasts with changes of texture and volume. Recognising these changes in the score helps pianists plan dynamics even when few markings are present.

Was it really all terraces?

Musicians and scholars have debated how strictly terraced Baroque performance was. Singers, string players and wind players could shape notes and phrases with subtle swells, and keyboard players created the impression of nuance through articulation, ornaments and timing. The terraced idea is a useful guide to contrast, not a rule that forbids shaping within phrases.

On the modern piano, which can play both gradual and sudden changes, pianists can combine clear terraced contrasts with gentle phrase shaping.

Applying terraced dynamics on the piano

Several practical strategies help.

Identify blocks

Mark repeated phrases, new sections and changes of texture in the score. Decide which blocks should be louder or softer.

Change touch along with volume

For softer echoes, use a lighter touch and perhaps less pedal; for louder sections, use fuller arm weight. This creates contrast in colour as well as volume.

Keep each level stable

Within a block, avoid big swells that blur the contrast. Small phrase shaping is fine, but the overall level should remain clear.

Use articulation

Detached or connected playing can mark sections just as clearly as volume.

Practising contrast

Record a short Baroque piece twice: once with gradual dynamics throughout, once with clear terraced contrasts. Listen to both and decide which suits the music better, or combine elements of each. Terraced ideas in later music

Although associated with the Baroque, sudden dynamic contrasts appear in many later styles. Classical composers used abrupt shifts for surprise, and modern composers sometimes write block-like dynamics for dramatic effect. Recognising the technique helps pianists interpret these moments clearly.

FAQ

Q: Why are there few dynamic markings in Baroque scores?

A: Performers were expected to make many decisions themselves, guided by style, structure and the instruments of the time.

Q: Should Baroque music on piano never use crescendos?

A: Not necessarily. Many pianists use gentle shaping within phrases while keeping clear contrasts between sections.

Q: What is an echo in Baroque music?

A: A repeated phrase played more softly, imitating an echo.

Q: Can terraced dynamics be used in modern pieces?

A: Yes. Sudden contrasts are effective in many styles when the music benefits from clear changes.
