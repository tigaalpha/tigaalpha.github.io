How to Use IMSLP for Piano: Find, Choose and Practise Free Scores

Practice tips · 2026-10-31

IMSLP, the International Music Score Library Project, is a free online library of scores, most of them out of copyright and some shared openly by living composers. For piano learners it is a practical way to find scores of classical pieces, compare different editions and print the music you want to practise. To use it well, search by composer and piece, check the copyright note for your country, choose a clean edition, and print or mark it up before you start.

This guide is a hands-on workflow. It covers what the library is, how to pick the right edition from a long list of files, how to make old scans easier to read, and how to turn a downloaded score into a practice plan.

What IMSLP is

  • Also called: the Petrucci Music Library, after Ottaviano Petrucci, a Venetian printer who published one of the first books of music printed with movable type in 1501.
  • Started: in 2006 by Edward W. Guo, then a music student.
  • How it works: it is a wiki. Volunteers upload scans, add details about each edition, and organise works by composer, genre and instrument.
  • What it holds: mostly older scores whose copyright has expired in at least some countries, plus some modern works shared by composers under open licences.
  • Cost: browsing and downloading are free. An optional paid membership removes waiting time before downloads.

Before you download: copyright is local

Copyright terms differ from country to country. A score may be public domain in one place and still protected in another, often because of when the composer or editor died. IMSLP shows notes on each file about its status in several regions, and some files are restricted in certain countries.

The safe habit is simple: read the copyright note on the file page, and follow the rules of the country where you live. When in doubt, choose an older edition whose status is clear, or buy a modern printed edition.

Choosing an edition from a long list

Popular piano works can have a dozen or more files. Here is how to narrow them down:

Look at the publisher and date

First editions and early editions show what the composer approved. Later editions often add fingering, pedalling and phrasing from an editor.

Decide how much editing you want

Beginners and returning players often appreciate fingering suggestions. Advanced players may prefer a cleaner text with fewer additions, so they can make their own choices.

Check the scan quality

Open the preview and zoom in. Faint staff lines, crooked pages or cut-off margins will slow your reading. A clean scan of a reasonable edition is more useful than a blurry scan of a famous one.

Compare one tricky bar

Find a passage you know is hard and look at it in two or three editions. Differences in fingering, slurs or notes will show you which editor's choices suit your hands.

Making old scans easy to read

Old engravings can look small and dense. A few simple fixes help:

  • Print at a larger size. Many scans can be enlarged a little without losing sharpness. Bigger noteheads mean fewer reading errors.
  • Crop the margins. Trimming wide borders lets the music fill more of the page.
  • Number the bars. Write bar numbers at the start of each line. This makes practice notes and loops much easier.
  • Mark repeats and jumps. Older scores sometimes use signs you are not used to. Circle them in colour so you never miss a turn.
  • Use a tablet for rehearsal, paper for marking. Many learners read from a screen but still like a printed copy for pencil notes.

Turning a score into a practice plan

A downloaded score is only the start. Try this four-step routine for any new piece:

Survey

Read through the whole piece without playing. Find the key, the time signature, the tempo marking and any sections that repeat.

Divide

Split the music into sections of four to eight bars. Write a short label for each, such as "A theme", "running bass" or "loud ending".

Rank

Mark the two or three hardest sections. These get the first and freshest minutes of every practice session.

Track

Keep a small log in the margin: the date, the tempo you reached, and one thing to fix next time.

This method works with any score, from a short teaching piece to a longer sonata movement, and it fits neatly alongside a structured piano course.

FAQ: using free scores

Q: Is everything on IMSLP free to use?

A: Downloading is free, but copyright status depends on your country. Always check the note on each file before using it.

Q: Which edition should a beginner choose?

A: Usually a clear edition with fingering suggestions. Compare a difficult bar in two editions to see which feels better for your hands.

Q: Can I use these scores in lessons?

A: Many teachers and students do, as long as the copyright status allows it where they live.

Q: Does IMSLP have recordings too?

A: Yes. Some work pages include recordings shared under open licences, which can help you hear a piece before learning it.

Q: How do I practise from a scan on a tablet?

A: Crop and enlarge the pages, add bar numbers, and keep a printed copy for pencil marks if you like writing notes by hand.
