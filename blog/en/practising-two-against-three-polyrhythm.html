Practising Two-Against-Three Polyrhythms at the Piano

Practice tips · 2026-11-12

Two-against-three is a common texture at the piano: one hand plays two even notes in the time of the beat, or of a longer pulse, while the other plays three. The difficulty is not the notes themselves, but the fact that the mid-points do not coincide. If you try to join the hands at full speed first, one part usually pulls the other out of shape. The reliable path is to give each hand its own even grouping, name the shared beat clearly, and only then put the parts together at a tempo where both groupings stay even.

Count the shared beat before you combine the hands

Choose a slow pulse you can tap with the foot or with a click. Say "one-and" for the duple part and "one-trip-let" for the triple part, always landing "one" together. Do this without notes, then with each hand alone on a single key, so the rhythm is the only job. If the click and the spoken count drift, slow down until they agree. Writing a small mark on each shared beat in the score keeps the eye honest later.

Practise each hand as an even group on the real keys

Once the count is steady, play the duple hand on its written notes, still with the click on the shared beat, and then the triple hand the same way. The figuration should feel even inside its own group. If a fingering makes the triple part bunch toward the start of the beat, fix the fingering now, while the hands are still separate. Do not add the second hand until each part can play two or three bars alone without losing the pulse.

  • Tap or click only the shared beat, not every subdivided note.
  • Speak or think each grouping separately until it is even on one key.
  • Play each written hand alone at the same slow pulse.
  • Join the hands for two bars only, and listen for evenness in both parts.
  • Raise the tempo in small steps only when both groupings still meet on the beat.

Join the hands in a short window, then widen it

Start with two bars, or even one bar repeated. Listen for the duple part staying even, not for a general sense of busyness. If the triple part collapses into the duple part, take the triple hand back to a single key for a moment, then rejoin. When the short window is secure, add a bar on each side, still at the same tempo. Speed is the last change, not the first.

Keep the texture when the music later asks for tone

In a piece, one of the two parts is often a melody and the other an accompaniment. After the rhythm is steady, return to voicing: the melody needs a clearer tone, and the accompanying group should stay even without becoming loud. Do this at the same careful tempo first. If you add voicing and speed in the same sitting, the rhythm usually slips. Mark in the score which hand carries the line, so the ears have a job as well as the pulse.

FAQ

Q: Should I always put the triples in the right hand?

A: No. Follow the score. Practise the triple grouping in whichever hand writes it, including in the left hand when the accompaniment is in three.

Q: Is it helpful to play both parts in one hand first?

A: A slow one-hand sketch can show where the notes fall, but each hand still needs its own even grouping on the real keys before you rely on that sketch.

Q: What if I can play it only when I watch my hands?

A: Keep the tempo slow enough to look at the score on the shared beats. The click can hold the pulse while the eyes stay on the page.

Q: How do I know the tempo is ready to rise?

A: When two or three joined bars stay even in both parts, with the shared beat matching the click, you may move the click a little.

Q: Does this method also help three-against-four?

A: The same idea applies: settle each grouping alone against a shared beat, then join a short window. The counts will be different, but the order of work is the same.
