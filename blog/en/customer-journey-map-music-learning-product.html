Mapping the Customer Journey of a Music Learning Product

News · 2026-11-05

A customer journey map is a simple drawing of every step a person takes with a product, from the moment they first hear about it to the point where it becomes a habit or is abandoned. For music learning products, such as courses, studios and practice apps, the map reveals where motivated beginners lose energy. It moves the conversation away from "we need more advertising" towards "we need fewer reasons for people to quit".

The stages most music learners pass through

Every product is different, but most music learning journeys share a recognisable shape. Naming the stages helps a team agree on where problems sit.

Spark

Something makes a person want to play: a song in a film, a child's school concert, a video of a friend at the keyboard.

Search

The person looks for options, reads articles, watches short clips and compares formats such as studio lessons, online courses and apps.

First try

A trial lesson, a free week or a first practice session. Expectations meet reality here.

Early weeks

The learner tries to build a routine around work, school and family. This is where many journeys quietly end.

Habit

Practice feels normal. The learner starts to set goals, perform for others and recommend the product.

Drawing the map

Use a long sheet of paper or a shared whiteboard. Across the top, write the stages. Under each stage, fill in four rows: what the learner does, what they think, what they feel and which touchpoints they meet, such as a page, an email, a lesson or a notification. Fill the rows from real evidence: support messages, teacher notes, survey answers and short interviews. Guesses are allowed only when marked as guesses.

The feelings row is the most revealing. A beginner who feels clumsy in week two needs a different message from one who feels bored. Mixing them up leads to the wrong fixes.

Finding the moments that matter

Once the map is filled, look for three kinds of moments:

  • Drop points, where many people stop without saying why.
  • Friction points, where people complain about setup, scheduling or confusion.
  • Bright spots, where learners express pride, such as playing a first full song.

Bright spots deserve as much attention as problems. If finishing a first song creates pride, the product should help learners reach that moment sooner. If setting up a keyboard with a phone causes friction, a short visual guide may do more for growth than a new campaign.

Acting on the map

Pick one or two moments and design small experiments. Shorten the first lesson so the learner plays something recognisable within minutes. Send a friendly check-in on the day many learners usually go quiet. Offer parents a short progress summary at the end of the first month. Measure whether the next group of learners moves further along the journey than the previous one.

Avoid fixing everything at once. Big redesigns make it hard to know what helped. A sequence of small, measured changes builds understanding as well as results.

Keeping the map alive

Journeys change as the product, the audience and the season change. Revisit the map every few months with fresh evidence. Invite teachers and support staff, because they hear what learners say in private. When the team shares one picture of the learner's path, marketing, teaching and product decisions start to pull in the same direction.

Journey maps for different learner groups

One map rarely fits everyone. Adult beginners, parents choosing for a child and returning players who stopped years ago each walk a different path. An adult may hesitate at the search stage because of embarrassment, while a parent may hesitate at the first try because the child loses focus. Draw a light version of the map for each major group and compare them side by side. Differences show where a single message or a single onboarding flow is failing someone. Small, targeted changes, such as a calmer first lesson for adults or a shorter, game-like start for children, often follow directly from that comparison.

FAQ

Q: Who should take part in drawing the map?

A: Include people from marketing, teaching, product and support so every touchpoint is represented.

Q: How much research is needed?

A: Start with what you already have, such as messages and notes, then add a few short interviews to fill gaps.

Q: Is a journey map the same as a sales funnel?

A: No. A funnel counts conversions; a journey map also captures thoughts and feelings, which explain why people move or stop.

Q: How often should the map be updated?

A: Review it every few months or after any major change in the product or audience.
