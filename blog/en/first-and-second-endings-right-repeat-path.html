First and Second Endings: Taking the Right Repeat Path

Music theory · 2026-11-21

First and second endings are the numbered brackets you often see near a repeat sign. The first time you play the section, you take the bars under the bracket marked 1. When you return and reach that point again, you skip those bars and play the bars under the bracket marked 2 instead. The device saves space on the page and keeps a melody from ending the same way twice.

How the brackets sit on the page

The first ending usually appears just before the repeat sign, with a hook or a line above the bars and a small number 1. The second ending follows immediately after, with a number 2, and it often leads onward into new music. Some pieces add a third ending later, but two is the pattern you will meet most often.

If the print is faint, trace the bracket with a pencil so your eye can find the exit before you arrive. A missed ending is usually a looking problem, not a playing problem.

Walking the path in order

Think of the repeated section as a loop with two exits.

  • Play from the start of the section up to the first ending, including the bars under bracket 1.
  • When you reach the repeat sign, go back to the beginning of the section, or to the sign that marks the return point.
  • The second time, skip bracket 1 and play bracket 2, then continue into the next part of the piece.

If you say this path aloud once before you play, the first run-through is already clearer. Many slips happen because the hands keep going in a straight line while the eyes have not yet planned the turn.

Why composers use two endings

The first ending often contains a short turn-around: a few notes that lead you back to the opening. The second ending replaces that turn-around with a close, or with a bridge into a new section. Writing both versions in full would take more staves; the brackets keep the shared music written only once.

This is useful for learners as well. You can practise the shared bars thoroughly, then practise each ending on its own, and only later join them in the right order.

A practice order that prevents wrong turns

Isolate the junction. Play the two bars before the first ending, the first ending itself, and the return to the opening, several times at a slow speed. Then practise the same two bars before the junction, but this time go into the second ending and onward. Finally, play the whole loop in the correct sequence.

If the endings feel similar, sing or tap the last beat of each one. The last beat is often where they diverge, and a clear last beat is easier to remember than a long string of notes.

Marks that help on a busy page

A small arrow toward the repeat, a boxed 1 and 2, or a light colour on the second ending can make the path obvious without cluttering the score. Write only what you need. The aim is to see the exit early, not to cover the notes.

Some editions place the second ending on a new system. Look ahead at the end of the line so the jump does not surprise you.

When more than two endings appear

A few pieces use a third or fourth ending, each taken on a later pass. The same logic applies: play the ending whose number matches the time you are going through the section, and skip the others. Count the passes quietly, or write the order at the top of the page if the form is long.

If you are unsure, walk the form with a finger on the page before you play. The map should be clear in silence first.

Listening for the form

Once the path is reliable, listen to how the two endings change the story of the phrase. The first ending often feels open, as if the music has more to say. The second ending often feels more settled, or it may lean forward into a new idea. Hearing that difference helps you remember which way to go, because the music itself is giving you a reason to choose.

FAQ

Q: Do I play the first ending on the second time through?

A: No. Skip the first ending the second time and play the second ending instead.

Q: What if there is a third ending as well?

A: Take the ending whose number matches the time you are passing through that section, and skip the others.

Q: Should I practise the endings separately?

A: Yes. Practise each ending with the bars that lead into it, then put the full loop together in order.

Q: The second ending starts on a new line. How do I avoid getting lost?

A: Look ahead at the end of the previous line and mark the entry clearly, so the jump is planned.
