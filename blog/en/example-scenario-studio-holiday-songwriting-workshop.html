Example Scenario: A Studio Runs a Holiday Songwriting Workshop

Stories · 2026-11-11

This scenario is invented for illustration and does not describe a real studio. It imagines a small piano and keyboard studio that wants to offer something new during the school holidays: a five-day songwriting workshop where young learners write, arrange and record a short original song. The example walks through the planning decisions so other studios can judge whether a similar programme would suit them.

Why the studio considers a workshop

In this example, regular weekly lessons slow down during holidays because families travel or change routines. The studio has empty rooms in the mornings and teachers who want to try different kinds of teaching. A short, intensive workshop could use that capacity, introduce the studio to new families and give current students a creative project beyond their usual repertoire. The owner also hopes the experience will feed back into regular lessons, since students who have written a song tend to listen to chord changes and lyrics differently afterwards.

Defining the programme

The team decides on clear boundaries before advertising anything.

  • Five mornings of three hours each, with a break every hour.
  • Ages ten to fourteen, with basic keyboard skills required.
  • A maximum of eight participants so each one gets individual attention.
  • A shared goal: each participant finishes a short song with a chord progression, melody and simple lyrics, recorded on the final day.

Teaching team

One teacher leads the songwriting sessions while another supports keyboard parts and recording.

Planning each day

Day one introduces song structure through listening and simple chord loops. Day two focuses on melody, with participants improvising over their chosen chords. Day three explores lyrics, including how syllables fit rhythms. Day four is for arranging and rehearsing. Day five is recording and a short sharing session for families.

Each day ends with a short reflection, where participants say one thing they discovered. This helps teachers adjust the next day's plan.

Setting the price and policies

The studio calculates the costs of teacher time, materials, recording equipment and room use, then sets a price that covers these with a modest margin. The policies are written clearly on the registration page: what is included, the refund policy if a child cannot attend, and how recordings will be shared. Recordings are given only to each participant's family unless parents agree otherwise in writing.

Promoting the workshop honestly

Announcements describe the daily activities, the age range and the maximum group size factually. The studio avoids phrases that suggest scarcity unless places are genuinely almost full. Current students receive the information first through the regular newsletter, and the studio also shares it on its own social pages and at the local library notice board.

Using technology thoughtfully

During the workshop, participants use notation software to sketch melodies and a simple recording setup for the final song. Between sessions, those who want extra practice can use an app such as TIGA ALPHA to work on keyboard parts, checking notes and rhythm before the next morning. The teachers present these tools as aids, keeping the focus on listening and creative choices.

Reviewing the results

After the workshop, the team asks families for brief feedback and reviews its own notes. In this imagined case, they find that the lyric day felt rushed and that participants wanted more time to rehearse together. For the next workshop, they plan to start lyrics earlier and add a group performance piece alongside the individual songs.

What other studios can learn

The scenario highlights a few transferable points. Choose a clear, achievable outcome. Keep groups small enough for individual support. Write policies on refunds and recordings before registration opens. Promote factually. Collect feedback and adjust. A short workshop does not need to be large or elaborate to be valuable.

FAQ

Q: Is this a real workshop?

A: No. It is a fictional scenario designed to illustrate planning steps.

Q: Do participants need to read music?

A: In this example, basic keyboard skills are required, but songwriting can also be taught by ear.

Q: How should recordings of children be handled?

A: Share them only with consent from parents or guardians and follow privacy laws.

Q: Can a workshop like this run online?

A: Parts of it can, though group rehearsal and recording are often easier in person.
