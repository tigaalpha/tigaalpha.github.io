The Neapolitan Sixth on Piano: A Dark Chord Before the Cadence

Music theory · 2026-11-11

The Neapolitan sixth is a major chord built on the lowered second step of a scale, most often played in first inversion so that the fourth step of the key sits in the bass. In C minor that means a D-flat major triad with F underneath: F, A-flat, D-flat. It usually appears just before the dominant, where its unusual colour makes the final cadence feel heavier and more dramatic.

Where the name comes from

The label points to a group of opera composers working in Naples in the eighteenth century who used the sound often, although they did not invent it and it can be found earlier. Theory teachers later attached the city name to the chord, and the term stuck. The word sixth refers to the interval between the bass note and the root above it in first inversion, so the full name simply describes a chord shape that was fashionable in one musical centre.

You do not need the history to use it, but the name helps you spot it in harmony textbooks and analysis notes, where it is often written as N6 or as a flat II with a small 6.

Spelling the chord in any key

The quickest method is to find the second note of the scale, lower it by a semitone, and build a major triad on that new note. Then move the third of that triad to the bottom.

  • In A minor: B becomes B-flat, the triad is B-flat, D, F, and the first-inversion shape has D in the bass.
  • In E minor: F-sharp becomes F, the triad is F, A, C, with A in the bass.
  • In D minor: E becomes E-flat, the triad is E-flat, G, B-flat, with G in the bass.
  • In C major: the chord is borrowed in the same way, D-flat, F, A-flat, which sounds even more surprising in a bright key.

A useful check is that the bass note of the Neapolitan sixth is always the same as the bass of the ordinary subdominant chord. That is why it slides so naturally into the place where a IV or ii chord would normally stand.

Hearing its job in a cadence

Play a simple minor-key cadence first: subdominant, dominant, tonic. Then replace the subdominant with the Neapolitan sixth and play it again. The bass does not move at all, but the upper notes suddenly lean downward with extra weight.

The falling semitone

The lowered second, D-flat in C minor, wants to fall. Most often it moves down through C to B, the raised seventh degree, inside the dominant chord. That little chromatic descent of a diminished third, D-flat to B, is the sound many listeners recognise as tragic or solemn.

The cadential six-four in between

In many classical pieces, a tonic six-four chord sits between the Neapolitan and the dominant. The bass stays on the fifth step while the upper voices step down, which delays the resolution and gives the moment more space.

Voicing it at the keyboard

Because the chord lies close to the tonic area, it is easy to play it in a muddy, crowded way. A few voicing habits keep it clear.

  • Double the bass note rather than the lowered second, so the chromatic note stays exposed and free to fall.
  • Keep the right-hand chord in the middle register, around the C above middle C, so it does not compete with the bass.
  • Move each voice by the shortest path into the next chord, which keeps the progression smooth and sounding like real part-writing.
  • Try the root position version as well, with D-flat in the bass. It sounds bolder and is common in later romantic music.

Finding it in real music

Once you know the sound, you will notice it in slow movements of sonatas, in dramatic piano preludes and in film scores that want a moment of shadow just before an ending. Many pieces use it only once, right at the climax, which is part of its power. Romantic composers sometimes stretch it further, approaching it from far-away keys or holding it for several bars.

Popular songwriters use a related move too: a major chord one semitone above the tonic, played just before the final chord. In that context it is often called a flat-two chord and can sound playful rather than sad, which shows how much the surrounding style shapes the meaning of the same notes.

Ten minutes with the Neapolitan

Set aside ten minutes for three steps. First, play the Neapolitan sixth in three minor keys, saying the note names aloud. Second, play the full cadence, Neapolitan to six-four to dominant to tonic, slowly and with a steady pulse. Third, improvise a four-bar phrase that ends with this cadence, keeping the melody simple so you can listen to the harmony.

FAQ

Q: Is the Neapolitan sixth only used in minor keys?

A: It is most common in minor, but composers also borrow it in major keys, where it sounds especially striking because the lowered note clashes with the bright scale.

Q: How do I recognise it by ear?

A: Listen for a major chord that sounds unexpectedly dark just before the dominant, often with a melody note that falls by a semitone or more toward the seventh degree.

Q: Why is it usually in first inversion?

A: Putting the fourth step in the bass lets the chord replace the subdominant smoothly and avoids awkward leaps in the lowest voice.

Q: Can beginners use it in their own pieces?

A: Yes. Once you can play a basic cadence, swapping in this chord is an easy way to add drama to the ending of a simple composition.
