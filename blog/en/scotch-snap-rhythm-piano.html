The Scotch Snap on Piano: A Short-Long Rhythm With a Bite

Practice tips · 2026-11-12

A Scotch snap on the piano is a short-long rhythm in which a brief, often accented note is followed at once by a longer note, commonly written as a semiquaver (sixteenth) plus a dotted quaver (dotted eighth), the reverse of a more familiar dotted pair. The character comes from the short first note. If that note is lengthened, the snap becomes an ordinary long-short pattern and the bite is gone.

Hearing the shape before you speed it up

Tap a steady beat, then speak a short syllable on the beat and a longer one after it, such as "tick — toooo." Transfer that shape to one key, then to the written pitches. The short note should be crisp, not crushed into silence, and the long note should sit calmly until the next beat or the next snap.

It can help to play the rhythm first as two even notes, then as a long-short dotted pair, then as the short-long snap, so the ear hears how the same two pitches change character when the lengths change.

Keeping the short note short

The usual error is to let the first note grow until it is almost even with the second. Practise a version that slightly exaggerates the shortness, then ease toward a natural snap. A metronome on the outer beat is useful: the first note must start with the click, and the long note must still be holding when the next click is near.

If the snap is slurred, the short note can be a light start of a pair rather than a sharp hammer, but it should still be short. If the snap is marked with a dash or a small accent, the first note may be clearer, yet it still must leave room for the long note.

Players sometimes correct a late first note by shortening the second one as well, and the whole beat shrinks. Hold the second note to the end of its written value. The snap lives inside the beat; it should not steal the following beat.

Snaps in a melody and in an accompaniment

Keep one layer steadier than the snap

When the snap is in the melody, keep the accompaniment simple and on time, so the short-long pair has a steady frame. When the snap is in an accompaniment under a smoother line, the left hand or the inner part should not drag the melody into the same bite unless the page asks for that. Hands-separate practice, then a quiet together pass, keeps the two layers distinct.

When several snaps sit in a row

A chain of snaps can feel jumpy. Keep the outer pulse obvious, and do not add a heavy accent on every short note if the line needs to run. Mark the start of the bar, and let some snaps be lighter than the first one in the group. If a chain always rushes, practise the last snap of the bar first, then add the earlier ones, so the end of the bar stays in place.

A small practice order

  • Speak the short-long shape on a count.
  • Play it on one key with a click on the beat.
  • Use the written pitches, still with the click.
  • Add the other hand, which often stays even.
  • Remove the click and check that the next bar still starts on time.

FAQ

Q: Is a Scotch snap the same as a dotted rhythm?

A: It is a dotted-style pair, but short then long, the reverse of the more common long-short dotted pair.

Q: Should the first note always be accented?

A: Often it has a slight bite. A heavy accent on every snap can sound stiff. Follow the mark and the line.

Q: What if I keep lengthening the short note?

A: Practise a slightly exaggerated short first note, then ease toward a natural snap, always starting with the beat.

Q: Can the snap fall over a bar line?

A: Usually the short note starts on a beat or a clear subdivision inside the bar. Read the written values carefully when the pair sits near a bar line.

Q: Does the name mean I should only use this rhythm in one style of music?

A: No. The name is a traditional label for the short-long shape. The same rhythm appears in many kinds of piano writing.
