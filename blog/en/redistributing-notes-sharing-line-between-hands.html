Redistributing Notes: Sharing a Line Between the Hands

Practice tips · 2026-11-21

Redistributing notes means moving one or more pitches from the hand that is written on the page to the other hand, so the phrase becomes easier to play or clearer to hear. The sound of the music stays the same. Only the division of labour changes. Pianists do this when a stretch is wide, when a melody needs a stronger finger, or when two parts collide in the same register.

Why the printed hands are not always the final plan

Editors usually write the upper staff for the right hand and the lower staff for the left, and that plan is a good starting point. It is not a law. If a right-hand chord leaves a melody note stranded at the edge of a stretch, the left hand may be free to take that note. If a left-hand inner voice sits under the right-hand thumb, a small swap can prevent a bump.

The test is simple: play the passage as written, then try a short share, and listen. Keep the version that is more comfortable and more even, as long as the sounding notes remain the same.

A careful method for deciding

Do not redistribute every awkward corner on the first day. Work in a clear order.

  • Play the passage slowly as printed and mark the exact notes that feel cramped or hidden.
  • Look at the other hand at that moment. If it has a spare finger and a clear path, try giving it one of those notes.
  • Check that the melody still sings and that the accompaniment still supports it.
  • Write a small cue in the score, such as “L.H.” or “R.H.” beside the moved note, so you do not forget later.

One well-chosen share is more helpful than a page covered with arrows.

Keeping the musical roles clear

When you move a note, you also move a responsibility. If the right hand gives a melody note to the left, the left hand must now shape that note as a melody, not as part of a soft accompaniment. Lean on it a little, and let the surrounding left-hand notes stay quieter. If the left hand gives a bass note to the right, the right hand must still provide a firm floor for the harmony.

It can help to practise the sounding parts, not the hands: play only the melody, then only the bass, then put the shared version together. Your ear stays in charge of the texture.

Physical comfort and a natural shape

A good redistribution reduces stretch, twisting, or a sudden jump. If the new plan creates a new stretch in the other hand, it is not yet a solution. Watch the wrists. They should look as calm as they do in an easy five-finger pattern. The thumb is useful for taking a nearby note, but it should not hook sideways under a tight chord if another finger is free.

Give the changed fingering a few slow repetitions on its own, the way you would learn any new fingering. The share needs to become automatic, or it will arrive late when you return to full speed.

When not to change the printed plan

If the original division is already comfortable, leave it. If a teacher has asked you to keep a particular fingering for a musical reason, follow that request. And if a share would make a legato line impossible, because the new hand cannot join the notes, keep the printed version and look for another solution, such as a different fingering in the same hand.

Redistribution is a tool, not a habit to apply everywhere.

Putting the share back into the phrase

Once the swapped notes feel natural, play a longer span: a bar before the share, the shared beat, and a bar after. Listen for a bump in tone at the moment the other hand takes over. If you hear one, match the touch of the two fingers that meet at the join. The listener should not be able to tell that the labour was divided in a new way.

Then return to your usual practice of the piece, with the cue in the score as a quiet reminder.

FAQ

Q: Is it wrong to change which hand plays a written note?

A: No, if the sounding pitch stays the same and the phrase becomes clearer or more comfortable.

Q: How should I mark a redistributed note?

A: A small “L.H.” or “R.H.” beside the note is usually enough. Keep the page easy to read.

Q: What if the other hand is already busy?

A: Do not force a share. Look for a different fingering in the original hand, or leave the passage as printed.

Q: Will this confuse me when I come back to the piece later?

A: It should not, if you write the cue clearly and practise the new plan slowly until it feels ordinary.
