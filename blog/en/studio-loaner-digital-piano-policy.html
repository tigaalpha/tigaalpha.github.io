Loaner Digital Pianos at a Studio: When a Learner's Instrument Is Out

News · 2026-11-12

A piano studio sometimes has a learner whose home instrument is temporarily out of use: a piano is being moved, repaired, or regulated, or a digital piano has failed. A small loan stock of digital pianos can keep practice going for a few weeks. Without a written policy, the loan can turn into an open-ended arrangement, with no clear return date and no record of what condition the instrument was in. A short loan policy keeps the focus on practice, protects the instruments, and makes the arrangement fair to other families who may need a loaner later.

Write who may borrow, for how long, and what is included

Decide in advance whether loans are offered to current learners only, for a set number of weeks, and whether a stand, a pedal, a bench, and a power supply are included. Write those items on a simple loan sheet. A clear end date, with a way to request a short extension, prevents a loan from silently becoming permanent. If there is a deposit or a fee, state it plainly in the written terms and do not change it on the day of collection.

Write the loan end date on the sheet before the instrument leaves

Check the instrument together at hand-over and return

At hand-over, play a few keys across the range, test the pedal and the volume, and note any marks on the case. Both the studio and the family can sign or initial the sheet. On return, repeat the same short check. This is not a full technical inspection; it is a shared record of what went out and what came back. If a fault appears during the loan, the family should report it promptly rather than attempting a repair at home.

Set care rules that suit a home practice room

Ask that the loaner be kept away from direct sun, liquids, and very damp places, that the cable be routed so no one trips on it, and that the cover be closed after practice if the model has one. Explain how to set a comfortable volume and how to use headphones if the household needs quiet hours. Agreements of this kind should comply with local consumer and contract law where the studio operates, including in Thailand, and should be read as a practical outline rather than a legal opinion.

Keep the loan stock fair and playable

Keep a short list of who has which loaner and when it is due. Rotate instruments if one is borrowed far more than the others, and set aside time between loans to clean the keys and test every function. If a loaner has a fault that cannot be fixed quickly, take it out of the pool rather than lending it with a note of apology. The aim is a small stock of dependable instruments that support practice during a gap, not a hidden rental business.

A loaner that is booked to go out the next morning should still receive the short function check, even if the previous return was only the night before. Skipping that check is how a silent key or a missing pedal leaves the studio with the next family.

  • Write who may borrow, for how long, and which accessories are included.
  • Check keys, pedal, volume, and case marks together at hand-over and return.
  • Give simple care rules for sun, liquids, cables, and the cover.
  • Keep a list of current loans and due dates.
  • Remove a faulty loaner from the pool until it is repaired.

FAQ

Q: Should the studio charge for a loaner?

A: That is a business decision. If there is a fee or a deposit, write it clearly before the loan begins and apply it consistently.

Q: What if the loaner is damaged at home?

A: Follow the written loan terms. Report the problem promptly, and do not attempt an internal repair.

Q: Can a loaner replace a home piano permanently?

A: The policy is for a temporary gap. If a family needs a long-term instrument, discuss options separately from the loan pool.

Q: Do we need a written agreement for a short loan?

A: A short written sheet helps everyone remember dates and condition. Keep it simple, and seek local advice if the terms become complex.

Q: What should the learner practise on a loaner?

A: The usual assignments, with a note to the teacher that the touch and pedal may differ from the regular instrument.
