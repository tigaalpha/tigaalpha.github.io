AI Source Separation: Splitting a Mix Into Stems

News · 2026-11-13

AI source separation is the task of taking a finished mix and pulling it apart into stems, which are recordings that try to isolate one part of the texture, such as the vocal, the drums, the bass or the piano. A listener can then hear one layer more clearly, mute another, or study how the parts fit together. The software does not receive the original studio tracks. It has to estimate each part from the single stereo file that remains after mixing, which is why the results can be impressive and still imperfect.

Why a mixed recording is hard to unmix

Once sounds have been blended, their frequencies overlap. A piano chord can occupy the same pitch region as a voice. Cymbals can mask the upper partials of a keyboard line. Reverb smears the edges of every attack so that one instrument's tail sits on top of another instrument's note. There is no simple filter that can cut "only the piano" out of that blend.

A time-frequency map

Many systems begin by turning the mix into a spectrogram, a map of how energy is spread across frequencies over time. The picture does not label instruments, but it makes patterns visible: a voice may appear as a wandering bright line, drums as short vertical bursts, and a pad as a smoother band of energy.

Learned masks

The model then estimates a mask for each stem: a set of values that say how much of each tiny region of the map should be kept for that part. Training uses many songs for which the original isolated tracks still exist, so the system can compare its guess with the true stem and improve. After training, it can be pointed at a new mix it has never seen.

What separated stems are useful for

Pianists and other learners reach for stems for several practical reasons:

  • To hear a keyboard part more clearly inside a dense pop or film cue.
  • To practise along with a track after the original keyboard has been reduced or removed.
  • To study how a bass line and a left-hand pattern share the low register.
  • To build a simple rehearsal mix, for example voice and piano only.
  • To check whether a harmony is coming from the keys, a guitar, or a stacked vocal.

Where automatic unmixing still struggles

Separation has become far more usable, yet the output should be treated as a sketch.

  • Quiet inner voices and soft pedal resonance are easy to lose or to leak into the wrong stem.
  • Two similar sounds, such as two keyboards or a piano and a clean guitar, may be split incompletely.
  • Fast releases and hall reverb often leave metallic smears or watery gaps.
  • A solo piano recording does not need this tool, and running it anyway can invent faint extra layers.

If a stem will be used for close practice, listen through once for musical sense, not only for whether the main instrument is present.

A careful way to study a mix with stems

You can turn a separation result into an ear-training session rather than a novelty.

Hear the full mix first

Write a short note of what you think each part is doing: who keeps the pulse, who carries the tune, and how busy the accompaniment feels.

Solo one stem, then put it back

Listen to the estimated piano alone, then return it to the mix. Notice what the isolation made obvious, and what only makes sense in context.

Hunt for leftovers

On the "piano" stem, listen for stray consonants, hi-hat ticks or bass thumps. On the other stems, listen for missing keyboard notes. Those leftovers teach you how mixed sound actually occupies the same space.

FAQ

Q: Is a stem the same as the original studio track?

A: No. A stem estimated from a mix is a reconstruction. It can be close enough to study, but it is not the clean recording made at the session.

Q: Can source separation pull a piano part out of a full band?

A: Often well enough to follow the part, especially if the piano is fairly clear in the mix. Soft inner notes and heavy effects are less reliable.

Q: Does this replace learning to hear inside a mix yourself?

A: No. The tool can highlight a layer, but judging balance, voicing and musical role still depends on human listening.

Q: Will separation tell me the exact notes of a piano part?

A: Not by itself. Turning a stem into notation is a further task, usually called transcription, and it has its own errors.
