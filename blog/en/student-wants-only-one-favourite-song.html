When a Student Wants to Play Only One Favourite Song

Stories · 2026-11-08

Every piano teacher meets this moment sooner or later. A student sits down, eyes bright, and says there is only one song they want to play. It might be a film theme, a tune from a game, a song a grandparent used to hum, or something from a playlist. Scales, method books and other pieces feel like obstacles in the way. It is tempting to see this as a problem to fix. It is more useful to see it as a door that is already open.

Why one song can matter so much

A favourite song usually carries a feeling, a memory or a sense of identity. Playing it is not just a musical goal; it is a personal one. When a student says "only this one", they are often saying "this is why I came". Respecting that reason builds trust, and trust is what later allows a teacher to suggest something new.

Brushing the request aside can quietly send the message that the student's taste does not count. Families notice this too. A gentler path is to agree to the song openly and then plan how it will teach as many skills as possible.

Turning the song into a full lesson plan

Almost any piece can be broken into building blocks. The teacher's job is to find them and make each one visible.

  • Melody: learn the main tune with one hand, at a simplified level if needed.
  • Rhythm: clap and count the trickiest pattern before playing it.
  • Harmony: identify the three or four chords that support the tune.
  • Reading: write out a short version so the student sees the notes they already know by ear.
  • Expression: decide where the song gets louder or softer, and why.

Each block becomes a small win. Without noticing, the student is practising reading, rhythm, chords and phrasing, all through music they care about.

Arrange it in layers

Start with a very simple arrangement: melody only. Then add single bass notes. Then add simple chords. Each new layer is a reason to return to the song, and each one introduces a skill the student will use in every future piece.

Widening the circle without shame

Once the favourite song is going well, the student has proof that practice works. That is the right time to widen the repertoire, and the way it is done matters.

Use bridges, not replacements

Suggest a second piece that shares something with the first: the same key, a similar rhythm, the same mood, or the same composer or artist. Say why. "This uses the same left-hand pattern you already learned" is far more inviting than "now it is time for real music".

Keep the favourite in the rotation

Do not retire the song too quickly. Let it stay as a warm-up or a reward at the end of a session. Knowing it will not be taken away makes it easier for the student to try something unfamiliar.

Let the student choose between two options

Offer two new pieces and let the student pick. Choice keeps ownership with the learner, which is exactly what the favourite song gave them in the first place.

What families can do at home

Parents and carers can help a great deal without any musical training. Ask the student to play the favourite song for the family and to explain one thing they learned from it, such as a chord name. Celebrate progress on the song, then show equal interest when a new piece appears. Avoid comparing the student to siblings or friends who play different music.

When the song is too hard

Sometimes the chosen piece is well beyond the student's current level. Honesty helps here, delivered kindly. A teacher can say the full version is a long-term goal and offer a short, simplified version now. One kind option is to keep the original recording as a "someday" target while the student enjoys an easier arrangement today. That way the dream stays alive and the student still experiences success this week.

FAQ

Q: Is it wrong to learn mostly by ear from one song?

A: Not at all. Learning by ear is a real skill. Add reading gradually by writing down what the student already plays.

Q: How long should a student stay on one favourite song?

A: There is no fixed rule. Move on when the student feels proud of a version and is curious about the next layer or a new piece.

Q: What if the student refuses every new piece?

A: Look for a piece closer to the favourite in style, or let the student suggest a second song. Interest usually grows with confidence.

Q: Can an app help with a single song?

A: A practice app can help with steady rhythm and note accuracy, while a teacher helps choose the arrangement and next steps.
