Added-Sixth Chords on Piano: A Major Triad With an Extra Sixth

Music theory · 2026-11-11

An added-sixth chord on the piano is a complete triad with the sixth degree of that chord included as an extra colour tone, not as a replacement for the fifth and not as a seventh in disguise. In C major the notes are C, E, G and A, often written C6. The same idea in minor, C–E-flat–G–A, can be written Cm6. The sound is familiar from popular, film and some early twentieth-century textures: a stable triad that feels slightly richer, without the pull of a seventh that wants to resolve downward.

Spelling the added sixth, not a first-inversion seventh

C, E, G and A can also be read as A minor seventh in first inversion (A, C, E, G with C in the bass). Context decides the name. If the bass and the phrase treat C as the root, and the next chord does not behave like a minor-seventh function, it is an added sixth on C. If the harmony is clearly ii or a similar minor-seventh role with C merely in the bass, it is an inversion of A minor seventh.

A practical test is to play the same four notes with C in the bass, then with A in the bass. With C at the bottom, many ears hear a major triad with a sweet extra; with A at the bottom, the same keys often sound like a minor seventh chord. The bass and the surrounding progression are part of the definition, not an afterthought.

An added sixth does not evict the fifth. If you drop G from a C6, you are left with C, E and A, which can sound like a thinner triad or like an incomplete minor shape. For teaching and for clear voicing, include the fifth unless the register is too tight and a later arrangement note says otherwise.

Voicings that keep the extra note in the air

Close position in the right hand, with C, E, G and A inside one octave, is easy to take in one hand and can be muddy in a low octave. Move the same four notes up, or spread them: C in the left hand, E–G–A or G–A–E in the right. Putting the sixth on top (A above a C triad) is a bright, singing spacing. Putting the sixth just above the fifth (G then A) makes a soft cluster at the top of the triad that works well in a quiet accompaniment.

Useful voicing habits:

  • Keep the left hand on the root, or on root and fifth, so the identity of the chord stays obvious.
  • Give the sixth a little air; do not bury it inside a thick inner fist.
  • Avoid doubling the sixth in a low register, where it can sound like a clumsy seventh.
  • In a minor added sixth, check that the third is still minor, so the chord does not drift into a major colour by accident.

Telling C6 from Cmaj7 and from C7

Three neighbouring colours, three different pulls

C major seventh is C, E, G and B. C7 is C, E, G and B-flat. C6 is C, E, G and A. The sixth sits a tone below the octave, which is the same pitch class as a possible thirteenth in some jazz stacks, but in a simple added-sixth voicing there is no seventh at all. If a seventh is present, the symbol and the function change.

Play the three chords in the same register, same bass C, and listen for the different pulls. The major seventh has a bright, sometimes bittersweet friction against the root. The dominant seventh leans toward a later tonic. The added sixth usually sits still, like a colour on a stable chord, unless the phrase later treats it as a passing tone.

When a lead sheet writes 6, realise a triad plus the sixth and leave the seventh out. When it writes 6/9, a ninth may be added as well; that is a related colour, not a reason to sneak in a seventh unless the chart also says so.

Using the chord in a short accompaniment

A simple pattern is to play a major triad on the first beat and add the sixth on the second, then return to the triad. The extra note becomes a gentle decoration rather than a new harmony. Another pattern holds C6 under a melody that already uses A, so the accompaniment agrees with a melody tone instead of fighting it.

In a minor key, Cm6 often appears in a tonic or iv colour, and it can also act as a short neighbour to a dominant with a different bass. Move slowly. The added sixth should be easy to sing back; if it feels like a mistake, check the spelling and the bass.

FAQ

Q: Is a sixth chord just a seventh chord in inversion?

A: The notes can overlap with a first-inversion seventh, so the bass and the function have to decide the name. A C bass under C–E–G–A usually supports the added-sixth reading.

Q: Can I add a sixth to a minor triad?

A: Yes. The minor added sixth (root, minor third, fifth, major sixth) is a common colour. Keep the third clearly minor.

Q: Should the sixth resolve?

A: It can stay as a colour tone. It may also step to a nearby chord tone when the harmony changes, but it does not carry the same downward duty as a seventh.

Q: Why does my C6 sound like A minor?

A: The A may be too strong, or the bass may be light. Firm up a C in the left hand and slightly soften the sixth until the major triad is obvious again.

Q: Does add6 mean the same as 6?

A: In many charts, yes: a triad plus the sixth, without a seventh. If a score is inconsistent, look at the sounding notes and the bass, then choose the clearer label for your own markings.
