Collecting Student Testimonials with Consent and Care

Stories · 2026-11-07

Student testimonials can help new families decide whether a music studio is right for them, yet a testimonial is only valuable when it is genuine, specific and shared with clear permission. An ethical process asks politely, explains where words will appear, protects personal data and lets people change their mind. Following that process protects your students, respects privacy law and makes your marketing more believable than polished slogans ever could.

Why honest testimonials matter

Families comparing music lessons often feel unsure. They cannot judge teaching quality from a website alone, so they look for signs that real people have had a good experience. A short, honest comment from another parent or adult learner answers that need. Exaggerated praise has the opposite effect: readers sense when words have been written for them.

Specific beats glowing

"My daughter now practises without being reminded" tells a reader much more than "Amazing teacher, highly recommended". Specific details make a testimonial useful.

Asking at the right moment

A good time to ask is shortly after a positive moment, such as a recital, the end of a term or a learner finishing a piece they cared about. Ask in a relaxed way and make it easy to say no. Never link a request to lesson prices, grades or performance opportunities.

Good prompts to offer:

  • What did you hope to get from lessons when you started?
  • What has changed in your playing or practice since then?
  • Is there a moment in lessons you particularly remember?
  • What would you tell a friend who is thinking about starting?

These open questions invite real stories instead of generic praise.

Recording permission properly

Consent must be clear and recorded. Explain where the words will appear, such as your website, social media or a printed brochure, and for how long. For learners under the age of consent, ask a parent or guardian. Decide together whether to use a full name, first name or initials. Under privacy laws such as Thailand's PDPA, people have the right to withdraw consent, so keep a simple record and remove content promptly when asked.

Photos need separate permission

A written quote and a photo are different things. Ask separately for each, and never use a child's image without explicit permission from a guardian.

Editing without changing meaning

Light editing for grammar or length is acceptable, but the meaning must stay the same. Send the edited version back for approval before publishing. Never combine comments from different people into one quote, and never invent a testimonial, even as an example on a website.

Presenting testimonials honestly

Place testimonials near the information they support. A comment about beginner confidence fits on a beginner course page. A comment about recital preparation fits near your concert information. Show a mix of voices rather than only the most enthusiastic ones, and avoid implying that every learner will have the same result.

Avoid outcome promises

If a testimonial mentions an exam result or a competition, add context so readers understand that results vary with effort, time and individual goals.

Keeping testimonials fresh

Review your testimonials once or twice a year. Remove old ones from students who left long ago if they no longer reflect how you teach. Ask current families for new perspectives. A living collection shows that your studio is active and growing.

Letting learners tell it in their own words

Some learners prefer to speak rather than write. Offer a short conversation after a lesson and take notes, then send the written version for approval. Adult learners and older students may enjoy writing a few lines themselves. Either way, the words should sound like the person, not like your marketing copy.

When feedback is negative

Not every response will be positive, and that is useful. Thank the person, consider what they say and make changes where needed. Never pressure anyone to change a review. Handling criticism calmly often builds more trust than a page full of praise.

If a comment points to a real gap, such as unclear scheduling or slow replies, share what you changed with the person who raised it. That small act of follow-through is often remembered longer than the original problem.

FAQ

Q: Can I use a testimonial from a group chat message?

A: Only after asking the writer directly and getting clear permission for public use.

Q: Should testimonials include full names?

A: Not necessarily; first names or initials are often safer and still credible.

Q: How long can I keep a testimonial online?

A: As long as consent allows, and remove it promptly if the person withdraws consent.

Q: Can I edit a testimonial?

A: Light edits are fine, but send the final version back for approval.

Q: Are video testimonials better?

A: They can feel personal, but they need extra consent and care, especially for children.
