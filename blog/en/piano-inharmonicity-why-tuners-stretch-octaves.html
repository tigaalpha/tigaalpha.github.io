Piano Inharmonicity: Why Tuners Stretch the Octaves

Music theory · 2026-11-13

Piano inharmonicity is the fact that the upper partials of a piano string do not line up at exact whole-number multiples of the fundamental. Because a real string is stiff, not an ideal thin thread, each higher partial runs a little sharp. If a tuner sets every octave as a perfect 2:1 frequency ratio, those sharp partials can beat against the notes above them. The usual practical answer is stretched tuning: octaves are widened slightly so that the important partials agree more peacefully. The effect is small, but it is one reason a well-tuned piano can sound clearer than a strictly "mathematically perfect" grid of pitches.

Why a stiff string does not obey simple ratios

In an idealised vibrating string, the second partial would be exactly twice the fundamental, the third exactly three times, and so on. A piano string has stiffness and thickness. That stiffness makes the string behave a little more like a bar, especially in the high partials and in shorter, thicker strings. The partials are therefore slightly compressed in time and slightly raised in pitch compared with the simple model.

The amount of inharmonicity is not the same all over the keyboard.

  • Short, thick bass strings, especially around the break between wound and plain wire, can show strong inharmonicity.
  • Mid-range plain strings are usually more regular, which is why this region often becomes the starting point for a tuning.
  • Very high notes are short and stiff, so their partials also wander, and there is less of a rich harmonic series for the ear to grab.

This is a property of the instrument, not a mistake in manufacture. Two pianos of different length, stringing and condition will not have identical stretch needs.

What stretched octaves are trying to do

A tuner, whether working mainly by ear or with the help of a measuring device, is listening for a smooth, calm relationship between notes that share partials. When a lower A is played, one of its partials sits near the A an octave higher. If that partial is already sharp, the upper A may be set a little sharp as well, so the two sounds agree. The same process continues up and down the keyboard, which produces a gentle widening of the octaves from the centre outward.

Stretched tuning is therefore a compromise with several aims at once:

  • Reduce audible beating between a note and the prominent partials of notes below it.
  • Keep a reasonably even sense of scale from bass to treble.
  • Avoid a lifeless, narrow centre or a piercing, overly wide top.

Electronic tuning devices often include a stretch curve that can be adjusted for the instrument. The curve is a starting map, not a substitute for listening, because the ear still has to accept the result in the room.

What a player can actually hear

You do not need to tune a piano in order to notice inharmonicity.

Compare octaves in different registers

Play a low octave, a mid-range octave and a high octave, each as a calm pair. Listen for whether the beating and the brightness feel similar. On many instruments the top octave has a slightly wider, shinier character.

Listen to a single bass note

Hold a low wound-string note without pedal and try to hear the metallic upper partials inside it. Those slightly sharp upper components are the raw material of the problem tuners are balancing.

Play a slow scale through the break

Move from the wound bass strings onto the plain mid-range and listen for a change in colour. A good tuning does not remove that change, but it stops the pitches from fighting it.

Why this matters for practice and for digital pianos

If you practise on both an acoustic piano and a digital instrument, you may feel that one of them is "a little sharp at the top" or "a little narrow". That impression can come from different stretch curves, or from a digital sound that models or samples a different instrument. Neither is automatically wrong. It is useful to know that a perfectly even 2:1 octave is not the only musical target on a piano.

FAQ

Q: Does stretched tuning mean the piano is out of tune?

A: No. It is a deliberate, small widening of octaves so that the instrument's slightly sharp partials agree more comfortably.

Q: Why not tune every note to a strict theoretical frequency?

A: A strict grid can make some octaves and double octaves beat unpleasantly, because real piano partials are not exact multiples of the fundamental.

Q: Is the stretch the same on every piano?

A: No. String length, thickness, condition and the scale design all change the inharmonicity, so the needed stretch varies from one instrument to another.

Q: Should I try to retune a digital piano to match this?

A: Only if the instrument offers a clear, reversible tuning option and you know why you are changing it. For most practice, it is enough to listen and adapt.
