Example Scenario: A Teacher Builds a One-Page Lesson Card

Stories · 2026-11-19

This is an example scenario, not a true story: the teacher and student are imaginary and are described only to show one possible way of working. In the scenario, a piano teacher keeps a one-page lesson plan card for each student. The card has four boxes: warm-up, focus skill, new material and a note for home. Because everything fits on a single page, the teacher can glance at it quickly and spend most of the lesson listening rather than searching for notes.

The setting

Imagine a teacher called Mali who teaches several beginners and returners in a small home studio. Mali used to keep long handwritten notes, and often found it hard to see at a glance what a student had worked on last week. So Mali decides to try a simple card, with the same layout for every student.

The example is fictional, and the same idea can be adapted by any teacher, with any number of students.

The four boxes

Mali's card has four labelled areas.

  • Warm-up: a short scale, chord or rhythm exercise.
  • Focus skill: one thing to improve this week, such as even rhythm or relaxed wrists.
  • New material: the piece or section to start.
  • Note for home: two or three clear practice steps.

Mali writes in short phrases, not full sentences. That keeps the card readable on a music stand.

Choosing only one focus

The most important rule on the card is that there is one focus skill. When a lesson tries to fix five things at once, the student remembers none of them. In the scenario, Mali asks: what would help this student most this week? The answer goes in the focus box.

  • If notes are right but the pulse wobbles, the focus is steady counting.
  • If the hands tense up, the focus is a loose wrist.
  • If the piece is almost ready, the focus is a calm performance.

Choosing one focus makes feedback clearer and practice easier to remember.

During the lesson

Mali glances at the card before the student arrives. It shows what was assigned last time, so the lesson starts with a quick check, not a long search. At the end, Mali fills in the note for home together with the student. The student reads it aloud and says what each step means. This simple habit shows whether the instructions are clear.

After the lesson

Mali writes one line on the back of the card, such as "listen to the left hand next week", so that the next lesson begins with a clear thread. The card is then kept in a folder or photographed for the teacher's own records. Names and personal details are kept private, and the cards are not shared with anyone outside the studio.

Why the format may help

In the scenario, the card helps in three ways.

  • It makes planning quick, so the teacher is less rushed before each lesson.
  • It makes the lesson feel consistent, which many students like.
  • It gives parents or adult learners a short, clear summary if they ask.

It does not replace a teacher's judgement. If a student arrives tired or excited about something else, the plan can change.

Adapting the idea

Other teachers might use a different layout. Some prefer a digital document, others a paper card in a plastic sleeve. A returning adult learner may need a box for goals, while a young child may need a box for a sticker or drawing. The point is to keep the plan small enough to use.

Between lessons, some learners also use a practice app. TIGA ALPHA, for instance, offers short lessons with feedback, which can sit beside a teacher's plan without replacing the teacher's guidance.

Questions to ask yourself

  • Can I explain this week's focus in one sentence?
  • Is the note for home clear enough for a student to follow alone?
  • Do I leave enough time to listen to the student play?

FAQ

Q: Is this a real teacher's method?

A: No. The scenario is made up to show one possible approach. Adapt it freely.

Q: How many boxes should a lesson card have?

A: Four is a good starting point, but fewer or more can work if the card stays easy to read.

Q: Should students see the card?

A: Many teachers share the note-for-home box and keep the rest as private planning.

Q: Does a card suit group lessons?

A: It can, with a short list of shared goals and a space for each student's focus.
