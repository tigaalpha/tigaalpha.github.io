First-Species Counterpoint: Writing Note Against Note on Piano

Music theory · 2026-11-09

First-species counterpoint is the simplest way to write two melodies that sound good together: one note in the new line for every note in a given melody, called the cantus firmus. It is the opening exercise in a teaching tradition that has trained composers for centuries, and it works surprisingly well as a hands-on puzzle at the piano. You learn to hear intervals, control motion between voices and make each line feel like a real melody.

Where the species idea comes from

In 1725 the Austrian composer and theorist Johann Joseph Fux published Gradus ad Parnassum, a textbook written as a dialogue between a teacher and a student. Fux organised counterpoint into five "species", each adding a new rhythmic possibility. The first species is note against note. The second puts two notes against one, the third four against one, the fourth uses ties and suspensions, and the fifth mixes everything freely.

Many later composers studied from this book or from teachers who used its method, which is why the exercises still appear in theory courses today.

The building blocks: consonant intervals

In first species, every vertical pair of notes must be consonant. The consonances fall into two groups.

  • Perfect consonances: unison, perfect fifth and octave.
  • Imperfect consonances: major and minor thirds, major and minor sixths.
  • Dissonances to avoid in this species: seconds, sevenths, the tritone and, between the two voices, the perfect fourth.

The fourth surprises many learners. In a two-voice texture it sounds unstable above the bass, so Fux treats it as a dissonance here.

Why imperfect consonances matter

Thirds and sixths are the workhorses. They sound full and sweet, and you can use several in a row. Perfect intervals sound hollow and strong, which makes them ideal for beginnings and endings but too bare for long stretches.

Four kinds of motion

When two voices move from one pair of notes to the next, the relationship between them falls into one of four types.

  • Contrary motion: one voice goes up while the other goes down.
  • Oblique motion: one voice stays on the same note while the other moves.
  • Similar motion: both move in the same direction by different intervals.
  • Parallel motion: both travel together, keeping an identical interval.

Contrary motion is the friend of independence. It keeps the two lines from sounding like one melody thickened. Parallel thirds and sixths are fine in moderation, but parallel fifths and octaves are not allowed in this style, because the two voices briefly merge and lose their separate identities.

The core guidelines in plain words

Textbooks phrase these slightly differently, but the spirit is consistent.

  • Begin on a perfect consonance, usually an octave, fifth or unison.
  • End on a unison or octave, approached by step in contrary motion.
  • Move mostly by step; use leaps sparingly and after a big leap, turn back by step.
  • Aim for a single high point in your new line rather than touching the same top note again and again.
  • Prefer imperfect consonances in the middle of the exercise.
  • Avoid moving into a fifth or octave by similar motion, sometimes called hidden or direct fifths and octaves.

These rules are not laws of nature. They describe one historical style, and learning them gives you a reference point that makes other styles easier to understand.

Trying it at the piano

Writing on paper is traditional, but the keyboard lets you hear each choice immediately.

Step one: choose a short cantus firmus

Use eight to eleven whole notes in a major key, starting and ending on the tonic. For example, in C major: C, D, F, E, F, G, A, G, E, D, C.

Step two: write a line above

Play the cantus firmus with your left hand. With your right hand, choose one note above each cantus note. Say the interval name out loud before you play it: "octave, sixth, third, third, sixth..."

Step three: check the motion

Play both lines slowly and watch for parallel fifths and octaves. Mark contrary motion with an arrow on your page to see how much independence you have.

Step four: sing one line, play the other

This tests whether your new melody stands up on its own. If it feels aimless when sung, adjust its shape.

Why it still matters for modern players

Counterpoint thinking shows up far beyond old church music. A pop bass line moving against a vocal, a jazz pianist's inner voice sliding under a melody and a film cue with a countermelody all use the same principles. Learning first species trains your ear to hear two lines at once, which also helps when you voice chords on the piano.

FAQ

Q: Do I need to read figured bass first?

A: No. First species only needs interval recognition and basic staff reading.

Q: Can the counterpoint go below the cantus firmus?

A: Yes. Writing a line underneath is a standard exercise, and then the opening interval must be a unison or octave so the key stays clear.

Q: How many leaps are acceptable?

A: There is no fixed number. A useful habit is to keep most motion stepwise and treat each leap as a special event.

Q: Why are parallel thirds fine but parallel fifths not?

A: Thirds keep the voices distinct and sound full; fifths are so blended that two voices start to sound like one.

Q: What comes after first species?

A: Second species, with two notes against each cantus note, introduces passing tones and the first controlled dissonances.
