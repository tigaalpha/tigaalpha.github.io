Compound Time on Piano: Feeling 9/8 and 12/8 in Groups of Three

Music theory · 2026-11-11

Compound time on the piano is a metre in which the beat divides into three equal notes rather than two. In 9/8 the beat is usually a dotted quarter, and there are three such beats in a bar. In 12/8 there are four. The written page may show a stream of eighth notes, but the body should feel a slower dotted pulse with a gentle triplet inside each beat. Once that pulse is secure, the inner notes have a place to sit and the bar stops feeling like a puzzle of nines or twelves.

Hearing the dotted beat before the page

Tap a slow pulse with one hand and fill each pulse with three even taps of the other hand. That is the inner life of compound time. Then transfer the slow pulse to the metronome, if you use one, and play a single repeating pitch in groups of three. Only after the grouping is easy should a real melody be added.

A first drill can be very small:

  • Tap three dotted-quarter beats, speaking "one, two, three".
  • Fill each beat with three equal eighths, speaking "one-la-li, two-la-li, three-la-li" or another even set of syllables.
  • Play the same grouping on a single piano key.
  • Keep the loudest sound on the dotted beat, not on every eighth.

Feeling 9/8 as three dotted beats

A bar of 9/8 at a moderate tempo is usually three groups of three, not nine equal steps of the same weight. Accent, however slightly, the first of each group. In a left-hand accompaniment this often looks like a dotted pattern or a rocking figure that returns every dotted quarter. In a right-hand melody, long notes will often last a whole dotted beat or two, which is easier to hold if you are already counting those slower beats.

If a score of 9/8 feels rushed, slow the dotted pulse, not the individual eighths as if they were the beat. The inner notes should stay even inside a calmer main pulse.

Feeling 12/8 as four dotted beats

12/8 has the same inner three as 9/8, but the bar is a group of four dotted beats. That can feel close to 4/4 with triplets in every beat. The difference is often one of notation and habit: 12/8 makes the triple division visible as eighth notes, while a 4/4 marking with triplets writes the same grouping another way. At the keyboard, line up the four dotted beats first, then fill them.

A ballad-like left hand in 12/8 may use four wide gestures per bar. A walking inner line may use all twelve eighths. Both still belong to the same four-beat frame. Practise the left hand as four pulses, then add the inner notes without changing the size of those pulses.

When simple time and compound time sit side by side

Some pieces switch from 3/4 to 9/8, or from 4/4 to 12/8, so that a passing group of triplets can be written as ordinary eighths. The main pulse may stay the same speed, with only the inner division changing from two to three, or the opposite.

Before a written change, rehearse the join:

  • Play the last bar of simple time and mark its beat.
  • Enter the first compound bar with the same beat speed, now dividing it into three.
  • If the score instead wants a new, slower or faster pulse, write that new speed in your own words above the bar.
  • Repeat the join until the hands do not jump.

A joint like this is easier than it looks once you decide whether the beat stays or changes. Guessing at the moment of arrival is what causes a sudden rush.

A short reading routine for new pages

When a new piece is in 9/8 or 12/8, start by boxing the dotted beats in the first line with a pencil. Speak one line of rhythm away from the keys, then play that line on a single pitch, then add the written pitches. This routine is slower on day one and much faster by day three, because the grouping is no longer being invented mid-bar.

FAQ

Q: Is 9/8 just a fast 3/4?

A: Not usually. In 3/4 the beat normally divides into two; in 9/8 it normally divides into three. The written page and the inner grouping are different even if a tempo marking looks similar.

Q: How should I set a metronome in 12/8?

A: Set it to the dotted-quarter pulse when that is the beat you feel, so you hear four clicks in a bar, not twelve.

Q: What if the music is so slow that I feel every eighth?

A: You can subdivide for control, but keep a quiet dotted beat in mind so the bar still has a shape.

Q: Why do my compound-time bars get faster as they go?

A: The inner eighths may be pushing the pulse. Practise one bar as four or three calm dotted beats with a pause after it, then connect bars.

Q: Are 6/8 and 9/8 the same family?

A: Yes, both are compound metres. 6/8 usually has two dotted beats, 9/8 has three, and 12/8 has four.
