Example Scenario: An Online Piano Course Adds Weekend Group Sessions

Stories · 2026-11-11

This is an illustrative scenario, not a description of a real company. It imagines a small online piano course that sells self-paced video lessons and decides to add optional weekend group sessions over video call. The example shows how a digital music business might test a new format, what questions it would need to answer and how it could judge whether the idea is worth keeping.

The challenge in the example

In this imaginary course, learners enjoy the flexibility of watching lessons whenever they like. However, the team notices from support messages that many learners feel unsure whether they are progressing, and some stop logging in after a few weeks. Messages often include questions like whether a passage sounds right or how other learners handle a tricky rhythm.

The team suspects that isolation, rather than the lesson content, is part of the problem. Before changing anything, they read through several months of anonymised support messages and group the questions by theme. Questions about confidence and comparison with other learners appear far more often than questions about the videos themselves, which supports their hunch and gives them a clear problem statement to test.

Designing a small test

Instead of rebuilding the product, the team decides to run a six-week trial of weekend group sessions.

  • Sessions last forty-five minutes and are held at two different times to suit different regions.
  • Each session has a maximum of eight learners so everyone can play if they wish.
  • A teacher leads a short warm-up, answers questions and invites volunteers to play a passage.
  • Learners can join with cameras on or off and can choose whether to play.

The team writes down in advance what they will measure: attendance, how many learners return for a second session, and whether course activity rises among participants.

Making sessions feel safe

Playing for others can feel intimidating, especially for adults. The team sets clear ground rules: feedback is kind and specific, recordings are never made without consent, and nobody is pressured to perform.

A gentle structure

Each session opens with everyone playing a simple exercise with microphones muted, so learners can take part without being heard. Volunteers play individually later.

Clear privacy settings

Participant names are shown by first name only, and chat messages are not saved after the session.

Combining group time with individual practice

The weekend sessions are meant to complement, not replace, daily practice. In the scenario, the team suggests that learners continue using the course videos and an AI practice tool such as TIGA ALPHA during the week for feedback on notes and timing, then bring specific questions to the weekend group. This division of roles helps the teacher spend group time on musical questions that software cannot easily answer, such as phrasing or how a passage should feel.

What the team learns

At the end of the trial, the imaginary team reviews its notes. They find that learners who attended two or more sessions were more likely to continue the course, although they recognise that motivated learners might have been more likely to attend in the first place. They also notice practical issues: some learners struggle with audio settings, and one time slot attracts far fewer participants.

Adjustments for the next phase

The team decides to add a short audio setup guide, move the quieter session to a different time and add a monthly session focused on one popular piece.

Questions for similar businesses

Any online music course considering group sessions can borrow the same approach. Start with a clearly defined problem. Run a limited, time-bound test. Decide on measures before starting. Protect participants' privacy and comfort. Finally, interpret results carefully, remembering that correlation is not proof that one change caused another.

FAQ

Q: Is this based on a real online course?

A: No. It is a hypothetical example created to illustrate planning and evaluation.

Q: How large should an online group piano session be?

A: Small enough that everyone who wants to play can do so. Many teachers find six to ten participants manageable.

Q: Should group sessions cost extra?

A: That depends on the business model. Some include them in a subscription, while others offer them as an add-on.

Q: How can audio problems be reduced?

A: Share a short setup guide, ask learners to test before joining and suggest turning off automatic noise reduction for music.
