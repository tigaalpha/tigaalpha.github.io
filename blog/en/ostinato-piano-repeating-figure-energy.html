Ostinato on Piano: Building Energy From a Repeating Figure

Music theory · 2026-11-05

An ostinato is a musical figure that repeats stubbornly while other parts change. On piano it can live in the left hand as a bass loop, in the middle register as a rhythmic chord cell, or even as a right-hand hook under a freer melody. Minimal pieces, film cues and practice drills all use ostinatos because repetition frees attention for listening, layering and improvisation. Learning to build a clean ostinato is a practical arranging skill as well as a coordination exercise.

What makes a figure worth repeating

A good ostinato is short, clear and comfortable to play many times. Two to four bars is enough. If the figure is awkward, fatigue will break the groove. Prefer patterns that:

  • Fit the hand without constant leaps at first.
  • Have a clear downbeat so the loop can restart cleanly.
  • Leave sonic space for a melody above or beside them.

Write the figure down. Seeing the loop on paper helps you resist accidental variations when you mean to stay strict.

Left-hand ostinatos for practice

Start in C with a simple broken pattern: C–G–C–D in even eighths, looping without pause. Keep the wrist soft and the dynamic even. After one minute, add a right-hand long-note melody using only chord tones. Then allow the melody to use passing notes while the left hand stays locked.

Change one variable at a time

First lock rhythm. Then lock pitches. Then add dynamics such as a swell every four loops. Changing everything at once usually collapses the repeat.

Layering without mud

When both hands are busy, low-register clashes blur the pulse. Keep ostinatos higher than the deepest octave if the melody also needs the bass, or thin the left hand to single notes. Pedal lightly or clear on each loop restart so the figure stays articulate.

A middle-register ostinato of two alternating chords can support a right-hand melody an octave above. Count the loops aloud for the first dozen repeats so you feel hypermeter — groups of loops that form larger phrases.

Creative uses beyond drills

Ostinatos help songwriters hold a groove while testing melodies. They help teachers keep a class pulse while students take turns improvising. They help film-style sketching when you need tension that grows without new bass notes every bar. You can also break an ostinato on purpose after eight or sixteen repeats to create a sense of arrival; the stop is powerful because the ear expected another loop.

Try recording a two-minute take with a fixed ostinato and three different melodies above it. Compare which melody respects the loop and which fights it.

Common pitfalls

Accidental variation. If the ostinato changes without a plan, the foundation wobbles. Use a score or a bold mental picture of the figure.

Too many notes. Dense loops leave no room for melody. Thin first, decorate later.

Rigid dynamics. Exact repetition of loudness can feel mechanical. Keep pitches fixed while allowing small waves in volume.

Ignoring posture. Long loops expose tension. Check shoulders and breathing every minute.

FAQ

Q: Is an ostinato the same as a riff?

A: Closely related. “Riff” is common in popular styles; “ostinato” is the general term for a insistently repeated figure.

Q: How long should I repeat a figure?

A: Long enough to feel stable, short enough to stay musical. Sixteen to thirty-two bars is a useful study length.

Q: Can the ostinato move to another pitch level?

A: Yes. A sequenced ostinato rises or falls by step every few loops. Learn a fixed-pitch loop before sequencing.

Q: Should beginners learn ostinatos?

A: Yes. A short loop builds pulse and frees the other hand for simple improvisation.

When to break an ostinato on purpose

Plan a structural break every sixteen bars in a sketch: silence for one bar, or a held chord, or a sudden register jump. Practised breaks prevent endless loops that tire listeners. In film-style sketches, the break can coincide with a scene change you imagine, which gives the stop a narrative reason.

Students sometimes fear stopping because the loop feels safe. Safety is useful in early coordination, yet music needs punctuation. Alternate “strict loop days” with “loop-plus-break days” so both skills develop.

If you compose in a notebook, draw repeat signs and then draw a box where the break will happen. Visual planning reduces surprise tension in the hands when the moment arrives.
