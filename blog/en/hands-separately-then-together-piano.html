Hands Separately, Then Together: Combining Hands in a New Piece

Practice tips · 2026-11-11

When a new piece looks difficult, many learners try to play both hands from the start and quickly feel overwhelmed. Others practise hands separately for weeks and then find that combining them feels like learning the piece all over again. Both extremes waste time. A balanced approach uses separate-hand practice for specific purposes and combines the hands early, in small and manageable steps. Understanding when and how to separate the hands makes learning new music faster and more secure.

Why separate the hands at all

Each hand in a piano piece has its own job: melody, bass, inner voices, chords or running figures. When both hands play at once, attention is split, and problems in one hand often go unnoticed. Practising hands separately lets you:

  • settle fingering without distraction
  • hear the shape and articulation of each line clearly
  • solve technical problems, such as jumps or fast passages, in isolation
  • learn the harmony of the left hand, which many players neglect

Separate practice is especially useful in polyphonic music, where both hands carry independent melodies, and in passages where one hand has much harder material than the other.

When separate practice stops helping

Separate practice has limits. The coordination between the hands is a skill of its own, and it can only be built by playing together. If you practise hands separately for too long, you may become fluent in each hand while the combination remains awkward. A useful guideline is to begin combining as soon as each hand can play a short section correctly at a slow tempo, rather than waiting until each hand is polished.

Combining the hands in small steps

Choose a tiny section

Start with one bar, or even one beat, plus the first note of the next bar. Short sections allow full attention to both hands.

Slow the tempo

Play the section together at a speed where nothing goes wrong. This may be much slower than your separate-hand tempo, and that is normal.

Check the vertical alignment

Look at the score and notice which notes in each hand sound together. Mark tricky alignments with a small vertical line in pencil.

Join sections gradually

When two small sections are secure, link them. Continue joining until a whole phrase flows.

Using one hand as a guide

In many pieces, one hand is simpler or more regular, such as a steady bass or repeated chords. Treat that hand as the anchor. Play it alone a few times, then add the other hand while keeping the anchor steady. This method helps especially when the melody has free rhythms over a steady accompaniment.

Another approach is to play one hand and sing or tap the other. Tapping the right-hand rhythm on your knee while playing the left hand gives a feel for how the two fit together without the full difficulty of both sets of notes.

Returning to separate hands

Even after combining, return to separate practice when needed. If one hand stumbles in a passage, isolate it for a few repetitions, then put the hands together again. This back-and-forth process is often more effective than either approach alone. Many experienced pianists practise hands separately throughout the learning process, even for pieces they know well, to refresh fingering and clarity.

Listening to balance

When the hands come together, balance becomes important. Usually the melody should be louder than the accompaniment. Practise the accompaniment hand very softly while the melody hand plays at full tone, then adjust. Recording a few bars helps you hear whether the balance is right, since it is difficult to judge while playing.

A sample weekly plan

For a new piece of moderate difficulty, a learner might work like this. On the first two days, learn the first section hands separately, with fingering marked. On day three, combine the hands slowly in short sections. On days four and five, join the sections, returning to separate hands for any passage that breaks down. Later in the week, move on to the next section in the same way while keeping the first section fresh.

FAQ

Q: Should beginners always practise hands separately first?

A: It is often helpful, but they should combine the hands early in short sections rather than waiting too long.

Q: Why does combining the hands feel like starting over?

A: Coordinating two hands is a separate skill; slowing down and working in small sections makes the transition easier.

Q: Which hand should I learn first?

A: Many learners start with the harder hand or the melody, but learning both before combining is the usual approach.

Q: Is hands-separate practice useful for advanced players?

A: Yes. Advanced pianists use it to refine fingering, voicing and difficult passages throughout their preparation.
