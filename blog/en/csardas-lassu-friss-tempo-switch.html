Csárdás on Piano: Switching From Slow Lassú to Fast Friss

Songs · 2026-10-31

The csárdás is a Hungarian dance that typically begins with a slow, expressive section called lassú and then bursts into a fast, energetic section called friss. Playing one on piano is mostly about the switch: prepare the new tempo in your head before it arrives, keep the slow part genuinely slow, and build the fast part from small, relaxed movements rather than force.

A widely played concert csárdás outside Hungary is the one by the Italian composer Vittorio Monti, written in 1904 and now heard on violin, mandolin, accordion and piano in many arrangements. Because the piece is in the public domain, piano versions are easy to find. This guide does not reproduce any score. It focuses on the skill that makes or breaks every csárdás: changing gear without losing control.

Csárdás basics

  • The csárdás developed in Hungary in the nineteenth century as a social dance.
  • Its two main moods are lassú (slow) and friss (fresh, fast).
  • Many pieces alternate these moods more than once, sometimes with a calm interlude in a major key.
  • Rhythmic features include short, snappy figures and accents that drive the dance forward.
  • Monti's piece follows this pattern: a free, singing slow opening, then fast sections with a lyrical slower episode before a brilliant finish.

The slow part is harder than it looks

Many pianists treat the lassú as an introduction to get through before the fun begins. Listeners notice. The slow section sets up the contrast, and if it is rushed, the fast section has nothing to jump from.

Let it breathe

The lassú often sounds improvised, with lingering notes and small hesitations. Mark where the phrase naturally stretches and where it moves on.

Keep a hidden pulse

Even when the rhythm is flexible, there should be an underlying beat you can return to. Tap it lightly with your foot in practice.

Colour, not volume

Use a warm, singing tone in the melody and a soft, supportive accompaniment. Save your bigger dynamics for later.

Preparing the gear change

The moment the friss begins is where most performances wobble. The hands are still in slow mode and the new tempo arrives as a surprise.

Hear the new tempo before it starts

In the last bar of the slow section, silently count the first bar of the fast section in your head. Your body then knows the target speed.

Practise the join on its own

Take the final two bars of lassú and the first two bars of friss. Repeat only this join, many times, until the switch feels natural.

Use a metronome in two settings

Set one tempo for the slow section and another for the fast one. Practise jumping between them by changing the setting at the join.

Breathe at the switch

A small physical reset, such as a breath or a slight lift of the wrists, helps signal the new character to both you and the listener.

Building speed in the friss

Fast csárdás passages often contain repeated notes, quick scales and short accented chords.

  • Start at half tempo. Make every note even and clear before increasing speed.
  • Group notes in beats. Think of runs as groups that land on each beat, rather than as a long string of separate notes.
  • Use small movements. Keep fingers close to the keys and let the wrist stay loose. Big lifts slow you down at higher tempos.
  • Increase in small steps. Raise the metronome slightly each day, only when the current tempo feels comfortable.
  • Keep accents short. In a dance, accents should be crisp, not heavy. Lighten the notes between them.

The lyrical middle episode

Monti's piece and many similar works include a slower, gentler section in the middle. This is a second gear change, from fast back to slow. Practise it the same way: hear the new tempo before it arrives, isolate the join, and shape the melody with a softer tone. The return to friss afterwards should feel like a fresh burst of energy.

Practising with feedback

Tempo changes are hard to judge from inside. An online piano teaching application can show whether you actually arrive at the new speed or drift towards it over several bars. Piano teaching with AI is well suited to this kind of measurement. A structured piano course or a broader piano learning course can then help you add more character pieces to your repertoire.

Frequently asked questions

Q: Is Monti's Csárdás suitable for intermediate pianists?

A: Simplified arrangements suit intermediate players. Full concert versions require advanced technique, especially in the fast sections.

Q: How fast should the friss be?

A: Fast enough to sound lively, but never so fast that notes become unclear. Control matters more than speed.

Q: Why do I speed up gradually instead of switching immediately?

A: Usually because the new tempo was not prepared. Counting the first fast bar silently during the last slow bar helps.

Q: Can I learn piano online and still master tempo changes?

A: Yes. Recording yourself and using an online piano lessons app to check tempo are effective ways to practise at home.

Q: Does an AI online piano course offer pieces like this?

A: It depends on the course library. The tempo-switch method in this guide works with any piece that changes speed.
