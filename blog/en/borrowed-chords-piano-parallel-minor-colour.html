Borrowed Chords on Piano: Colour From the Parallel Minor

Music theory · 2026-11-05

Borrowed chords, sometimes called modal interchange in teaching materials, are chords taken from a parallel mode or parallel minor while the home key stays in place. On piano, the most common doorway is borrowing from the parallel minor while you are in a major key. That is why a song in C major might suddenly use A flat major or F minor and still feel as if C remains home. Learning a few reliable borrows gives your progressions more colour without forcing a full modulation.

Parallel major and minor

C major and C minor share the same tonic. Their diatonic chords differ because the third, sixth and seventh degrees change. When a C major progression inserts a chord that “belongs” more naturally to C minor, listeners hear a shadowed colour, then often a return to bright major harmony.

Useful early borrows in C major include:

  • A flat major (the flat VI).
  • F minor (the minor iv).
  • E flat major (the flat III).
  • B flat major (the flat VII), used carefully so it still points home.

Play C major, then A flat major, then F minor, then C major. Notice how the flat-side chords darken the mood while C still feels like the centre.

How to practise recognition

Build two columns on paper: diatonic chords of the major key, and candidate borrows from the parallel minor. Play each borrow between two tonic chords. Ask whether the tonic still feels like home. If the music feels like it moved to a new key for good, your voice motion or duration may be signalling a modulation rather than a brief borrow.

  • Keep borrows short at first — one or two bars.
  • Resolve into a clear tonic or dominant chord afterward.
  • Sing the tonic note quietly while you play so the ear keeps a reference.

Simple progressions to learn by heart

Try these loops slowly in C, then transpose:

  • C – A flat – F minor – C
  • C – F minor – G – C
  • C – E flat – F minor – G – C

Use root-position triads first. Later, smooth the right hand with inversions so common tones stay in place. The left hand can play roots in whole notes to keep the bass easy to hear.

Dynamics

Borrow moments often sound better a little softer or more sustained than the surrounding major chords. The colour is the event; volume does not have to shout.

Writing with restraint

One well-placed borrow can characterise a whole bridge. Stacking every flat-side chord in a row can confuse the key centre. Choose a single emotional job for the borrow — a sudden shadow before the final chorus, a reflective pre-chorus, or a film-like pause — and return to diatonic harmony so the ear remembers where home is.

Improvisers can treat the flat VI chord as a place to linger on long melody notes that belong to that triad, then walk stepwise back into major-key tones.

FAQ

Q: Are borrowed chords the same as secondary dominants?

A: No. Secondary dominants temporarily tonicise another chord. Borrowed chords import colour from a parallel mode without necessarily targeting a new tonic.

Q: Do I need advanced theory first?

A: You need solid major and minor triads and a clear sense of the home tonic. That is enough to begin.

Q: Can I borrow from parallel modes other than minor?

A: Yes, later. Parallel minor is the clearest starting point on piano.

Q: How do I avoid muddy sound?

A: Keep voicings in the middle register, limit dense left-hand doubles, and clear the sustain pedal between coloured chords and the return home.

Borrowed chords in song bridges

Many pop and film bridges darken for eight bars before a final chorus. Borrowed flat-side chords are a reliable way to create that shadow while keeping the same tonic. Map a bridge as: two bars diatonic, four bars borrowed colour, two bars dominant preparation, then chorus tonic. Play the map as block chords first, then arpeggiate.

Sing the bass roots while you play. If the bass story is clear, the borrow usually reads clearly too. If the bass leaps without direction, revise the root path before decorating the right hand.

Keep a bridge sketch folder with dates. Replaying old sketches shows which borrows you overuse so you can intentionally choose a different flat-side chord next time.
