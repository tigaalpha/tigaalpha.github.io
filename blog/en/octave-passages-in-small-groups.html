Practising Octave Passages in Small Groups on Piano

Practice tips · 2026-11-12

Long strings of octaves can tempt a pianist to push through in one gesture until the arm tightens and the outer notes thin. A more musical method is to divide the passage into small groups that match the beats or the shape of the line, settle a comfortable octave span, and practise each group as a landing, then join the groups. The aim is an even tone and a pulse that still belongs to the phrase, not a stiff march of locked wrists.

Set an octave shape you can repeat without gripping

On a single pair of notes, find a span that lets both tones speak without a clenched thumb or a stiff fifth finger. The wrist should feel able to bounce or to travel, according to the style of the passage. If the hand is small, a slightly broken octave in practice can help you hear both notes; the written togetherness can return when the shape is familiar. Write a fingering that you will keep, including any substitution at a turning point.

Practise three or four octaves as a group, then stop

Choose a group that ends on a beat or at a natural turn of the line. Play it slowly, listen for even outer notes, and release the hand at the end of the group before you begin again. If a landing is late or thin, loop just those two octaves. Do not extend the group until the short one is even. A click on the beats can show whether the group sits inside the bar or spills over.

Release the hand at the end of each group before you lengthen the line

  • Settle an octave span on one pair of notes and keep that fingering.
  • Choose groups that match beats or turns in the line.
  • Loop a group slowly, listening for even top and bottom notes.
  • Join two groups only when each landing is secure.
  • Raise the tempo after the joined groups still feel free, not locked.

Join groups along the phrase, not as a new technical race

When two groups join, keep the same release and the same tone at the seam. If the seam tightens, go back to the second group alone. Pedal, if the style allows it, should follow the harmony and the phrase, not smear a group that is already uneven. Once several groups connect, play a longer stretch at the same moderate tempo and listen for the line, especially if the octaves carry a melody on the top notes.

When the octaves turn, leap, or change dynamic

Turns and leaps need their own short group, with a clear landing target. Dynamics should be practised inside the group, not added later as a surprise at speed. If the left hand has octaves under a right-hand line, or the reverse, settle the octave groups first, then add the other part at a tempo that does not disturb the landings. The small-group plan stays in place when the music gets louder; volume is not a reason to abandon the joins.

FAQ

Q: How many octaves should a practice group contain?

A: Often three or four, or one beat's worth, depending on the passage. The group should be short enough to land and release clearly.

Q: Should I practise octaves staccato or held?

A: Follow the score for the later run. In early loops, a slightly shorter touch can reveal whether both notes of the octave speak, then you can return to the written length.

Q: What if the fifth finger tires quickly?

A: Keep the groups short, insert a release, and check that you are not pressing from a locked wrist. A tired hand is a sign to stop the sitting, not to push the passage longer.

Q: Can I start at the end of the octave passage?

A: Yes. Practising the last group first, then adding the group before it, often steadies the close of the phrase.

Q: Do I need a click for octave groups?

A: A click on the beat is helpful if the groups rush toward the bar line. Once the pulse is secure, you may use it more sparingly.
