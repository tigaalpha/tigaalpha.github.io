Humanizing MIDI: Timing and Velocity That Sound Played

Music theory · 2026-11-04

Humanizing MIDI means adjusting computer-made note data so it sounds as if a person played it. Perfectly even timing and identical loudness can make a part feel stiff. Producers add small, musical variations: slight timing shifts, a natural rise and fall in velocity, gentle accents on strong beats and longer or shorter note lengths. Random changes alone rarely sound convincing; the variations work when they follow the shape of the phrase. Pianists can use the same ideas in reverse, listening for where their own playing is too mechanical or too uneven.

What MIDI actually records

MIDI is not sound. It is a set of instructions: which key, when it starts, how fast it was pressed (velocity) and when it stops. A digital piano can send this data to a computer, and the computer can send it back to make sound. Because the data is just numbers, software can align every note exactly to a grid. This process is called quantising.

Why quantising is useful

It tidies up mistakes and makes it easy to edit parts. The cost is that every note lands at exactly the same micro-moment and often with similar strength.

Why perfect can sound wrong

People do not play like a grid. Even very precise players let some notes arrive a little early or late in ways that support the music. In a waltz, the second beat may come slightly early. In a ballad, the melody may lag behind the accompaniment. Loudness also changes across a phrase, rising towards a high point and relaxing at the end. When all of that is removed, the listener notices something missing, even if they cannot name it.

Timing adjustments

  • Partial quantise: move notes only part of the way to the grid, keeping some of the original feel.
  • Groove templates: apply a timing pattern taken from a real performance.
  • Swing settings: delay off-beat notes by a chosen amount.
  • Melody lead: place melody notes a fraction later or earlier than the chord to separate them.

Small changes go a long way. Large random shifts usually sound sloppy instead of human.

Velocity shaping

Velocity controls loudness and often tone. A humanized part might:

Follow the phrase. Raise velocities towards the peak of a melody and lower them as it ends.

Separate voices. Keep the melody slightly stronger than the inner notes of a chord.

Mark the meter. Give beat one a gentle extra weight, but avoid a heavy thump on every bar.

Vary repeated notes. Repeated notes at identical velocity sound like a machine. Small differences help them breathe.

Note length and pedal

Legato lines need notes that slightly overlap or connect through the sustain pedal. Staccato notes need short lengths that still vary a little. Editing note lengths and pedal data is often as important as editing timing.

What pianists can borrow

Looking at MIDI editing is a clear way to see your own playing. Record a short passage on a digital piano and open the data in free software. You may notice that one finger is always louder, that your left hand rushes or that the end of each phrase drops too suddenly. These are the same details producers adjust by hand.

  • Record a simple melody twice: once thinking only of correct notes, once shaping the phrase.
  • Compare the velocity graphs.
  • Listen to which version you prefer and why.

The aim is not to imitate a computer edit but to become aware of the choices you already make.

Balancing control and freedom

Humanizing is not the opposite of accuracy. A good performance is steady where the music needs stability and flexible where it needs breath. Whether you are editing data or playing live, ask what each phrase is trying to say, then let timing and loudness serve that idea.

FAQ

Q: Is humanizing the same as adding random timing?

A: Not quite. Random variation can help a little, but convincing results come from changes that follow the music's phrasing.

Q: What is velocity in MIDI?

A: It is a number that represents how fast a key was pressed, which usually controls loudness and tone.

Q: Should beginners quantise their recordings?

A: It is fine for editing, but listening to the unquantised take first tells you more about your playing.

Q: Can I learn expression by studying MIDI?

A: It can make your habits visible, which helps you decide what to change at the piano.
