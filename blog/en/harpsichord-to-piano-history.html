From Harpsichord to Piano: How Keyboards Learned Soft and Loud

Stories · 2026-10-30

The piano exists because keyboard players wanted something the harpsichord could not give them: control of loud and soft through touch alone. Around 1700 in Florence, the instrument maker Bartolomeo Cristofori built a keyboard whose hammers struck the strings instead of plucking them, and he called it a harpsichord "with soft and loud". That idea grew into the modern piano.

Knowing this history changes how you play older music. When you open a book of Bach, you are reading pieces written mostly for instruments that responded very differently to the fingers. Here is the story in brief, and the practical lessons it holds for today's learners.

Key points to remember

  • The harpsichord plucks strings; the piano strikes them with hammers.
  • Cristofori's escapement let the hammer fall away so notes could ring.
  • "Pianoforte" refers to the new ability to play soft and loud.
  • Older keyboard music relies heavily on articulation for expression.
  • On a modern piano, choose dynamics and pedal with the original sound in mind.

Before the piano: plucked and struck strings

In the harpsichord, pressing a key lifts a small wooden jack fitted with a plectrum, traditionally made of quill, which plucks the string. The pluck sounds the same whether you press gently or firmly. Players created contrast by switching between sets of strings or between two keyboards, which gave music a "terraced" effect: one level, then another.

The clavichord worked differently. A small metal blade called a tangent pressed against the string, so players could shape volume and even add a gentle vibrato. Its weakness was volume itself. A clavichord is so quiet that it suited small rooms and private study rather than concerts.

Musicians therefore faced a choice: power without touch control, or touch control without power.

Cristofori's invention

Cristofori's solution combined the strengths of both. His key mechanism threw a hammer toward the string and let it fall away immediately, so the string could keep vibrating. This "escapement" is still at the heart of every acoustic piano. Because the hammer's speed depended on the player's touch, the instrument could play softly or loudly, which is why it eventually took the name pianoforte, Italian for "soft-loud".

Three of Cristofori's pianos survive, dated 1720, 1722 and 1726. They are kept in museums in New York, Rome and Leipzig, and they look surprisingly like harpsichords from the outside.

A slow takeover

The new instrument did not replace the harpsichord overnight. Through much of the eighteenth century, the two existed side by side. Builders in Germany and elsewhere improved the mechanism, and by the time of Mozart and Beethoven, the fortepiano was central to keyboard music. Over the nineteenth century, pianos gained heavier strings, stronger frames and a much wider range, becoming the powerful instrument we know today.

What this means when you play older music

Articulation carries the shape

On a harpsichord, separation between notes was a main tool of expression. When playing Baroque pieces on piano, try slightly detached notes in faster passages and connected playing for lyrical lines. The music often becomes clearer and lighter.

Use dynamics, but with taste

You have a tool Bach rarely had at the harpsichord: graded dynamics. Many pianists use gentle changes to show phrase structure, while others prefer a more terraced approach, with clear levels per section. Listen to several recordings and decide what makes the counterpoint clearest.

Keep the pedal sparing

A modern sustain pedal can blur busy textures. Practise Baroque pieces without pedal first, and add only brief touches where they really help.

Let each voice speak

In polyphonic music, two or three melodies run at once. Practise each voice separately, then combine them while keeping each line audible.

Frequently asked questions

Q: Who invented the piano?

A: Bartolomeo Cristofori, an instrument maker working in Florence, built the first pianos around 1700.

Q: Did Bach write for the piano?

A: Most of Bach's keyboard music was written for harpsichord, clavichord or organ. Early pianos existed during his lifetime, but they were not yet widespread.

Q: Should I play Bach without dynamics?

A: Not necessarily. Many pianists use dynamics to clarify structure. The key is to keep the texture clear and the voices balanced.

Q: Can I study early keyboard music if I learn piano online?

A: Yes. An online piano lessons app or an AI online piano course can help with notes and rhythm, while recordings help you explore style.
