Secondary Dominants on Piano: Temporary Tonics in a Phrase

Music theory · 2026-11-05

A secondary dominant is a dominant chord that points at a temporary tonic inside a key. Instead of only V going to I, you can aim V of V at the dominant, or V of vi at the relative minor. On piano these chords add forward pull without a full modulation. Once you can spell and resolve a few secondary dominants, ordinary progressions gain clear directional colour.

The basic idea

In C major the home dominant is G7 resolving to C. The secondary dominant of G is D7, which wants to resolve to G. The secondary dominant of A minor is E7, which wants to resolve to A minor. You briefly treat the target chord as a local tonic, then continue in the home key.

Spell the secondary dominant as a major triad or dominant seventh whose root sits a fifth above the target. From the target, count up a fifth to find the secondary root, then add the major third and minor seventh of that new chord.

  • Say the target name first, then the secondary name, so the arrow stays clear.
  • Play target alone, then secondary into target, before embedding them in a longer phrase.
  • Keep left-hand bass on roots at first; fancy inversions come later.

Ear cue

Secondary dominants often raise a note that was lowered in the home key. In C major, D7 uses F sharp, a pitch outside the key signature. That raised note is a helpful alert.

Placing them in simple progressions

A classic path is I–V/V–V–I. In C: C, D7, G, C. Another is I–V/vi–vi–V–I: C, E7, A minor, G, C. Play each chord for two beats at a slow tempo and listen for the temporary pull.

Useful practice frames:

  • Four-bar loops that insert one secondary dominant per loop.
  • Melody on top that leans into the raised note of the secondary chord.
  • Left-hand roots with right-hand close-position sevenths.

Do not stack every possible secondary dominant into one phrase. One clear arrow per short progression is enough while learning.

Voice leading that keeps the resolution clean

Keep the third of the secondary dominant moving to the root or third of the target by step when possible. The seventh usually resolves down by step. On piano, hold common tones and move only the notes that must change. Quiet voice leading makes the tonicisation feel intentional rather than jumpy.

If a stretch appears, drop the fifth of the seventh chord. The third and seventh carry the function. Thin voicings also leave room for a melody above.

Linking study to repertoire

Many classical and popular phrases use secondary dominants before a cadence. When you meet a sudden raised note that resolves to a non-tonic chord, pause and label the arrow. Labelling turns a surprise accidental into a pattern you can reuse in improvisation and arrangement.

Teachers often introduce secondary dominants after students know diatonic triads and the home dominant seventh. That sequence helps because listeners already expects V–I, so V of something else stands out clearly.

One-week practice plan

  • Day 1–2: spell V/V and V/vi in C, G and F major, hands separately.
  • Day 3: play I–V/V–V–I in those keys with metronome.
  • Day 4: add V/ii and resolve to ii, then continue to V–I.
  • Day 5: harmonise a short melody using one secondary dominant.
  • Day 6–7: transpose your clearest progression into two new keys and record both.

Short loops beat long lectures. The skill is aiming and resolving, not naming every theory term at once.

Secondary dominants in minor keys

The same arrow idea works in minor. In A minor, V/V can appear as B7 resolving to E, which then resolves toward A minor. Spell carefully: minor keys already use accidentals, so mark the temporary raised notes in the score until they feel familiar.

Play a short loop: A minor, B7, E7, A minor. Listen for each arrow. Then insert the loop into a longer phrase that returns to a plain cadence so the home key remains clear.

Labelling arrows in repertoire

When you meet a sudden raised note that resolves to a non-tonic chord, write a small arrow above the bar: from secondary dominant to target. Two weeks of labelling trains your eye to spot tonicisations before your fingers guess. Erase labels only after you can explain the arrow without looking at the pencil mark.

FAQ

Q: Is a secondary dominant a modulation?

A: Not by itself. It creates a short tonicisation. A modulation usually confirms a new key with a cadence and continues there.

Q: Must I always use a seventh chord?

A: A major triad can work, but the minor seventh makes the dominant function clearer to most ears.

Q: Which secondary dominant should I learn first?

A: V/V is the most common starting point, then V/vi.

Q: Can this help improvisation?

A: Yes. Aiming a temporary dominant at the next chord gives fills a sense of direction.
