Chaconne and Ground Bass on Piano: Variations Over a Repeating Bass

Music theory · 2026-10-31

A chaconne is a set of continuous variations built over a short pattern that keeps repeating, usually a bass line or a chord progression in triple time. The repeating bass is called a ground bass, or ostinato. On the piano, the easiest way to understand it is to loop a four-bar bass line in the left hand and invent a new right-hand idea each time it comes round.

This article explains where the chaconne came from, how it differs from the passacaglia, which famous pieces use a ground bass, and how to write and play your own chaconne at the keyboard.

Key ideas in one list

  • Ground bass: a short bass pattern, often two to eight bars, repeated many times.
  • Variation: each repetition gets something new above it: a different rhythm, texture, register or mood.
  • Metre: Baroque chaconnes and passacaglias are usually in triple time.
  • Continuity: the variations flow into each other without stopping, so the form feels like one long journey.
  • Contrast inside unity: the bass gives stability; the upper parts give surprise.

From dance to grand variation form

The chacona began as a lively dance with sung refrains that became popular in Spain around the end of the sixteenth century, with roots often traced to the Spanish colonies in the Americas. As it travelled through Italy, France and Germany during the seventeenth century, it slowed down and grew into a serious instrumental form.

Composers loved it because the repeating bass gave them a frame for invention. The listener always knows where they are, so the composer can take bold risks above it.

Chaconne or passacaglia?

The two names are often used for very similar pieces, and composers did not always keep them apart. A common rule of thumb:

  • Chaconne: the repeating element is often a chord progression, so the bass itself may change slightly while the harmony stays the same.
  • Passacaglia: the repeating element is more often a clear melodic bass line that stays recognisable.

In practice, both use repetition plus variation, and both make excellent study material for pianists.

Famous examples to listen to

  • J. S. Bach, Chaconne in D minor: the final movement of the Partita No. 2 for solo violin, BWV 1004. Pianists know it through arrangements by Johannes Brahms, for the left hand alone, and by Ferruccio Busoni, for two hands.
  • Johann Pachelbel, Canon in D: three violins play a canon over a two-bar ground bass that repeats throughout. Its chord pattern later appeared in many pop songs.
  • J. S. Bach, Passacaglia and Fugue in C minor, BWV 582: an organ work built on an eight-bar bass theme.
  • George Frideric Handel, Passacaglia from the Suite in G minor, HWV 432: a keyboard piece whose short, repeating harmonic pattern is popular with intermediate pianists.

Listen to one of these and count how many times you can hear the pattern return. Then notice what changes each time.

How variations are built

Composers use a small toolkit again and again. Here are the most common moves:

  • Rhythmic speed-up: the first variation in long notes, the next in eighths, then sixteenths.
  • Register change: move the melody up an octave, or put the bass pattern in a higher voice.
  • Texture change: switch between single lines, chords and broken patterns.
  • Mode change: shift from minor to major for a section, then back.
  • Dynamics: build gradually toward a climax, or contrast loud and soft variations.

Once you know these tools, a long chaconne stops sounding like repetition and starts sounding like a story with chapters.

Build your own chaconne in four steps

Step 1: choose a ground

Use a four-bar descending bass in A minor: A, G, F, E, one note per bar in 3/4. This descending shape is sometimes called a lament bass.

Step 2: add plain chords

Over each bass note, play a simple chord in the right hand: A minor, G major (or E minor over G), F major, E major. Loop it until it feels natural.

Step 3: write four variations

Variation 1 in slow chords. Variation 2 with a simple melody in quarter notes. Variation 3 with broken chords in eighth notes. Variation 4 in a higher register, softer, with a single singing line.

Step 4: shape the whole

Play all four without stopping. Decide where the high point is and plan the dynamics to reach it.

This kind of composing exercise is a powerful way to learn music theory, because you hear every chord instead of just naming it.

Why ground bass is great for practice

  • Left-hand security: repeating a bass pattern many times makes it automatic.
  • Improvisation without fear: the loop never changes, so you can experiment freely.
  • Memory: recognising a repeating pattern is easier than memorising unrelated bars.
  • Musical planning: long variation sets teach you to shape a performance across many minutes.

Frequently asked questions

Q: What is a ground bass?

A: A short bass line repeated many times while other parts vary above it.

Q: Is Pachelbel's Canon a chaconne?

A: It is a canon over a ground bass, so it uses the same repeating-bass idea, even though it is not usually called a chaconne.

Q: Can beginners play a chaconne?

A: Yes, in simple form. A four-bar ground with basic chords and simple right-hand variations is suitable for early players.

Q: What is the difference between a chaconne and a passacaglia?

A: Both are variations over repetition. A common distinction is that a chaconne repeats a chord pattern while a passacaglia repeats a bass melody.

Q: How long should my own chaconne be?

A: Four to eight variations is a good start. Focus on clear contrast between each one.
