Ties and Slurs: Two Curved Lines With Different Jobs

Music theory · 2026-11-23

Two curved lines in a piano score look almost the same, yet they ask for different things. A tie links repeated pitches so they last longer, and a slur links varied pitches so they flow together.

How to spot the difference

Look at the notes at each end of the curve. If both notes are on the same line or the same space, the curve is a tie. If the notes are different, the curve is a slur.

This simple check solves most confusion. A tie always connects two neighbouring notes of the same pitch, while a slur can stretch over several notes or even a whole phrase.

What a tie asks you to do

With a tie, you play the first note and hold it for the combined length of both notes. You do not play the second note again. If a half note is tied to a quarter note, you hold the key for three beats.

Ties are useful because they let a note last across a bar line or across the beat. Without ties, composers would need awkward note values to write the same length.

What a slur asks you to do

A slur asks for a smooth, connected sound, often called legato. You play each note under the curve, but you join them gently so that there is no gap between them.

On the piano this means moving from one key to the next while the previous sound still fades naturally. The weight of the hand shifts softly, and the wrist stays relaxed.

Slurs and phrasing

A slur often marks a musical sentence. A common habit is to give the last note of a slur a little less weight, so the phrase seems to end like a gentle sigh. Try it with a short tune and listen to the shape.

Some learners find it helpful to sing the slurred notes on one breath. If the notes fit comfortably in a single breath, the phrase may feel natural at the keyboard too.

A short comparison

  • Tie: two notes with the same pitch, played as one longer sound.
  • Slur: two or more notes with different pitches, played smoothly.
  • Tie: you press the key once for the whole length.
  • Slur: you press a key for each note.

When both appear together

Sometimes a tie and a slur appear in the same bar. A tied note may sit inside a longer slur, so you hold the tied note for its full length and still keep the phrase connected.

When reading these bars, find the ties first and circle them with a pencil. Then look at the larger curve to see where the phrase begins and ends.

Reading ties across bar lines

A tie often crosses a bar line. When you see the curve leaving the end of one bar, look at the first note of the next bar. If it has the same pitch, hold the key down and do not strike it again.

At the start it can be tempting to play the second note anyway. Counting aloud helps, because you can say the beats of both notes while your finger stays quietly on the key.

Practising the two signs

Choose a short melody with one tie and one slur. Clap the rhythm and hold your hands together for the tie, then use a smooth rolling motion for the slur. After a few rounds, play it at the piano slowly, listening for a clean hold and a connected line.

A piano teaching app that highlights notes can make this easier to see, but a pencil and a calm pace work just as well. Children often enjoy colouring ties in one colour and slurs in another. Over time the two signs become familiar, and you will read them at a glance while your attention goes to the music itself.

FAQ

Q: Do I play the second note of a tie?

A: No. You press the first note and hold it through the length of both notes.

Q: Can a slur cover many notes?

A: Yes. A slur can cover a few notes or a whole phrase, as long as they are meant to sound smooth.

Q: Does a slur mean I should play louder?

A: No. It describes connection, not volume.

Q: How can I remember the difference?

A: A tie joins the same pitch, a slur joins different pitches.
