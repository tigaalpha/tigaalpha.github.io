Practising Hand Crossings So the Melody Still Sings

Practice tips · 2026-11-12

Hand crossings appear in many piano textures: a left hand that reaches over for a high bass note after a low one, or a right hand that dips under to take a mid-range tone. The danger is not only a missed landing, but a bump in the melody or a hole in the pulse while the arm travels. Practising the crossing as a musical event, not only as a gymnastic one, means planning which finger arrives, when the arm moves, and how the line on the far side should sound.

Mark the crossing and the landing finger in the score

Look at the two or three notes on each side of the crossing and write the landing finger clearly. Decide whether the moving arm goes over or under, and whether a held note in the still hand can cover the travel time. If a redistribution would avoid an awkward cross and still keep the voicing, consider it, but write the decision down and keep it. Changing the plan every sitting is itself a source of bumps.

The still hand should know what it is doing during the travel: holding, releasing, or preparing the next chord.

  • Mark the landing finger and the direction of the cross in the score.
  • Practise the two notes before and the two notes after as a short unit, slowly.
  • Keep a held tone, if the score allows one, to cover the travel.
  • Listen to the melody note on each side of the crossing at the same dynamic plan.
  • Only then play a wider span of bars that includes the crossing.

Practise the landing as a short unit before you play the phrase

Play the last note before the cross, the landing, and the next note, in a loop, at a tempo where the arm can arrive without a jab. The landing should feel like a planned place, not a guess. If the melody is in the moving hand, sing or hum that note so the ear has a target. If the melody is in the still hand, that hand must not tighten while the other arm travels.

Check that the line still sings after you join a longer phrase

Once the short unit is secure, play from earlier in the phrase through the crossing and a little beyond. Listen for a dip in tone or a wait before the landing. If either appears, return to the short unit rather than repeating the whole phrase at speed. Pedal can hide a hole in the line; use it only after the notes themselves connect, or after a planned silence if the score asks for one.

When the crossing also changes register or pedal

Some crossings arrive in a new register with a new pedal change. Practise the pedal motion in the short unit as well, so the foot and the arm are not improvising together later. If the new register needs a different standing position at the keyboard, include that shift in the unit. The aim is that the musical line, not the logistics, is what you listen for when the phrase is finally played through.

FAQ

Q: Should I look at the landing key as I travel?

A: A short glance can help while the unit is new. As the motion becomes familiar, try to stay with the score at the moment of the landing, at a slow tempo first.

Q: What if the still hand has a trill or a held chord during the cross?

A: Practise the still hand alone until that figure is calm, then add the travelling hand in the short unit so the still hand does not flinch.

Q: Is it better to avoid every crossing by redistributing notes?

A: Only when the new distribution still gives a clear voicing and a comfortable reach. A well-practised crossing can be clearer than a cramped alternative.

Q: How slow should the first loops be?

A: Slow enough that the landing is silent and on time. If you hear a bump, the loop is still too fast.

Q: The melody goes under after the crossing. How do I keep it singing?

A: Name the first melody note after the landing, give it a clear tone in the short unit, and only then lengthen the phrase.
