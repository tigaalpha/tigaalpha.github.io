The Intermezzo on Piano: A Character Piece Between Larger Works

Songs · 2026-11-12

An intermezzo, in piano music, is often a short character piece: a lyrical, thoughtful, or lightly moving work that can stand on its own, even if the name originally suggested something "in between" larger acts or movements. On a recital, an intermezzo can be a complete small world. In practice, it asks for a clear mood, a modest form, and a finish that does not overgrow the piece.

The name and the listening habit

Historically, an intermezzo could be an interval between larger parts of a stage work. In later piano writing, the title often simply names a short piece with a distinct character. When you listen, ask what that character is in a short phrase: song-like, walking, reserved, or lightly playful. Then notice whether the piece stays with that character or briefly contrasts and returns.

Write that character in one sentence at the top of the score. A vague hope of "being expressive" is harder to practise than a named mood.

Form that is small and complete

Many piano intermezzos use a simple sectional shape: a first idea, a middle with a different texture or key, and a return, sometimes shortened. Because the piece is short, a blurred return is very obvious. Learn the opening and the close early, so the middle has a place to come back to.

A middle section can be more mobile or more inward than the opening, but it should not sound like a new, larger piece. If you always start practice in the middle, because it is harder, also end a session with the opening and the return, so the whole arc remains in the ear.

Sound and pacing

An intermezzo often lives in a mid-dynamic world, with a singing line and a soft accompaniment. That does not forbid a sudden warmth or a brief outburst, if the page asks for one. It does suggest that a constant loudness will flatten the character. Pedal should keep the harmony clear; a haze that never lifts can make a short piece feel longer and less distinct than it is.

Tempo should let the line speak in complete phrases. A rushed intermezzo can sound like a leftover between two louder works, which is the opposite of giving the piece its own space.

Placing an intermezzo in a programme

If you play an intermezzo beside a larger work, leave a real silence before and after, so the small piece is not swallowed. In a group of short character pieces, vary mood and key where you can, and keep the intermezzo’s own close intact — do not rush the last bars in order to start the next title. The "in between" history of the name is not a reason to treat the music as filler.

A short working plan

A plan that includes the close

  • Write one sentence for the character of the piece.
  • Mark the opening, the middle and the return.
  • Practise opening and close as a pair, then the middle in proportion.
  • Keep the accompaniment quieter than the line, with clear pedal changes.
  • Play a complete run at a tempo that lets phrases finish.

FAQ

Q: Is an intermezzo always slow?

A: No. Many are lyrical and moderate. Some move more quickly. The title names a character piece, not a single tempo.

Q: Should I treat it as a short improvisation?

A: No. Follow the written form and detail. The character can feel intimate without becoming unplanned.

Q: What if the middle section feels like the "real" piece?

A: It may be the more intense part, but the opening and return still frame it. Practise the frame as carefully as the middle.

Q: How much pedal is typical?

A: Enough to join the harmony, not so much that the character turns into a blur. Change the pedal when the harmony changes.

Q: Can a very short intermezzo stand alone in a recital?

A: Yes, if it is played as a complete piece, with silence around it. It can also sit in a small group of character pieces.
