Passing and Neighbour Tones: Notes That Colour a Melody

Music theory · 2026-11-21

Passing tones and neighbour tones are short notes that decorate a melody without changing its basic harmony. A passing tone walks from one chord tone to another by step. A neighbour tone leaves a chord tone by step and comes straight back. Both are common in tunes you already know, and naming them helps you decide which notes to lean on and which notes should stay light.

Chord tones and the notes between them

In a simple phrase, some notes belong to the chord underneath, and some do not. The chord tones are the pillars. The notes in between are often passing or neighbour tones. You do not need a full harmonic analysis to start. Play the melody slowly, then play only the notes that fall on the stronger beats. The notes that disappear in this thinner version are often the decorations.

This listening test is gentle and practical. It shows you the skeleton of the line, which is the part that should stay clear when you add the extra steps again.

Passing tones: walking from one pillar to the next

A passing tone fills the gap between two chord tones that are a third apart. If the melody goes from C to E, a D in the middle can be a passing tone. It usually arrives by step and leaves by step, in the same direction.

  • The passing tone is often shorter than the notes on either side.
  • It is usually approached and left without a leap.
  • In a rising line it continues to rise; in a falling line it continues to fall.

When you practise, you can play the two pillars first, then add the walking note so it joins them smoothly. The decoration should not poke out in volume unless the score asks for a special accent.

Neighbour tones: a step away and a step home

A neighbour tone leaves a chord tone by a step up or a step down, then returns to the same chord tone. An upper neighbour steps above and comes back; a lower neighbour steps below and comes back. The effect is a small wave in the line, not a journey to a new place.

Because the main note is heard twice, it is easy to make the neighbour too heavy. Keep the main note clear and let the side step be lighter, as if the melody were nodding and then standing still again.

Hearing the difference in a familiar tune

Take a short melody you can already sing. Mark the notes that feel like destinations, then look at the notes that only pass through. You will often find passing tones in scales that climb or fall, and neighbour tones in ornaments or written-out turns. Saying “walk through” or “step aside” as you play can be enough of a label.

If two decorations sit close together, slow the bar and sing the skeleton first. The ear recognises the main path more easily when the extra notes are added second.

How this helps your playing

Once you know which notes are pillars, you can shape the phrase with more confidence. A slight lean on a chord tone, a lighter touch on a passing tone, and a soft neighbour that does not delay the return will often make a line sound more spoken. This is not a rigid rule, and a composer may mark an accent on a decoration. The point is to have a reason for what you do, rather than giving every note the same weight.

The same idea helps with fingering. A passing tone is often comfortable as a middle finger in a short scale; a neighbour tone may use a finger that can leave and return without a twist. Compare two fingerings slowly, then choose the option that keeps the decoration light.

Writing a tiny example at the piano

Choose a C major chord in the left hand and play E in the right hand. Add a passing D between C and E, then try an upper neighbour F that returns to E. Listen to how the harmony still feels like the same chord, while the melody becomes more mobile. Repeat the experiment with a different chord, always starting from the skeleton.

A few minutes of this kind of play will make the signs in printed music less mysterious, because you have already heard the same patterns under your own hands.

FAQ

Q: Is a passing tone always on a weak beat?

A: Often it is, but not always. The clearer test is whether it walks by step between two chord tones.

Q: How is a neighbour tone different from a passing tone?

A: A neighbour tone leaves a note and returns to it. A passing tone continues in the same direction to a new chord tone.

Q: Should decorations always be played more quietly?

A: They are often lighter, but follow any accent or dynamic mark on the page. The aim is a clear musical reason, not a fixed volume.

Q: Can these notes appear in the left hand as well?

A: Yes. Bass lines and inner parts use passing and neighbour tones too, and the same walking or stepping-aside idea applies.
