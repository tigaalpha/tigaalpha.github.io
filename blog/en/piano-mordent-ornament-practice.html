Mordents on Piano: A Short Turn Around the Written Note

Practice tips · 2026-11-11

A mordent on the piano is a brief ornament: the written note is sounded, a neighbour is touched, and the written note returns, all inside a small amount of time that still belongs to the main note. The neighbour is usually the note below (a lower mordent) or, in some styles and signs, the note above. The job of the ornament is to articulate the main pitch, not to start a new melody of its own.

Reading the sign and choosing the neighbour

Know which way the neighbour lies

Printed signs vary by edition and by period. Some marks ask for a lower neighbour, some for an upper one, and an accidental written over or under the sign tells you to raise or lower that neighbour. When the page is unclear, check the preface of the edition, or try both neighbours slowly and choose the one that matches the scale of the bar and the style of the piece.

A first slow method:

  • Play the main note alone, on time, with the intended finger.
  • Insert the neighbour as a soft, short extra, still inside the same beat.
  • Return to the main note and hold whatever time remains.
  • Only then raise the speed, keeping the return note calmer than the visit.

Fingering that leaves the line intact

The fingers that play the main note should still be the fingers that belong to the passage without the ornament. Add the neighbour with a neighbouring finger that can leave quickly. In a right-hand pair, a common pattern uses a slightly higher finger for an upper neighbour or a slightly lower one for a lower neighbour, then returns. Avoid a fingering that forces a twist just as the phrase is trying to sing.

If the mordent sits on a black key with a white-key neighbour, keep the hand close to the keys so the visit is a small drop, not a poke. If both keys are white, a tiny, even movement is enough.

Where the accent should go

The written note, especially its return, is usually the note that carries the line. The neighbour is lighter. If the neighbour is louder, the ornament sounds like a mistake or like a new melody starting on the wrong pitch. Practise with a deliberately soft neighbour, then allow a little more tone once the rhythm is even.

In a chain of mordents, keep the main notes equally placed. The ornaments should not push the beat forward. A metronome on the dotted or plain beat, not on every inner note, can help at a middle speed, then should be used more sparingly as the ornament becomes small.

Adding mordents to a short practice phrase

Practise the ornament in a short phrase

Take one bar that already has a clear on-beat note of moderate length. Add a single lower mordent to that note, using the method above. Play the bar with and without the ornament so you can hear what the extra notes are doing. Do not decorate every long note in the same phrase; one well-placed mordent is easier to judge.

Signs that are easy to mix up

A mordent is shorter than a full turn, and it is not the same as a trill, which repeats the neighbour many times. It is also not an acciaccatura, which usually bites before the beat. If two signs appear in one line, mark them in your own words — "short lower neighbour" or "short upper neighbour" — so rehearsal does not blur them together.

FAQ

Q: Does a mordent start on the neighbour or on the written note?

A: In the common short mordent practised here, the written note is sounded first, the neighbour is a visit, and the written note returns. Always check a period-specific rule if a preface gives one.

Q: How fast should it be?

A: Fast enough to feel like an articulation of the main note, slow enough to stay even. Speed comes after evenness, not before.

Q: What if the next note is the same as the neighbour?

A: Keep the ornament tidy and leave a little space so the following note can still speak. A slightly smaller neighbour helps.

Q: Can the neighbour be chromatic?

A: Yes, when an accidental in the sign or the key of the bar asks for it. Spell that neighbour clearly.

Q: Should both hands play a mordent together?

A: Only if the score says so. In most single-line cases the ornament belongs to one voice. Do not add a copy in the other hand for symmetry.
