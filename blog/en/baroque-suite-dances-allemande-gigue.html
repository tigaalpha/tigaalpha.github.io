The Baroque Suite: Allemande, Courante, Sarabande and Gigue

Music theory · 2026-11-03

A Baroque suite is a set of instrumental pieces based on dance types, usually all in the same key, that composers of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries wrote for keyboard, lute and ensembles. The core sequence often follows four dances: allemande, courante, sarabande and gigue, with optional dances such as the minuet, bourrée or gavotte added before the gigue. Johann Sebastian Bach's French Suites and English Suites for keyboard are well-known examples. For pianists, suites are a practical way to learn how rhythm and character create contrast.

The four core dances

Each dance has its own meter, tempo and character, which gives the suite variety while staying in one key.

  • Allemande: moderate, flowing, in four beats, often starting with a short upbeat and full of running notes.
  • Courante: lively, often in three, with shifting accents; the French type is more stately, the Italian corrente faster.
  • Sarabande: slow and dignified, in three, often with emphasis on the second beat.
  • Gigue: fast and energetic, usually in compound meter such as 6/8 or 12/8, often with imitation between the hands.

Optional dances

Composers often placed one or two lighter dances between the sarabande and gigue, such as a pair of minuets or bourrées, where the first returns after the second.

How the order creates a journey

The suite moves from a flowing opening to a lively dance, then to a slow, reflective centre and finally to an energetic close. Even without a story, the sequence feels like a complete experience, with changes of pace that keep listeners engaged. Some suites begin with a prelude, an introductory movement in a free style before the dances start.

Playing each dance on the piano

Allemande

Keep a steady, unhurried pulse and shape the running notes into phrases. Light fingers and clear articulation help the lines stay transparent.

Courante

Watch for hemiola, where the accents shift so that two bars of three feel like three groups of two, especially near cadences.

Sarabande

Play with a sustained, singing tone. Lean slightly on the second beat where the music suggests it, and give ornaments time to sound graceful.

Gigue

Feel two or four main beats per bar rather than counting every small note. Keep the energy bouncing and bring out entries when the hands imitate each other.

Ornaments and repeats

Baroque dances usually have two halves, each repeated. Performers traditionally add extra ornaments or small variations on the repeat to keep the music fresh. Start by playing the written notes cleanly, then add a trill or a passing note on the repeat once the piece is secure.

A study plan for a whole suite

  • Choose a short suite or select three contrasting dances.
  • Clap the rhythm of each dance before playing, saying "one-two-three" or "one-and-two-and".
  • Learn the slow dance first to develop tone, then the faster ones for clarity.
  • Play the dances in order at a gentle tempo to feel the overall journey.

Listening tips

Listen to recordings on harpsichord and on piano and compare them. On harpsichord, notes cannot be made louder by touch, so players use timing and ornaments for expression. On piano you can use dynamics too, but many pianists keep them subtle to respect the dance character. Notice how each performer makes the sarabande feel different from the gigue.

How suites influenced later music

The dance suite did not disappear after the Baroque era. Later composers wrote suites inspired by older forms, sometimes as tributes and sometimes with modern harmony. Ballet and film music also use suites, collecting the main dances or scenes into a concert version. Recognising the old dance types helps you hear these connections. A minuet in a classical symphony, for example, keeps the triple meter and elegant character of its Baroque ancestor, even though the setting has changed. Listening for these family resemblances makes concert programmes easier to follow and more enjoyable.

FAQ

Q: What are the four core dances of a Baroque suite?

A: Allemande, courante, sarabande and gigue.

Q: Are all dances in a suite in the same key?

A: Usually yes, which gives the set unity while the dances provide contrast.

Q: Which dance is the slowest?

A: The sarabande, a dignified dance in three beats.

Q: Where are optional dances placed?

A: Commonly between the sarabande and the gigue.
