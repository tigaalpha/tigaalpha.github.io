Son Clave on Piano: How to Feel 3-2 and 2-3 Rhythm

Music theory · 2026-10-30

Son clave is a two-bar rhythm pattern of five strokes that organizes much of Cuban son, salsa and many Latin styles. On piano, you feel it either as 3-2 (three strokes, then two) or 2-3 (two, then three), and you place chords and accents so they agree with that pattern instead of floating against it.

If Latin charts have ever felt slippery under your fingers, the clave is usually the missing reference point. This guide explains the pattern in plain counting, shows how to feel it away from the keyboard, and then moves it into simple left-hand and right-hand parts you can practice today.

Clave at a glance

  • Five strokes spread over two bars of 4/4.
  • The "three side" has strokes on beat 1, the "and" of beat 2, and beat 4.
  • The "two side" has strokes on beats 2 and 3.
  • 3-2 starts with the three side; 2-3 starts with the two side. The pattern itself does not change, only where the song begins it.
  • Arrangers usually decide the direction from the melody, so the clave works as a fixed grid the whole band respects.

Counting the pattern without a piano

Before touching any keys, count eighth notes out loud: "1 and 2 and 3 and 4 and". Now clap only on the clave strokes.

Bar one (three side): clap on "1", on the "and" after 2, and on "4".

Bar two (two side): clap on "2" and "3".

Repeat the two bars slowly until the clapping feels like a sentence rather than a calculation. A useful trick is to tap a steady quarter-note pulse with your foot while your hands clap the clave. The foot gives you the grid; the hands give you the shape. When you can talk over it, the pattern is starting to live in your body.

Next, try the same thing starting on bar two. That is 2-3 clave. Notice how the music suddenly feels as if it leans forward, because the short side comes first.

Why the three side feels "pushed"

The middle stroke of the three side lands on the "and" of beat 2, not on a beat. That off-beat stroke is what gives the rhythm its forward tilt. Musicians sometimes call the three side the "strong" or "tense" bar and the two side the "relaxed" or "answering" bar. You do not need the labels, but you do need to hear the contrast: one bar pushes, the next settles.

This also explains a common mistake. Players coming from pop music tend to straighten the off-beat stroke onto beat 2 or beat 3. The pattern then sounds square, and the groove disappears. Keep that middle stroke clearly between the beats.

Moving the clave to the keyboard

Step one: left hand on the clave

Choose two chords, for example C major and G7. Play the root of each chord with the left hand, but only on the clave strokes. Three notes in bar one, two notes in bar two. It will feel sparse, and that is the point.

Step two: right hand on the pulse

Keep the left hand on the clave and let the right hand play simple chord shapes on every quarter note. Now the two hands disagree on purpose, and the clave becomes the thing you are listening for.

Step three: swap the roles

Put the steady pulse in the left hand and the clave in the right hand as short chord stabs. This is closer to what a pianist does in a band, where the bass and percussion carry the grid and the piano answers.

Step four: a basic montuno idea

A montuno is a repeating piano figure in Cuban music. A simple version alternates the third and fifth of the chord in eighth notes, with the accents arranged so the strongest notes match the clave strokes. Start very slowly, and record yourself to check that the accents really line up.

Common problems and quick fixes

  • The pattern drifts after a few bars. Go back to clapping with the foot pulse, then return to the piano at a slower tempo.
  • You lose your place in 2-3. Say "two side" and "three side" quietly as you play until the order is automatic.
  • Accents land between clave strokes. Play only the accented notes first, then add the filler notes once the skeleton is solid.
  • Everything sounds stiff. Relax the wrist and let the off-beat stroke be slightly lighter than the downbeat. Groove comes from balance, not from force.

Frequently asked questions

Q: Is clave only for Latin music?

A: It started in Afro-Cuban traditions, but the same rhythmic idea appears in many modern styles. Learning it improves your sense of syncopation in general.

Q: How do I know if a song is in 3-2 or 2-3?

A: Listen to where the melody and the main accents fall. Clapping both versions along with the recording usually makes the answer clear within a few bars.

Q: Can beginners learn clave on piano?

A: Yes. Start with clapping, then single notes in the left hand. You only need basic chords to play piano parts that respect the clave.

Q: Can I learn this kind of rhythm if I learn piano online?

A: Yes. Rhythm is well suited to self-study with recordings and an online piano lessons app, because you can slow the tempo and repeat a bar as many times as you need.

Q: Does an AI online piano course teach Latin rhythms?

A: Courses differ, so check the lesson list. Whatever you use, the method above works alongside any course as a daily rhythm drill.
