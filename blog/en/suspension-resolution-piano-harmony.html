Suspensions on Piano: Holding a Note Into the Next Chord

Music theory · 2026-11-11

A suspension on the piano is a prepared dissonance: one note that was consonant in the first chord is held (or repeated) while the other voices move, so that same pitch now rubs against the new harmony, and only then steps to a chord tone. The classic shape has three stages — preparation, suspension, resolution — and once those stages are clear under the hands, the ornament stops sounding like a late change of chord and starts sounding like a planned lean.

The three stages under the fingers

The preparation is a chord tone, usually on a weaker part of the bar. The suspension itself often arrives on a stronger beat, which is why the clash feels purposeful. The resolution almost always falls by step, most often a tone or a semitone downward, into a note that belongs to the new chord.

A simple 4–3 in C major

Play a C major triad in the right hand: C, E, G. Move to a G major chord, but keep the upper C sounding through the change. That C is now a fourth above the bass G, so the ear hears a 4. Then walk the C down to B, the third of G major. That walk is the 3. The left hand can sit on G for both chords so the upper voice does all the expressive work.

Common interval labels in figured or analytical writing include 4–3, 9–8 and 7–6. The first number is the dissonant interval above the bass during the clash; the second is the interval after the step down. The numbers are a map, not a fingering.

Finding suspensions in a score

Look for a tied note, or a repeated pitch, that crosses a change of chord and then steps downward. The tie is a strong visual clue, but some styles re-strike the suspended note instead of tying it. In both cases the pitch itself does not change until the resolution.

A useful pencilled check:

  • Circle the note that stays put while the bass or inner voices move.
  • Write the two interval numbers above the staff if the bass is clear.
  • Mark the resolution with a small arrow down by step.
  • Confirm that the preparation was a real chord tone, not a random passing pitch.

If the held note rises to resolve, many textbooks call it a retardation rather than a suspension. The playing approach is similar, but the label helps when a theory exercise asks for one and not the other.

Voicing so the clash can be heard

A suspension is easy to bury. If the dissonant note is in a weak inner voice and the left hand is thick, the lean disappears. Bring the suspended pitch slightly to the front of the texture, keep the bass firm and simple, and thin any inner doubling that fights the same register.

On a piano the decay works in your favour: the preparation can be a little fuller, then the held note rings into the new chord without a fresh accent, and the resolution can speak clearly. If the line feels swallowed, replay the three stages with only the bass and the moving voice sounding, then add the inner parts back one at a time.

Chain of 4–3 motion

In some Baroque and Classical passages, a sequence of 4–3 suspensions walks down a scale. Each resolution becomes the preparation for the next clash. Practise that chain slowly, with a light thumb on the repeated resolutions so the line stays even. Writing a suspension into a short cadence

Start with a plain cadence: subdominant, dominant, tonic. On the dominant, delay the third by keeping the fourth of the key in the upper voice for a beat, then let it fall. In C major that means holding F over a G-bass chord and dropping to E, or, in a 4–3 above G, holding C and dropping to B as in the earlier example. Play both and listen for the difference in colour.

Keep the rest of the cadence honest. Do not add extra passing tones on the same beat as the resolution, or the ear will not know which note is doing the work. One clean suspension is easier to hear than three ornaments stacked on the same chord.

FAQ

Q: Is a tied note always a suspension?

A: No. A tie can simply hold a chord tone that remains consonant. A suspension needs the held pitch to become a dissonance against a new chord, then resolve by step.

Q: Can a suspension resolve upward?

A: The usual resolution is downward. An upward step from a prepared dissonance is often labelled a retardation, which is a close cousin rather than the same term.

Q: Do I have to use the interval numbers?

A: They help when analysing or writing harmony exercises, but at the keyboard it is enough to feel preparation, clash and step.

Q: Why does my suspension sound messy?

A: The dissonant note may be too quiet, or other voices may be moving at the same moment as the resolution. Isolate bass plus moving voice, then rebuild the chord.

Q: Are suspensions only for old styles of music?

A: The pattern is common in earlier repertoire, yet popular and film textures still use a held note that leans into the next chord. The same three stages apply.
