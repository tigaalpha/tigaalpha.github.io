Preparing the Next Hand Position One Beat Early

Practice tips · 2026-11-12

A frequent cause of a missed chord or a late leap is not the note itself, but a hand that is still in the previous position when the new one is due. Preparing the next position one beat early means using a rest, a held note, or a quiet moment in one hand to travel, so the fingers are already over the new keys when the pulse arrives. This is a timing skill as much as a stretch. It can be marked in the score and practised as a small unit.

Find the travel moment in the bar

Look one beat ahead of each shift and ask when the moving hand is free enough to leave. A rest, a long note that can be released a little early if the style allows, or a moment when the other hand carries the sound, can all be travel time. Mark that beat in the score with a small arrow or the word "go". If no beat is free, you may need to redistribute a note, or accept a very compact motion that still starts earlier than the sounding note.

Practise the leave, the travel, and the silent landing

Play the last event in the old position, move on the marked beat, and arrive on the new keys without sounding them, then play the new chord or note on time. Loop this slowly. The landing should feel quiet and early, not a last-moment lunge. If the travel needs a change of torso or bench balance, include that in the loop. The click can sit on the beats so you can hear whether the arrival is early enough.

  • Mark the beat when the hand should leave the old position.
  • Loop the leave, the silent landing, and the new note at a slow pulse.
  • Use a rest or a held sound in the other hand to cover the travel when the score allows it.
  • Check that the new chord is already in shape before it sounds.
  • Lengthen the phrase only after the landing is early and quiet.

Put the early preparation back into the phrase

Once the unit is secure, play from a bar earlier so the preparation grows out of real music. Listen for a hole in the sound if you left too soon, or a late bump if you left too late. Adjust the written mark if needed, then keep it. Pedal can cover a brief travel, but it should not become a way to hide a late hand. The musical aim is that the new harmony or the new melody note arrives with the pulse.

When both hands shift at once

If both hands must change position in the same beat, give each hand a slightly different travel plan if you can: one moves on the second half of the previous beat, the other on a later rest. If they must leave together, practise the double travel as its own loop, with both silent landings before the new notes. Slow practice here is not a delay of learning; it is the only way to make two landings meet the same pulse.

FAQ

Q: How early is too early to leave a key?

A: Early enough to land in time, not so early that a written length is cut in a way the style cannot accept. Test by ear whether the previous tone still has its place in the phrase.

Q: What if the score has no rest to travel on?

A: Look for a finger that can stay, a redistribution, or a smaller motion that starts sooner. Some shifts have very little time; those need an even more precise loop.

Q: Should I look at the new keys as I travel?

A: A glance can help in the first loops. Later, try to stay with the page and let the marked beat, not a last-moment look, trigger the motion.

Q: Does this apply to leaps in only one finger?

A: Yes. A single-note leap still needs a leave time, a travel, and a landing that is ready before the note is due.

Q: Can I prepare more than one beat early?

A: If a long held note or a rest allows it, and the previous sound does not suffer, a little more time can be helpful. Mark whatever plan you will actually use.
