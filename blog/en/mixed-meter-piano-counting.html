Mixed Meter on Piano: How to Count 5/4, 7/8 and Changing Bars

Practice tips · 2026-10-30

Mixed meter means the number of beats changes from bar to bar, or a bar groups its beats unevenly, as in 5/4 or 7/8. The reliable way to play it on piano is to stop counting long numbers and count small groups of twos and threes instead. Once each bar becomes "2 + 3" or "3 + 2 + 2", the rhythm turns from a puzzle into a pattern your hands can feel.

Odd and changing meters appear in folk dances, film scores, jazz and progressive rock. They look intimidating on the page, yet the counting method is simple. This guide gives you a practice sequence that works for any time signature that refuses to stay still.

Quick facts on changing meters

  • A time signature tells you how many beats are in a bar and which note value gets the beat.
  • Irregular meters such as 5/4 and 7/8 are usually felt as combinations of twos and threes.
  • In the Bavarian zwiefacher, a folk dance, the music alternates between three-beat and two-beat bars, and dancers switch steps to match.
  • "Take Five", recorded by the Dave Brubeck Quartet in 1959, is a famous example of 5/4 in jazz.
  • Gustav Holst's "Mars" from The Planets is a well-known orchestral piece in 5/4.

Think in groups, not numbers

Counting "1 2 3 4 5 6 7" in a fast 7/8 bar is almost impossible to sustain. Musicians instead use group syllables. A three-group can be "1-2-3" and a two-group "1-2", or you can use words with matching syllables, such as "straw-ber-ry" for three and "ap-ple" for two.

So a 7/8 bar grouped 2 + 2 + 3 becomes "ap-ple ap-ple straw-ber-ry". The same seven pulses grouped 3 + 2 + 2 become "straw-ber-ry ap-ple ap-ple", which feels completely different. Composers often show the grouping with beams in the score, so look at how the eighth notes are joined before you start.

A four-stage practice sequence

Stage one: speak it

Say the group syllables out loud while tapping the first pulse of each group. Do this for the whole passage before touching the piano.

Stage two: clap and step

Clap every pulse and step on each group's first note. Your feet now feel the long and short groups, which is exactly what dancers do in a zwiefacher.

Stage three: left hand only

Play a single bass note on the first pulse of every group. In 5/4 grouped 3 + 2, that means two bass notes per bar, one long and one short.

Stage four: hands together, slowly

Add the right hand at half speed. Keep saying the syllables quietly in your head. Increase the tempo only when three run-throughs in a row feel easy.

When the meter changes every bar

Some music switches time signature constantly, for example 3/4, 2/4, 3/4, 3/4, 2/4. The trick is to keep the smallest pulse constant. If the quarter note stays the same speed, a 2/4 bar is simply one beat shorter than a 3/4 bar. Before playing, write the beat count above each bar in pencil, then read the sequence aloud as a list: "three, two, three, three, two".

Folk musicians who play zwiefacher tunes often learn the sequence as a kind of melody of bar lengths. You can do the same: hum the tune while clapping only on the first beat of each bar, and the changing meter becomes part of the song's shape.

Mistakes that slow learners down

  • Rushing short groups. A two-group should not feel hurried. Each pulse keeps the same length.
  • Adding an extra beat. In 5/4, many players unconsciously turn the bar into 6/4. Recording yourself makes this easy to spot.
  • Ignoring beaming. The way notes are joined in the score is often the composer's clue to the grouping.
  • Practising too fast. Odd meters reward slow, accurate repetition far more than speed.

Frequently asked questions

Q: Is 6/8 a mixed meter?

A: No. 6/8 is a regular compound meter felt as two groups of three. Mixed meter refers to uneven groupings or changing time signatures.

Q: How do I know the grouping of a 7/8 bar?

A: Look at the beaming and the bass line. If neither is clear, listen to a recording and clap along until you hear where the strong pulses fall.

Q: Are odd meters too advanced for beginners?

A: Simple 5/4 pieces are accessible to early intermediate players. The counting method works at any level.

Q: Can I practise this when I learn piano online?

A: Yes. An online piano lessons app or an AI online piano course lets you loop and slow down difficult bars, which suits odd-meter practice well.
