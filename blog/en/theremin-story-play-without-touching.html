The Theremin Story: The Instrument You Play Without Touching

Stories · 2026-10-31

The theremin is an electronic instrument that you play without touching it. It was invented around 1920 by the Russian physicist Leon Theremin, and it makes sound from two antennas: moving one hand near the upright rod changes the pitch, and moving the other hand near the loop changes the volume. Its eerie, sliding voice became famous in film music, and its story says a lot about what pianists take for granted when they press a key.

This is the story of a strange invention, the musician who turned it into a concert instrument, and the lessons it holds for anyone learning a keyboard today.

An accident in a laboratory

Leon Theremin, born Lev Termen, was working on devices that could sense nearby objects through changes in electrical fields. While experimenting, he noticed that the position of his hand altered the tone produced by his equipment. Instead of treating it as a fault, he treated it as music.

The instrument he built used two high-frequency oscillators. One was fixed; the other changed as the player's hand moved closer to the antenna. The difference between the two frequencies fell into the range humans can hear, producing a single, smooth tone. Because there were no keys or frets, every pitch in between was possible.

Theremin toured Europe and the United States in the 1920s, giving demonstrations that astonished audiences. He received a United States patent in 1928, and the RCA company began producing theremins for sale in 1929.

The musician who made it sing

A new instrument needs players who take it seriously. The most celebrated was Clara Rockmore, a trained violinist who turned to the theremin and developed a precise technique for it. She worked with Theremin to improve the instrument's range and response, and her recordings of classical pieces showed that it could be expressive, not just spooky.

Her approach is fascinating for pianists. Because the theremin has no fixed positions, she developed a system of small finger and hand positions to land on each note accurately. In effect, she built an invisible keyboard in the air.

From concert halls to film soundtracks

The theremin's gliding, ghostly sound made it a favourite of film composers in the 1940s and 1950s:

  • Spellbound (1945): Miklós Rózsa's score used the theremin to create an uneasy, dreamlike mood.
  • The Day the Earth Stood Still (1951): Bernard Herrmann's music made the theremin a symbol of science fiction.
  • The 1950s and beyond: a young engineer named Robert Moog built and sold theremin kits before going on to develop his famous synthesizers.

So when you hear an analogue synth today, part of its family tree leads back to a hand waving near an antenna.

What the theremin teaches pianists

The piano and the theremin sit at opposite ends of a spectrum. On the piano, the pitch is fixed by the instrument; you choose which key, when and how hard. On the theremin, the player creates every pitch, and nothing is fixed.

That contrast highlights several skills keyboard players can develop:

  • Hearing pitch before playing it. Thereminists must imagine a note before their hand moves. Pianists benefit from the same inner hearing, especially when sight-reading or improvising.
  • Knowing where your hands are. Rockmore's invisible keyboard shows how much accuracy comes from body awareness. On the piano, learning to find keys by feel frees your eyes for the score.
  • Shaping a line, not just hitting notes. A theremin melody is one continuous gesture. Pianists can aim for the same sense of connection by listening to how each note fades into the next.
  • Controlling volume separately. One theremin hand controls loudness alone. Try practising a melody where you think only about dynamics, with the notes already secure.

A short experiment at the keyboard

Sing, then play

Choose a simple melody. Sing the first phrase, then play it. Did the piano notes match the pitch in your head? This is the thereminist's core skill.

Eyes-closed landmarks

Close your eyes, find middle C by touch, and then find the G above it. Repeat with other intervals. You are building your own invisible map.

One hand for volume

Play a five-note melody with your right hand and let your free left hand "conduct" the dynamics in the air. Let your playing follow the conducting hand.

These activities take only a few minutes and pair well with any piano course or daily practice routine.

Why an old invention still feels modern

A century later, the theremin remains a reminder that music technology works best when someone treats it as a real instrument. Rockmore did not see a gadget; she saw a voice that needed technique and listening. The same is true of modern learning tools. An AI piano teaching app can measure timing, accuracy and dynamics, but the music still depends on a player who listens and chooses.

Frequently asked questions

Q: Who invented the theremin?

A: Leon Theremin, a Russian physicist, around 1920. He later demonstrated it in Europe and the United States.

Q: How do you play a theremin?

A: One hand moves near a vertical antenna to change pitch, and the other moves near a loop antenna to change volume. The player never touches it.

Q: Who was Clara Rockmore?

A: A violinist who became the most famous theremin performer and helped refine the instrument with its inventor.

Q: Is the theremin related to the synthesizer?

A: Yes, in part. Robert Moog built theremins before developing his synthesizers, and both are electronic instruments.

Q: Can theremin ideas help me learn piano?

A: Yes. Inner hearing, hand awareness and shaping a line are useful skills for any pianist.
