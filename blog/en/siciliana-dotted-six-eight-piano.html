The Siciliana on Piano: How to Play a Gentle Dotted 6/8 Sway

Music theory · 2026-10-31

A siciliana is a slow, rocking piece in 6/8 or 12/8 built on a dotted figure: a long-short-long pattern that sounds like a gentle sway. To play one well on piano, count in two big beats per bar, keep the dotted note slightly lingering and the short note light, and let the left hand hold the music steady underneath.

The name points to Sicily, and the style is often linked with pastoral images of shepherds and calm countryside. Composers of the Baroque era, including Bach and Handel, wrote many movements in this character, and the same lilting rhythm still appears in later music and in teaching pieces. This guide explains the rhythm itself, then shows how to make it sound graceful rather than mechanical.

Siciliana essentials

  • Usually written in 6/8 or 12/8, felt in two or four large beats per bar.
  • The signature figure is a dotted eighth, a sixteenth and an eighth: long, short, long.
  • Tempo is moderate to slow, more like walking calmly than dancing.
  • Minor keys are common, which adds a gentle, wistful colour.
  • The texture is often simple: a singing melody over a steady bass or a held drone.

Counting compound time without getting lost

In 6/8, each bar has six eighth notes, grouped into two groups of three. Count it as "ONE two three FOUR five six", with stress on the first and fourth notes. Those are your two big beats.

The siciliana figure fills one big beat. The dotted eighth takes the space of one and a half eighth notes, the sixteenth takes half, and the last eighth takes one. A helpful way to say it aloud is "lo-ong, short, step". Tap your foot only on the big beats while you clap the figure. You will feel the dotted note stretch over the foot tap and the short note slip in just before the final eighth.

A common trap

Many players turn the figure into a triplet, three equal notes, or into a quick jerky dotted rhythm borrowed from marches. Neither sounds right. The siciliana dotted note is relaxed, and the short note is soft and close to the following note.

Shaping the sway

Think of a swing in a garden. The motion goes out, pauses for a tiny moment and comes back. In the music, that pause sits on the dotted note.

  • Give the dotted note a little more weight than the short note that follows.
  • Connect the short note smoothly into the next note, as if it leans on it.
  • Lighten the final eighth of each group so the next big beat can begin fresh.
  • Avoid accenting every group the same way. Shape longer phrases of two or four bars with a gentle rise and fall.

The left hand: drone, bass line or broken chords

Siciliana accompaniments come in a few common shapes. Practising each one gives you a toolkit for many pieces.

Held drone

A low fifth, such as A and E, held for a full bar or longer. It creates the pastoral, bagpipe-like colour and lets the melody float. Keep it quiet, and refresh it only when the harmony changes.

Walking bass in big beats

Single bass notes on each big beat, moving by step. This gives forward motion without fuss. Play them evenly and slightly detached.

Broken chords in eighths

Six even eighth notes per bar, outlining the harmony. Here the challenge is to keep the left hand smooth while the right hand plays the dotted figure on top. Practise hands separately until both feel automatic.

A short practice routine

  • Clap and count: two minutes clapping the figure while tapping big beats with your foot.
  • One-note melody: play the rhythm on a single key in the right hand, focusing on the long-short-step feel.
  • Simple tune: make up a four-bar tune in A minor using the figure on each big beat.
  • Add the drone: hold A and E in the left hand under your tune.
  • Change the bass: replace the drone with a stepwise bass on each big beat.
  • Listen back: record yourself and ask whether it sways or marches.

This routine takes about fifteen minutes and builds the rhythm from the body outward, which is often more reliable than reading alone.

Why this rhythm is worth learning

Compound time appears in lullabies, folk songs, film music and many classical pieces. Once your hands understand the siciliana, other 6/8 patterns become easier to read and play. The figure also trains control: the ability to place a short, soft note exactly where it belongs, which helps in every style.

Frequently asked questions

Q: Is a siciliana always in a minor key?

A: Not always, but minor keys are very common and suit the gentle, wistful character.

Q: How is a siciliana different from a gigue?

A: Both often use compound time, but a gigue is usually fast and lively, while a siciliana is slow and swaying.

Q: My dotted rhythm sounds stiff. What should I change?

A: Relax the short note and connect it into the next note. Counting "lo-ong, short, step" aloud usually softens the shape.

Q: Can I learn this if I learn piano online at home?

A: Yes. The rhythm can be clapped, counted and recorded anywhere, and an online piano lessons app helps you check timing.

Q: Is this suitable for a beginner piano learning course?

A: The rhythm works well for late beginners. Short siciliana-style pieces appear in many method books and an AI online piano course can also introduce compound time gradually.
