Breath Marks in Piano Music: A Tiny Pause in a Phrase

Practice tips · 2026-11-21

A breath mark in piano music is a small comma, tick or v-shaped sign that asks for a tiny lift in the line, as if a singer were taking a short breath. It is not a long rest, and it is not a full stop unless the phrase already wants to end there. The mark tells you to finish one thought cleanly and then begin the next, with a brief space that the listener can feel.

What the sign is doing on the page

The mark usually sits above the staff, between two notes or just after a phrase. Editions differ in the exact shape, but the idea is the same: do not join those two notes as if they were one unbroken group. If you are unsure whether a small sign is a breath or a different ornament, look at the surrounding phrasing. A breath sits at a seam in the music, not in the middle of a tight ornamental figure.

When you first meet the sign, say “lift” as you arrive, so the eye and the hand share a plan.

How a pianist can take a breath

You cannot inhale with the keys, but you can create the same shape with timing and touch. Let the note before the mark finish, then lift the hand slightly instead of tying it into the next note. Keep the pulse in mind, so the lift is a short comma, not a lost beat, and begin the next note with a clear, calm attack, as if a new sentence were starting.

If the left hand is holding an accompaniment, it may continue through the lift while the melody breathes. In other passages both hands lift together. Listen to the texture and choose the option that makes the phrase clear.

Breath, slur and rest: three different seams

A slur asks you to join notes. A written rest asks for measured silence. A breath mark sits between those ideas. It is shorter and more flexible than a rest, and it often happens where a singer would change air without breaking the larger line. If a rest and a breath mark appear together, honour the rest first; the breath mark then confirms that the silence is also a musical comma.

Comparing the three in one session is useful. Play a short phrase with a slur, then with a rest, then with only a breath. The lengths will feel different, and your hands will learn that the comma is the smallest of the three.

Placing the lift without losing the pulse

A few checks keep the comma short and musical.

  • Finish the note before the mark, then lift instead of joining it to the next note.
  • Keep a pulse in mind, so the space stays a comma rather than a lost beat.
  • Start the next note with a calm, clear attack, as if a new sentence were beginning.

A common difficulty is that the lift becomes a stumble, or that the next phrase starts late. Practise with a slow pulse, spoken or tapped, and put the breath inside that pulse. The beat does not stop; the tone stops briefly. If you use a metronome, the click should still arrive on time after the lift.

You can also practise the two notes on either side of the mark on their own, first joined, then separated by a tiny space, until the space is intentional.

When the mark is editorial

Some breath marks come from the composer, and some are added by an editor as a suggestion. If the same piece appears in two editions, you may find a mark in one and a slur in the other. Try both, then keep the shaping that makes the sentence clear. If you have a teacher, ask which version to follow for a lesson or a performance. A light pencil comma in your copy can record that decision.

The aim is not to collect as many lifts as possible. Too many breaths can chop a melody into fragments. Use the sign where it helps the listener follow the thought.

A short experiment with a sung line

Sing a sentence from a familiar song, then play that sentence on the piano, adding a lift where you needed air. The physical breath of the voice is longer than a piano comma, so shorten it at the keyboard, but keep the same place in the phrase. This experiment is especially helpful for people who already like to sing, and it gives a clear reason for a sign that might otherwise look optional.

Then return to your printed piece and look for the same kind of seam: the end of a question, the end of a small group, or a place where a new idea enters.

Carrying the lift into the next session

When you come back to the same page, look first at the breath marks you have already tried. Play only the notes on either side of each one, then put them back into the phrase. A short review like this keeps the comma from disappearing once you start thinking about other details.

FAQ

Q: Does a breath mark mean I should stop for a whole beat?

A: No. It is usually a short lift, closer to a comma than to a full rest, unless the music already contains a longer silence.

Q: Should both hands lift at every breath mark?

A: Not always. If the accompaniment supports the line, it may continue while the melody lifts. Listen and choose what makes the phrase clear.

Q: What if two editions disagree about a breath?

A: Try both shapings, then keep the one that makes the sentence clear, and write a light reminder in your copy.

Q: Can I add a breath where none is printed?

A: Sometimes a small lift helps, especially if you would breathe there when singing. Add only what serves the phrase, and keep the pulse steady.
