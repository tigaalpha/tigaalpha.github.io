Fugue Basics: Subject, Answer and Countersubject Explained

Music theory · 2026-11-11

A fugue is a piece of music built from a short theme, called the subject, that is introduced by one voice and then taken up by other voices in turn, each entering while the earlier ones continue. The result is a texture of several independent melodic lines woven together. Fugues are central to Baroque keyboard music, and understanding their basic parts makes them far easier to listen to and to practise.

The subject: the main idea

The subject is a short, memorable melody, usually lasting a few bars. It often has a distinctive rhythm or interval at its beginning so that listeners can recognise it whenever it returns. A fugue opens with the subject played alone in one voice, giving the ear a clear introduction before the texture thickens.

Learning to sing or hum the subject before playing a fugue is one of the most helpful preparation steps. If you know it well, you will notice it in every voice.

The answer: the subject in a new key

After the subject finishes, a second voice enters with the answer. The answer is the subject transposed, usually to the key a fifth higher, the dominant. Sometimes it is an exact copy at the new pitch, known as a real answer. Sometimes a note or two is adjusted to keep the music anchored in the home key, known as a tonal answer.

Why adjustments happen

If the subject begins by leaping from the tonic to the dominant, an exact transposition would move the music too far away too quickly. Composers tweak the answer so that it still sounds closely related to the opening.

The countersubject: a faithful companion

While the second voice plays the answer, the first voice continues with new material. If that material returns regularly alongside later entries of the subject, it is called a countersubject. A good countersubject contrasts with the subject in rhythm and direction, so both can be heard clearly at once.

  • If the subject moves in long notes, the countersubject may move in shorter ones.
  • If the subject rises, the countersubject may fall.
  • The two lines are written so that either can sit above the other, which is called invertible counterpoint.

The exposition and what follows

The opening section, in which each voice enters with the subject or answer, is called the exposition. In a three-voice fugue, there are three entries; in a four-voice fugue, four.

After the exposition, the fugue alternates between further entries of the subject, sometimes in new keys, and passages called episodes. Episodes usually do not contain the full subject but develop fragments of it, often through sequences that move the music towards a new key. Near the end, composers may bring entries closer together, overlapping them in a device called stretto, to build intensity.

Practising a fugue at the piano

Playing several independent lines with two hands is demanding. A structured approach helps.

  • Mark every entry of the subject in the score with a coloured pencil, noting which voice has it.
  • Practise each voice alone, singing or playing it as a single melody, until its shape is clear.
  • Combine two voices at a time before playing all of them together.
  • Choose a consistent fingering that allows each voice to stay connected, even when the hands share a voice.

Listening with a map

When listening to a fugue, start by identifying the subject in the opening voice. Then count the entries in the exposition and note the order of voices, for example middle, top, bottom. Afterwards, listen for the subject returning in new keys and notice the episodes in between. With practice, the texture that once sounded like a tangle becomes a clear conversation.

Smaller cousins of the fugue

Students often meet fugal writing first in shorter forms such as two-part inventions or fughettas. These pieces use the same principles of imitation on a smaller scale and are excellent preparation for full fugues.

FAQ

Q: Does every fugue have a countersubject?

A: No. Some fugues use freely changing material alongside the subject instead of a regular companion line.

Q: What is stretto in a fugue?

A: Stretto occurs when a new entry of the subject begins before the previous one has finished, creating overlapping statements.

Q: How many voices does a fugue have?

A: Most keyboard fugues use three or four voices, though two-voice and five-voice fugues exist.

Q: Is a fugue a form or a technique?

A: Many musicians describe it as a technique or procedure, because fugues vary widely in overall shape.
