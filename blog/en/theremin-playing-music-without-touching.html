The Theremin: Playing Music Without Touching

Music theory · 2026-11-14

The theremin is an electronic instrument played without touching it. The player moves their hands in the air near two metal antennas. One antenna controls pitch and the other controls volume. The instrument was invented in the early twentieth century and is known for a smooth, gliding, voice-like tone that sounds a little eerie.

How it makes sound

The theremin uses electrical circuits that react to the closeness of the player's body.

Pitch from one hand

An antenna senses how near the hand is. As the hand moves closer, the pitch rises. Moving it away lowers the pitch. There are no keys or frets to mark the notes.

Volume from the other hand

A second antenna works in a similar way for loudness. Often, bringing the hand closer makes the sound quieter, and moving it away makes it louder. The player shapes the start and end of each note with this hand.

A continuous tone

Because the pitch changes smoothly, the player can slide between notes. This is what gives the instrument its singing quality.

Why it is difficult to play in tune

Without a fixed place for each note, the player must rely on ear and muscle memory. Several things make this demanding.

  • The hand has to hold a position in empty air with no support.
  • A small movement can change the pitch noticeably.
  • The player's body position can affect the response.
  • Quick passages need practised, precise gestures.

What keyboard learners can take from it

A piano has fixed pitches, so the instrument will always play the right note if you press the right key. The theremin shows the other side of musicianship: the listening needed to place a pitch in your mind before you play it.

  • Hearing the note inwardly before you play it.
  • Shaping the beginning and end of a note with care.
  • Moving smoothly between pitches in a phrase.
  • Treating volume as part of the melody, not an afterthought.

An ear-training game at the piano

You can borrow the idea without a theremin.

Sing, then check

Sing a note, then play the key you think matches it. Listen to how close you were.

Slide in your voice

Glide from one note to another with your voice, then play the two notes on the piano. Notice what the keyboard does not show you, which is the path between them.

Shape one note

Play a single key and listen to its decay. Try to make the next note match the loudness of the first one's tail.

What this says about technology and music

Gesture as an instrument

New instruments often invite new ways of moving. The theremin turned gesture into sound. Today, software can also respond to movement, voice or playing, and learning tools can listen to what you do. A little history in context

The theremin belongs to the story of early electronic instruments, when inventors discovered that electricity could produce musical tones. It was one of the first instruments to be played without physical contact, and it appeared in concerts and later in film soundtracks to suggest strange or futuristic worlds. Its place in history reminds us that the line between a musical instrument and a scientific device has always been thin. Learning about such instruments broadens what a young musician thinks of as possible, and it can make the familiar piano feel like one choice among many.

Why that matters to learners

Knowing that many sound sources exist makes listening more curious. You start to ask how a sound was made, and that curiosity feeds practice.

FAQ

Q: Do you touch a theremin?

A: No. The player moves their hands near the antennas without making contact.

Q: Is it hard to learn?

A: Many find it demanding because there are no markers for pitches, so ear and control matter a great deal.

Q: Can a piano player learn it quickly?

A: Keyboard skills help with musical knowledge, but the hand control is different and needs separate practice.

Q: Why does it sound like a singing voice?

A: The pitch can glide continuously, just as a voice can.
