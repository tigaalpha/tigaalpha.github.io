Two-Note Slurs for Beginners: The Drop and Roll Touch

Practice tips · 2026-11-08

A two-note slur is one of the first expressive shapes beginners meet in piano music. On the page it looks small: a curved line joining two notes. In sound, it should feel like a sigh or a spoken word with the stress on the first syllable, such as "MA-ma" or "HEL-lo". The first note is fuller, the second lighter and slightly shorter. The physical motion behind that sound is often called "drop and roll", and it is easier than it looks.

What the two-note slur asks for

When two notes are joined by a short slur, the general convention is to connect them smoothly, give the first note a little more weight and release the second note gently. The second note is often played a touch shorter, so that a small space appears before whatever comes next. Teachers sometimes describe it as "strong, weak, lift".

Listening comes first

Before working on the motion, play two notes and say "sigh" out loud as you do it. Many learners find the sound they want simply by matching the shape of the spoken word.

The drop: sending weight into the first note

The first note begins with a relaxed drop of the wrist and arm. Imagine your hand as loose and heavy, held just above the key. Let it fall gently so the weight of the arm, not a push from the finger, makes the sound.

  • Keep the shoulder low and the elbow free.
  • Let the wrist dip slightly as the key goes down.
  • Keep the fingertip firm enough to carry the weight, but not stiff.

The result should be a warm, rounded tone rather than a sharp hit.

The roll: rising out of the second note

For the second note, the wrist moves upward and slightly forward while the next finger plays. It feels as though the hand is rolling off the key and floating away. Because the arm is rising, the second note is naturally softer. When the wrist reaches the top of its motion, the hand lifts off the key completely, which creates that small release at the end.

Think of a single movement

The drop and the roll are not two separate actions. They are one smooth down-up curve, like a small wave. Practising the wave away from the keyboard, on a tabletop or your knee, helps make it feel natural.

Common beginner tensions and how to release them

Pressing the second note too hard

Some learners give both notes equal force. Slow down and exaggerate the upward wrist motion so the arm cannot push.

Locking the wrist

A stiff wrist turns the slur into two separate taps. Rest the hand in your lap, let it hang, then return to the keys with that loose feeling.

Lifting too late

If the second note is held too long, the sigh disappears. Count "one-and" and lift on "and".

Raising the shoulder

Tension often creeps up into the shoulder. Take a breath out on the drop, and check in a mirror or a short video that the shoulder stays down.

A short practice sequence

Use five minutes at the start of practice for this touch.

  • Play slurs on neighbouring keys with fingers 2 and 3 in the right hand, slowly, ten times.
  • Repeat with fingers 3 and 4, which often feel weaker.
  • Do the same in the left hand.
  • Move the slur up and down the keyboard, one step at a time.
  • Finally, find the slurs in a piece you are learning and play just those pairs.

How the touch grows over time

As beginners get comfortable, the motion becomes smaller and quicker. Advanced players still use the same idea, but the wave may be almost invisible. Longer slurs over three or four notes build on it too: the arm drops at the start, carries weight through the middle and rolls off at the end. Getting two-note slurs right early lays down a habit that supports phrasing for years.

Getting feedback

A teacher can watch the wrist and arm and spot tension quickly. A practice tool like TIGA ALPHA can help you check whether notes are steady, while you focus on the feel of the motion. A useful reminder is that the sound should guide the movement, not the other way around.

FAQ

Q: Should the second note always be shorter?

A: Usually a little, but style and tempo matter. Follow your teacher's advice and listen to recordings of the piece.

Q: Can I use the drop and roll on fast music?

A: Yes, with a much smaller motion. Learn it slowly first so the shape is clear.

Q: What if my wrist feels tired?

A: Stop and shake out the hand. Fatigue often means too much effort; the motion should feel light.

Q: Is a two-note slur the same as a tie?

A: No. A tie joins two notes of the same pitch into one longer sound. A slur joins different pitches.
