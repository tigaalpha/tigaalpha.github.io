The Tarantella on Piano: A Fast Dance in Compound Time

Songs · 2026-11-12

A tarantella on the piano is a fast dance, often written in a compound metre such as six-eight or twelve-eight, in which a larger dotted beat holds a spinning surface of quicker notes. The name recalls a southern Italian dance tradition; piano tarantellas are usually concert or salon pieces that keep the sense of a rapid, circling motion. The technical task is to feel those larger beats, to keep repeating figures even, and to reach a lively tempo without losing the downbeats.

Compound time at a speed you can still count

In six-eight, two dotted-crotchet beats in a bar are usually more useful than six small counts, especially as the tempo rises. Clap the two larger beats, then tap the three inner notes of each beat, then play a simple repeating figure in one hand with the same grouping. If you only count six, the dance can feel busy and small; if you only feel one rush per bar, the inner groups can collapse.

A first counting check:

  • Clap two larger beats in a bar.
  • Tap three inner notes on each beat.
  • Play one repeating figure with the same grouping.

Mark the opening beat of every bar, and also the half-bar, in a few difficult passages, so the eye has a landing place.

Repeating figures that must stay even

Many tarantellas use circling accompaniment patterns or repeating right-hand figures. Practise a single bar, then two, until the figure is even at a modest speed. A bump on the same finger every time will become very obvious when the dance is fast. If a rotation of the hand or a slight shift of position makes the figure smoother, write that in and reuse it.

The left hand is a motor, not a series of surprises

When the left hand is the repeating figure, it should be learned until it can run quietly while attention stays on the tune or on a sudden contrast. When the right hand is the spinner, the left hand often marks the larger beats. Either way, one layer should be predictable enough to support the other.

Building speed without losing the dance

Use a ladder of tempos: exact at a modest speed, then a little quicker, then back down if the downbeats slip. Do not jump to the final tempo with a hope that excitement will carry the notes. Accent-planning helps: a small sense of arrival on the larger beats, with inner notes lighter, keeps the spin organised.

Contrasts, slower corners and a final stop

Some tarantellas include a more lyrical middle, a sudden piano, or a brief slower indication before the dance returns. Practise those corners as carefully as the fast figuration, and practise the restart of the quick tempo, which is often where control is lost. The ending should be planned: a clear final pattern, a last downbeat, and a still hand, not a vague tumble off the last page.

If you programme a tarantella after a quiet piece, leave enough silence, then start from the inner count, so the first bar is already in the dance metre.

A short order for learning a tarantella

  • Clap the larger dotted beats, then the inner groups.
  • Learn one repeating figure until it is even.
  • Keep one hand as a stable motor under or over the other.
  • Raise tempo in steps, protecting the downbeats.
  • Practise lyrical corners and the restart of the fast dance, plus a clear ending.

FAQ

Q: Should I always feel two beats in a six-eight tarantella?

A: Two larger beats are a useful default. In a very broad middle section you might feel the inner groups more clearly, then return to the two-beat dance.

Q: What if my repeating figure always speeds up?

A: Practise it with a click on the dotted beats, then without, and return to a slower exact version as soon as the downbeats slip.

Q: Is a tarantella meant to sound out of control?

A: It should sound lively and circling, not careless. Excitement comes from speed plus evenness, not from lost beats.

Q: How much pedal is useful?

A: A little may warm a lyrical corner. In the spinning figures, too much pedal smears the groups. Try those bars without pedal first.

Q: Can I start practice at the final tempo for a short burst?

A: A short burst can be a check later. It should not replace a slower, exact version of the same figure.
