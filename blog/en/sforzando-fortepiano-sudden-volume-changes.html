Sforzando and Fortepiano: Sudden Changes in Volume

Music theory · 2026-11-21

Sforzando and fortepiano are marks that change the volume in an instant, rather than over a long phrase. Sforzando, often written sf or sfz, asks for a sudden strong accent on a note or a chord. Fortepiano, written fp, asks you to begin loudly and become soft at once. Both signs are easy to overplay if you treat them as a general invitation to use more force. They work better when you treat them as precise, short events.

What each sign is asking for

Sforzando is an accent with a clear bite. The marked note stands out from its neighbours, then the music usually returns to the dynamic that was already in place. Fortepiano is a two-part idea on the same note or chord: a loud beginning, then an immediate drop to a quiet level, which often continues into the notes that follow.

The two marks can look similar on a busy page, so it is worth saying the full word when you first meet them. “Sforzando” reminds you to accent. “Fortepiano” reminds you to start loud and then become soft.

Preparing the touch before the note

A sudden change is prepared in the beat before it happens. For a sforzando, arrive from a ready hand, not from a last-moment jab. The key still goes down to the same place; the difference is a quicker, more defined start to the tone. For a fortepiano on a long note or a chord, begin with a firm tone, then release the arm weight at once so the sound decays, or, on a digital piano, drop the following notes to a quiet level.

If the hand tightens, the accent becomes a thump and the quiet that follows is harder to control.

How these marks differ from a hairpin

A crescendo or diminuendo hairpin is a gradual change over several notes. Sforzando and fortepiano are sudden. If you spread an sf over a whole bar, the special moment disappears. If you fade an fp too slowly, it starts to sound like an ordinary diminuendo. Practise the contrast by playing the same phrase twice: once with a long hairpin, once with the sudden sign, and listen for the different shape.

This comparison is especially useful in teaching, because many learners already know hairpins and only need to hear that these marks are shorter.

Placing the accent inside a phrase

A few habits keep the sudden mark under control.

  • Keep the wrist free, even when the sound is strong.
  • Aim the accent at the centre of the key, not at the edge.
  • After an fp, listen to the quiet level and stay there until the score asks for something else.

An accent is still part of a line. After a sforzando, the next notes should belong to the same phrase, not sound like a new piece that starts from silence. Play the bar before the mark, the marked note, and the bar after, and ask whether the story continues. If the music is generally soft, the sforzando may be only a little stronger than its neighbours. If the music is already loud, the mark still needs a clear start, but not a blow that hurts the tone.

Fortepiano often appears at the beginning of a theme, or on a chord that then accompanies a quieter melody. In that case, the quiet that follows is as important as the loud start. Give it time in practice.

A small drill you can reuse

Choose one chord. Play it five times as a gentle mezzo piano, five times with a clear sforzando, and five times as fortepiano, letting the sound drop. Rest between groups so the arm stays soft. Then put the same touches into a short phrase from your piece. The drill is short, and it keeps the two marks distinct in your hands.

If you record a single attempt, listen for whether the sf is a spike in a continuing line, and whether the fp really falls to a quieter place rather than staying half-loud.

When several sudden marks appear together

Some passages have an sf on every beat, or a row of fp chords. In that case, the surprise is no longer a single event; it becomes a texture of repeated attacks and drops. Keep each attack clear, but do not let the arm stack tension from one mark to the next. A small lift between chords helps. If the page looks crowded, circle the marks lightly so you see them early.

FAQ

Q: Is sforzando the same as a regular accent mark?

A: They are related, but sf or sfz often asks for a more sudden, noticeable stress than a small accent wedge.

Q: Does fortepiano mean I should strike the key as hard as possible?

A: No. Begin with a firm, loud tone, then become soft at once, without forcing the sound or tightening the wrist.

Q: What if the fp is on a short staccato note?

A: The drop may be heard in the notes that follow, which should stay quieter after the short loud attack.

Q: Should I use the soft pedal for every fp?

A: Not automatically. Try controlling the drop with touch first; add a pedal change only if it helps the colour and keeps the line clear.
