Practice Goals Children Can Measure: Clean Repetitions, Not Minutes

Practice tips · 2026-11-02

Measurable practice goals tell a child exactly what to achieve, such as "play bars 1 to 4 three times in a row with no stops", instead of only how long to sit at the piano. Time-based instructions like "practise for 20 minutes" often lead to watching the clock, playing the easy parts again and again, or rushing through the piece. A countable goal gives the session a clear purpose and a clear end, so children can see their own progress and feel proud when they reach it.

Parents do not need musical training to support this approach. They only need to help the child choose a goal and notice when it is reached.

Why minutes alone do not work well

Twenty minutes can contain a lot of useful work or very little. A child may spend most of the time on a favourite section that is already secure, avoiding the tricky bar that really needs attention. Clock-watching also teaches children that practice is something to endure. When the goal is a result rather than a duration, the child naturally spends time where it is needed.

Combine both if needed

Some families like a time frame for routine. That is fine; simply add a goal inside the time, such as "in today's 15 minutes, make the left hand of line two smooth".

Five types of goals that children understand

  • Repetition goals: "Play this bar correctly three times in a row."
  • Tempo goals: "Play the scale with the metronome at the next speed up."
  • Accuracy goals: "Play the first line with all the correct notes and fingers."
  • Musical goals: "Make the last phrase get softer like it is falling asleep."
  • Independence goals: "Play the piece from memory to the end of the first page."

Rotate the types through the week so practice does not become repetitive.

A simple tracking chart

A paper chart on the piano works well for younger children. Each day, write one or two goals in simple words and leave a box to tick. Stickers or coloured stars make the chart attractive. Older children may prefer a notes app or a small notebook. The important part is that the goal is written before practice starts, not decided afterwards.

Making goals the right size

A good goal is challenging but reachable within one session. If a child fails repeatedly, the goal is too big. Break it into smaller parts, such as one hand alone or two bars instead of four. If the goal is reached in two minutes, raise it slightly next time. Teachers can suggest suitable sizes, and many will happily write weekly goals in the lesson notebook.

The "three in a row" rule

Playing a passage correctly once may be luck. Three correct runs in a row show the skill is becoming reliable. Count aloud together so the child feels the achievement.

How parents can respond

Praise effort and specific results: "You kept the rhythm steady even in the hard bar" is more useful than "Great job". Avoid correcting every note during practice, since that can feel like a test. Instead, ask the child to tell you the goal and show you when it is reached. If the child is frustrated, suggest a short break and a smaller target.

  • Ask: "What is today's goal?"
  • Listen: let the child show you without interruption.
  • Notice: mention one specific improvement.
  • Celebrate: tick the box together.

Keeping motivation over months

Measurable goals add up. After a few weeks, the chart becomes a visible record of progress, which can be motivating on difficult days. At the end of each month, look back at the chart together and choose one piece to play as a small home performance. Linking daily goals to a bigger musical event gives the effort meaning.

Children also enjoy choosing some goals themselves. Offer two or three options and let them pick, which builds ownership and independence. Over time, the aim is for children to set sensible goals without help, a skill that is useful far beyond the piano.

FAQ

Q: How many goals should a child have per session?

A: One to three is enough. More goals make sessions feel crowded.

Q: What if my child reaches the goal quickly?

A: Celebrate it, then either finish early or add a small bonus goal. Raise the challenge slightly next time.

Q: Should I stop using practice time completely?

A: Not necessarily. A regular time slot helps routine; goals simply make that time more productive.

Q: Can apps help track goals?

A: Yes. Some learning apps show completed steps, accuracy or tempo, which makes goals easy to see.
