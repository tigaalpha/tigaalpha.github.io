Stop-Prepare Practice: Landing Piano Leaps Accurately

Practice tips · 2026-11-11

A piano leap is any jump where the hand leaves one position and must arrive on a new one, often with little time in between. Missed leaps usually come from travelling and playing in one rushed motion, so the fingers arrive late or on the wrong keys. Stop-prepare practice separates those actions: you move, you stop over the landing notes, you confirm the position, and only then do you play. This article explains how to use the method in everyday practice and teaching.

Why leaps are often missed

When a leap is practised at full speed from the start, the hand tends to throw itself toward the new position and hope the notes will be there. The eyes may also leave the score too late, or stay on the keyboard the whole way and lose the place on the page. The result is a habit of guessing rather than travelling to a known place.

  • The departure notes may be released too early, so the phrase loses shape.
  • The arm may swing past the target and have to correct in the air.
  • The landing fingers may not be ready, so the notes speak late or unevenly.

The stop-prepare idea

Stop-prepare turns one blurry motion into two clear steps.

Move and stop

From the last note before the leap, travel to the new position and stop above the keys, touching them lightly or hovering just over them, without sounding the notes yet.

Check, then play

Look at the landing, or feel the key edges if the eyes need to stay on the score, confirm that the correct fingers are over the correct notes, then play.

The stop can be long at first, even several beats. The point is not a dramatic pause for its own sake; it is to teach the hand a reliable path and a clear destination.

A simple drill for one leap

Choose a single leap that is not yet secure. Work on it by itself, not inside a full run-through.

  • Play the note or chord you leave from, at a slow tempo.
  • Move to the landing position and stop, silently, until the position feels obvious.
  • Play the landing notes, then pause again and notice whether they were the right keys and a clean sound.
  • Repeat the same leap several times before changing anything else.

If the landing is a chord, form the whole shape in the air during the stop, not one finger at a time after you have already arrived.

Shrinking the stop to fit the beat

Once the landing is consistent at a slow pace, shorten the stop little by little. The stop shrinks from a long pause, to a small hesitation, to a tiny preparation that fits inside the written beat.

Only then play the leap in its musical context, still a little under tempo, and listen for a clean arrival that does not disturb the pulse. If the leap becomes messy again, lengthen the stop for a few more repetitions rather than repeating the passage faster.

A short cycle works well: isolated stop-prepare, then the surrounding bar with a smaller stop, then the written rhythm. Keep each stage unhurried.

Eyes, arm path and landing spots

A few details make the drill more reliable.

Choose a landmark

Pick a clear landing reference, such as a black-key group or the edge of a long key, so the hand travels to a place it can recognise, not to a vague area of the keyboard.

Use an efficient path

The arm moves in a smooth arc, just high enough to clear the keys, and arrives from above rather than sliding in from the side at the last moment.

Decide what the eyes do

For a wide leap, a quick glance at the landing during the stop is useful. As the stop shortens, practise glancing and returning to the score so the next notes are still readable.

Checking landings with clear feedback

A recording, or another listener, can confirm what you felt. It is easy to believe a leap was accurate when the landing was only close. An app such as TIGA ALPHA can also mark whether the landing note was the right pitch and whether it arrived on time, which turns a vague impression into specific information you can practise with. Use that information to decide whether the stop is still needed or can be shortened.

Teaching stop-prepare in a lesson

Teachers can demonstrate the stop in an exaggerated way, then ask the student to copy the same leap with a silent pause on the landing keys. It helps to isolate the leap first, rather than stopping a whole piece each time the jump appears.

  • Ask the student to name the landing notes before moving.
  • Watch whether the hand shape is ready during the stop.
  • Revisit the same leap at the end of the lesson, still with a short preparation, so the student has a clear home practice task.

Parents who sit in on practice can listen for a brief, deliberate pause before a difficult jump, and remind the learner to use it rather than repeating the bar at speed.

FAQ

Q: Does stop-prepare only work for large jumps?

A: No. It is also useful for smaller shifts and for leaps in either hand, including a left-hand bass note that must be found quickly.

Q: Should I stop above the keys or rest on them silently?

A: Either can work. Resting lightly on the keys often makes the position clearer; hovering is useful when you want to feel the final drop into the notes.

Q: How slowly should I start?

A: Slow enough that the stop is genuinely still and the landing notes are prepared before they sound. Speed comes after the path is familiar.

Q: What if I still miss the leap once the stop is gone?

A: Bring a short stop back for a few repetitions, check the landmark and the hand shape, then shorten the pause again. Returning to the drill is part of the method, not a setback.
