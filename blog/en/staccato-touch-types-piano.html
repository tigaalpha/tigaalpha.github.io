Staccato Touches on Piano: Short Notes With Different Characters

Practice tips · 2026-11-11

Staccato is an instruction to play notes detached, with space between them, and it is usually shown by a dot above or below the notehead. On the piano, however, there is no single staccato sound. A dot in a lively dance calls for a crisp, bouncing touch, while a dot in a soft lullaby asks for a gentle separation. Learning several kinds of staccato, and deciding which one a passage needs, turns a mechanical instruction into an expressive choice.

What the markings mean

Several symbols indicate shortness, and their meaning depends partly on style and period.

  • A dot: ordinary staccato, roughly half the written value or shorter.
  • A wedge or vertical stroke: a sharper, more pointed staccato in many editions.
  • A dot under a slur: portato or mezzo-staccato, gently separated notes within a phrase.
  • A dot with a line: a slightly separated but weighted note.

Editors and composers have not always used these signs consistently, so the musical context is always the final guide.

Finger staccato

Finger staccato uses a quick, small motion from the finger itself, with the hand and arm staying quiet. It produces light, fast, precise notes, ideal for running passages in classical sonatinas and for delicate ornaments.

How to practise it

Place the fingertip on the key, then flick it slightly towards the palm as the key goes down, letting the key spring back immediately. Keep the motion small. Try five-note patterns at a soft dynamic, listening for even spacing between notes.

Wrist staccato

Wrist staccato comes from a small bounce of the hand at the wrist, like a gentle dribble of a ball. The fingers stay firm but not stiff, and the wrist provides the movement. This touch suits repeated chords, octaves and medium-speed accompaniment patterns.

Practise by bouncing a single chord in different rhythms, keeping the elbow loose and the shoulders down. If the sound becomes harsh, reduce the height of the bounce rather than pressing harder.

Arm staccato

For loud, dramatic chords, the whole forearm can drop into the keys and rebound. Arm staccato produces a full, resonant sound because more weight is involved. It appears in climaxes, marches and big chordal endings. The key is to let the arm fall and then release immediately, without holding the keys down or forcing the sound.

Choosing the right staccato for a passage

Before practising, ask what the passage is meant to express. A playful scherzo might call for bright finger staccato; a solemn march might need heavier arm staccato; a nocturne with dotted notes under a slur might call for soft portato. Listening to several recordings helps, as does singing the line and noticing how your voice naturally separates the notes.

Tempo matters too. Fast staccato usually relies on fingers and a little wrist; slow staccato can use more arm because there is time to rebound fully.

A practice routine for clean staccato

Short, focused work brings faster improvement than long repetition.

  • Play a five-note scale with finger staccato, soft, then with wrist staccato, medium loud.
  • Alternate one bar legato with one bar staccato to train quick changes of touch.
  • Play staccato notes against a sustained note in the other hand to build independence.
  • Record yourself and check that the silences between notes are even.

Using the pedal with staccato

Sometimes composers ask for staccato notes while the damper pedal is down, creating a dry attack inside a resonant sound. In other places, any pedal would blur the effect entirely. Check the score carefully, and when in doubt, practise first without pedal so the touch is clear before adding colour.

FAQ

Q: Does staccato mean playing loudly?

A: No. Staccato refers to length, not volume. Staccato notes can be very soft or very loud.

Q: How short should a staccato note be?

A: It depends on style and tempo. As a general guide, the note should be noticeably shorter than written, with clear space before the next note.

Q: Why do my staccato notes sound uneven?

A: Different fingers often rebound at different speeds. Practise slowly with a metronome and listen to the gaps as well as the notes.

Q: Can I use pedal during staccato?

A: Sometimes. Follow the composer's markings and the style; many staccato passages sound clearer without pedal.
