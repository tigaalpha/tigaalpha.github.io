Counting Sixteenth Notes on Piano: One-e-and-a Made Simple

Music theory · 2026-11-11

Sixteenth notes are where rhythm starts to feel fast. Each quarter-note beat is divided into four equal parts, and many pieces use them for running passages, lively accompaniments and rhythmic patterns. Without a reliable way to count them, players tend to rush, drag or blur the notes together. A simple counting system, combined with careful practice, makes sixteenths feel controlled and even.

What a sixteenth note looks like

A sixteenth note has a filled notehead, a stem and two flags. When grouped, sixteenths are joined by two beams. Four sixteenths equal one quarter note in common time. A sixteenth rest looks like an eighth rest with an extra hook.

The one-e-and-a count

The most widely used counting system divides each beat into four syllables:

  • "one" on the beat
  • "e" on the second sixteenth
  • "and" on the halfway point, where the eighth note falls
  • "a" on the fourth sixteenth, just before the next beat

A bar of 4/4 filled with sixteenths is counted: one-e-and-a, two-e-and-a, three-e-and-a, four-e-and-a. Say the syllables evenly, as if each one has exactly the same length. Clapping while counting helps fix the pattern.

Common rhythm patterns

Sixteenths often appear combined with eighth notes. Learn these four patterns, each lasting one beat:

Four sixteenths: one-e-and-a, all notes played.

Eighth plus two sixteenths: one, and-a. The first note lasts for "one-e".

Two sixteenths plus an eighth: one-e, and. The last note lasts for "and-a".

Sixteenth, eighth, sixteenth: one, e, a. The middle note is longer, which gives a syncopated feel.

Dotted rhythms are closely related. A dotted eighth followed by a sixteenth is counted "one, a": the long note fills "one-e-and", and the short note falls on "a".

Practising sixteenths evenly

Start slowly

Set a metronome to a slow tempo, such as 60 beats per minute, and play four even notes per click. Count aloud.

Use rhythm variations

Practise a run of sixteenths in dotted rhythms (long-short, then short-long) and in groups that pause on different notes. Returning to even notes afterwards often reveals improved control.

Accent the beat, then remove the accent

At first, accent the first sixteenth of each group to anchor the beat. Later, play all four notes with equal weight so the line sounds smooth.

Subdivide the metronome

If your metronome can play subdivisions, set it to click every sixteenth. This shows immediately whether any note is early or late.

Fingering and sixteenths

Fast notes depend on good fingering. Choose fingerings that avoid unnecessary thumb crossings and keep the hand in a natural position. Write the fingering in the score and use it consistently, so the hand learns one pattern. When a passage of sixteenths feels uneven, check whether a weak finger, often the fourth, falls on an important note, and consider an alternative.

Sixteenths in different styles

In Baroque music, sixteenth-note runs often form long, flowing lines that should sound even and clear. In Classical sonatas, they appear in scales and Alberti-style accompaniments. In pop and funk, sixteenth-note patterns create rhythmic grooves where certain notes are accented. Listening to examples from each style helps you understand how the same rhythmic unit can feel different.

Reading sixteenths quickly

Reading speed and playing speed develop together. A learner who can recognise rhythm shapes at a glance has more attention left for notes, fingering and dynamics, so time spent on rhythm reading pays off across every piece.

When reading, look for beamed groups rather than individual notes. Recognising the four common patterns as shapes, instead of counting each note separately, makes sight-reading faster. Pencil in counting syllables above difficult rhythms at first, then remove them as recognition improves.

FAQ

Q: How do I count sixteenth notes?

A: Use "one-e-and-a" for each beat, saying the four syllables evenly.

Q: Why do my sixteenth notes rush?

A: Often because the tempo is too fast for control; slow down, count aloud and use a metronome with subdivisions.

Q: How are dotted eighths related to sixteenths?

A: A dotted eighth lasts three sixteenths, so a dotted eighth plus a sixteenth fills one beat.

Q: What is the hardest sixteenth pattern?

A: Many learners find the sixteenth-eighth-sixteenth pattern tricky because the long middle note creates syncopation.
