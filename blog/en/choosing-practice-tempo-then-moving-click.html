Choosing a Practice Tempo With a Click, Then Moving It

Practice tips · 2026-11-12

A practice tempo is useful only if the job you set can actually be done there. A click makes that tempo visible, and it stops the hands from drifting faster as a passage becomes familiar. The next skill is to move the click later, in small steps, after the aim has held for several loops, rather than jumping to a hoped-for speed in one sitting. Teachers and learners can share this plan, because the number on the click is easy to write in the score and easy to return to the next day.

Choose the tempo at which the written aim still holds

If the aim is an even left-hand figure, a clean fingering seam, or a counted tie, pick a click where that aim is possible, even if the tempo feels modest. Play a loop and ask only whether the aim held, not whether the music already sounds finished. If the aim fails, slow the click before you add more bars. Writing the number beside the passage saves a later guess. A tempo chosen from pride, or from a recording, is often too fast for the job in hand.

Stay at that click for several honest loops

One clean loop is not yet a reason to move. Repeat the same bars enough times that the aim holds without a rescue, then stop or rest. If a later loop fails, stay at the same click and repair the specific bar; do not treat the failure as a sign to go faster "to get through it." The click is a fence as well as a help. Learners sometimes need this point said plainly: speed is not the reward for a single good try.

Move the click in small steps, then test the same bars

When the loops have held, raise or lower the click by a modest amount and play the same passage. Do not add new bars at the same moment as a new speed. If the aim holds at the new number, you may stay there and collect a few more loops. If it fails, go back to the previous click and work the failing bar, then try the step again another day. A series of small, honest moves usually travels further than one large jump that cannot be repeated.

  • Write the aim and choose a click at which that aim is possible.
  • Stay for several loops that hold, not for a single lucky pass.
  • Move the click by a small step, then test the same bars again.
  • If the aim fails at the new number, return to the previous click and repair.
  • Record the number that worked, so the next sitting has a starting point.

When not to move the click in this sitting

Do not move the click if the hands are tired, if the aim has only held once, or if you have just changed a fingering or a pedal plan. Those sittings are for keeping the passage steady. Teachers can assign a starting number and a rule such as "move only after four loops that hold." The piece then gains speed as a result of controlled work, not as a race against a clock at the end of the lesson.

FAQ

Q: How small should each step on the click be?

A: Small enough that the aim might still hold. A modest rise is more useful than a jump that immediately breaks the passage.

Q: What if I do not have a click?

A: You can still choose a modest pulse and count it, but a click is clearer to write down and easier to share with a teacher.

Q: Should every passage use this method?

A: It is most useful for bars with a clear technical aim. A slow, sung line may need a pulse without a hard click, or with the click on a larger beat.

Q: Can I move the click downward as well as up?

A: Yes. A slightly slower click is the right move when the aim has started to slip, or when a new fingering has just been written in.

Q: How do I remember yesterday's number?

A: Write it in the score beside the passage, with the date or a short note if the aim was special. The next sitting then starts from a known place.
