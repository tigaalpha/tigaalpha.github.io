Redistributing Notes Between Hands: Sharing a Line on Piano

Practice tips · 2026-11-11

Redistributing notes between the hands is a practical decision, not a change of the written music's meaning: a pitch that was printed in the right-hand staff may be taken by the left, or the other way around, so the passage becomes more secure. The listener should still hear the same line. The hands are simply sharing the work in a more comfortable way.

When a share is worth considering

A redistribution is useful when a stretch is unreliable, when two voices in one hand collide, or when a leap leaves no time to prepare the next tone. It is less useful when it merely moves a problem into the other hand, or when it hides a voice that should stay visibly separate for teaching.

Ask a few honest questions:

  • Does one hand have an easier reach to this pitch without a twist?
  • Will the other hand still be able to play its remaining notes?
  • Can the musical line still be heard as one line, not as two bumps?
  • Is the change possible at the tempo of the piece, not only at a slow rehearsal speed?

Marking the change so rehearsal stays consistent

Write the new hand in the score

Once you choose a redistribution, write it in the score: a small bracket, a fingering number that belongs to the other hand, or a tiny "L.H." or "R.H." above the note. Do not rely on memory alone. In a later session you will forget which version you used, and the hands will hesitate.

Keep the original printed staff as the source of the pitches. Your marks are a performance choice. If a teacher prefers the printed distribution, the marks can be discussed and changed; they should not be invisible.

Keeping voicing and phrasing after the share

A note taken by the left hand still belongs to the right-hand melody if that is its musical role. Match the tone to the line, not to the usual weight of a bass. Conversely, an inner alto note taken by the right hand should not suddenly sound like a new soprano.

Practise the line as a single voice, slowly, with the new distribution, then add the remaining accompaniment. If the join between hands is audible as a bump, overlap the two tones slightly or match the decay so the ear hears continuity.

A careful way to test the idea

Play the passage as printed, then play your redistributed version, both at a comfortable tempo. Keep the one that is more accurate and more even. If they are equal, the printed version may still be better for reading, especially if you later play from a clean copy. Choose on musical and technical grounds, not on novelty.

After the choice is stable, a short session in TIGA ALPHA can be used for even repetition of the same phrase. The decision about which hand plays which pitch should already be written in the score.

Redistribution in ensemble and accompaniment

When accompanying, a left-hand note that is also in a singer's pitch may sometimes be taken higher or lower in the texture, but that is an arrangement choice, not only a redistribution. Stay closer to the page in ensemble parts unless a colleague has agreed a change. In solo practice you have more freedom, as long as the sounding line remains the line written in the score.

FAQ

Q: Is redistribution a way of avoiding the page?

A: No. It is a common practical solution when it keeps the written pitches and the musical line intact. It should be marked and consistent.

Q: Should I redistribute a whole piece?

A: No. Use it where a local problem is clearly solved. Constant reassignment can make reading harder.

Q: What if a stretch is often unreliable?

A: A well-chosen share can reduce that stretch. It cannot replace slow practice of the passages that still belong in one hand.

Q: How do I stop a bump at the hand-off?

A: Match tone and timing, and practise the two notes around the join as a mini legato pair, even if two hands produce them.

Q: Should fingering numbers stay with the original hand?

A: Write fingerings for the hand that will actually play the note, so rehearsal is unambiguous.
