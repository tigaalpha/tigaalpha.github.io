Canons and Rounds on Piano: One Melody Chasing Itself

Songs · 2026-11-11

A canon is a piece in which one melody is played and then imitated exactly by another voice that enters a little later, so the tune overlaps with itself. A round is the simplest kind of canon: every voice sings or plays the same melody at the same pitch, entering one after another, and the music can loop as many times as the group likes. On the piano, playing a canon with two hands is a delightful way to build coordination, because each hand must keep its own place while the other plays the same notes at a different moment.

How imitation creates harmony

For a canon to sound good, the melody must be written so that its different parts fit together when stacked. In a round, the composer usually designs each phrase over the same chord pattern. When the second voice starts the first phrase, the first voice is playing the second phrase, and both fit the harmony underneath.

That is why many familiar rounds use only one or two chords. Simple harmony makes it easier for overlapping lines to agree.

Types of canon worth knowing

Composers have invented many variations on the basic idea.

  • Canon at the unison: both voices play the same pitches, like a classic round.
  • Canon at the octave: the second voice plays the same melody an octave higher or lower, which suits the two hands on the piano.
  • Canon at the fifth or other interval: the imitation starts on a different note, creating new harmonic colours.
  • Canon by inversion: the imitating voice turns the melody upside down, so rising steps become falling steps.
  • Augmentation canon: the second voice plays the same tune in longer note values, making it slower.

Hearing these variations in Baroque keyboard collections shows how much invention can come from one simple rule.

Playing your first piano canon

Choose a short folk melody or round you already know, such as a four-phrase tune in C major. Play it in the right hand first until it is completely secure.

Add the second voice

Next, play the same melody in the left hand an octave lower, starting when the right hand reaches the second phrase. Go slowly and count aloud.

Keep each hand honest

The most common problem is that one hand begins copying the rhythm of the other. If that happens, practise the overlap of just two phrases, hands together, very slowly until each line feels independent.

Listening to canons with fresh ears

When listening to a canon, try following one voice from beginning to end, then listen again and follow the other. On a third hearing, try to hear both at once. Repeated, focused listening of this kind trains the ear for counterpoint, where several melodies sound together, and it makes later study of fugues much easier.

Some composers hide canons in the middle of larger works, so listeners may not notice them at first. Spotting one is a satisfying discovery and a good test of attention.

A three-pass listening plan

  • First hearing: follow the upper voice.
  • Second hearing: follow the lower voice.
  • Third hearing: try to hold both in your ear.

Writing a short round

A round can be composed in a few steps, even by beginners.

  • Choose a simple chord loop, such as C major for two bars and G major for two bars.
  • Write a four-bar melody that fits the chords.
  • Write a second four-bar phrase over the same chords, using a different rhythm.
  • Check that the two phrases sound good when played together, then add a third if you like.

Testing the overlap

Play the result with a friend on one piano or record one line and play the other live. Tools such as TIGA ALPHA can check the timing of each hand while you practise the entries, which matters because canons expose any unevenness immediately.

Canons in group lessons

Teachers often use rounds in group classes because they let several students play at their own level while still sounding like one piece. Beginners can play the melody in single notes while more advanced students add a bass line, and everyone experiences the excitement of hearing a big texture built from simple parts.

FAQ

Q: What is the difference between a canon and a round?

A: A round is a type of canon in which voices enter at the same pitch and the melody can repeat endlessly. Canons can also start at other pitches and use other transformations.

Q: Are canons hard to play on piano?

A: Simple canons are accessible to late beginners. The challenge is keeping the hands independent, which improves with slow practice.

Q: Is a fugue the same as a canon?

A: No. A fugue begins with imitation but then develops the theme freely, while a canon keeps strict imitation throughout.

Q: Can I play a canon alone?

A: Yes. Canons at the octave work well with the right hand starting and the left hand imitating.
