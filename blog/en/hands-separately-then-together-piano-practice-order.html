Hands Separately, Then Together: A Clear Practice Order

Practice tips · 2026-11-20

When a piece is new, practise each hand alone first, then combine them in short sections at a slow speed. This lets your brain learn the notes and fingering for one hand without also coordinating the other. When you join the hands, you only have to manage the meeting point. Many learners find this order faster and less frustrating than trying to play everything at once.

Why separate practice works

Playing with both hands asks you to read two lines, plan two sets of fingers and keep a steady pulse. That is a lot at once. By practising one hand, you reduce the load. You can listen to shape and rhythm and notice fingering problems early. Later, the combined version rests on solid ground.

A step-by-step order

Try these steps with a short section of four to eight bars.

  • Read the right hand and clap its rhythm.
  • Play the right hand slowly, with the fingering written in the score.
  • Do the same for the left hand.
  • Play each hand twice more, aiming for calm, even notes.
  • Join the hands very slowly, one bar at a time.
  • Speed up in small steps, only when the combination is accurate.

If a step feels messy, go back one step. There is no shame in returning to hands alone.

Which hand first?

Many people start with the harder hand, because it needs more attention. Others start with the right hand because it carries the tune. Either is fine. The key is to give both hands equal time, so the weaker one catches up. If the left hand has repeated patterns, you can learn it quickly and then let it run on its own while the right hand adds detail.

Joining the hands

When you combine them, slow down more than you expect. A speed that feels almost silly is often right. Count aloud so that both hands share the same pulse. If one hand rushes, stop and play that hand alone again for a few seconds.

Check the meeting points

Look for places where both hands play together, such as the first beat of each bar. Practise these landing spots as pairs: play the two notes together, then move on. If the hands only meet on certain beats, you can think of those beats as anchors and fill in between them.

When it still feels hard

If a spot refuses to work, shrink it. Practise two beats at a time, then three, then a full bar. Change the rhythm temporarily, such as long-short or short-long, to break the pattern. Rest for a minute and try again. Short, focused attempts beat long, tired ones.

A note on patience

Hands-together playing often feels worse before it feels better, because the brain is adjusting to a new kind of coordination. Do not judge the result after one session. Come back the next day, and you may find that the passage has settled overnight. Many learners notice this effect, and it is a good reason to practise a little every day rather than for a long time once a week.

Keep it musical

Even in slow practice, shape the phrase. Make a small crescendo toward the highest note and relax at the end. Playing musically from the start helps you avoid a mechanical sound later.

A sample ten-minute session

Imagine a short section of eight bars. Spend two minutes on the right hand, two on the left, and two on each hand again with attention to tone. Use the last four minutes to join the hands for two bars at a time, resting briefly between attempts. This is only an example of how to divide your time. You can change the numbers to suit your own piece, but the principle holds: alone first, then together, with gentle repetition and plenty of listening throughout.

FAQ

Q: Should I always practise hands separately?

A: For new or tricky passages, yes. For easy sections, you can try both hands sooner.

Q: How slow is slow enough?

A: Slow enough that you play without hesitation or mistakes.

Q: Can I memorise hands separately too?

A: Yes, and many players do. It can make the memory more secure.

Q: What if my hands will not stay together?

A: Return to one hand, and count aloud when you combine again.
