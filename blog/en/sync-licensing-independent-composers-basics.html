Sync Licensing for Independent Composers: The Basics

News · 2026-11-04

Sync licensing is the permission to pair a piece of music with moving images, such as a film, advertisement, online video or game. The name comes from "synchronisation". For independent composers, a sync placement can bring a fee, ongoing royalties and new listeners. To get started, you need to own or control the rights to your music, prepare clean and well-labelled files, understand the two main rights involved and read each agreement carefully before signing.

The two rights in every recording

A recorded piece of music carries two separate sets of rights.

  • The composition: the melody, harmony and lyrics as written. This belongs to the songwriter or composer and any publisher they work with.
  • The master recording: the specific recorded performance. This belongs to whoever paid for or made the recording, often the artist or a label.

A sync licence usually needs permission for both. If you wrote, performed and recorded the track yourself, you control both sides, which makes licensing much simpler. That simplicity is one reason independent composers can be attractive to video producers working to tight deadlines.

Where sync music is used

Online video. Creators and brands need music for tutorials, travel videos and product clips.

Advertising. Commercials may license existing songs or commission new music to match a mood.

Film and television. Scenes often need background cues or a featured song at an emotional moment.

Games and apps. Interactive media use loops and adaptive tracks that change with the action.

Each area has different budgets and expectations. Small online projects may pay modest fees, while broadcast advertising can pay more but usually asks for broader rights.

Preparing your tracks

Music supervisors and editors work quickly, so make their job easy.

  • Provide high-quality audio files along with shorter edits, such as thirty-second and sixty-second versions.
  • Offer instrumental versions without vocals, which are easier to place under dialogue.
  • Label files with the title, your name, tempo, key and a few mood words.
  • Embed contact information and rights details in the file metadata.
  • Keep a clear record showing that you own every part of the track, including any samples.

Piano pieces are often in demand for emotional scenes, documentaries and calm brand videos. A simple, well-recorded piano cue with clear structure can be surprisingly useful.

Ways to find placements

Direct contact. Reach out to video producers, small agencies and creators whose work fits your style. Keep messages short and include a link to a few relevant tracks.

Libraries. Production music libraries collect tracks and license them to clients. Terms vary widely, so read whether the deal is exclusive or non-exclusive and how income is shared.

Collaborations. Working with local filmmakers or student projects can build experience and a portfolio, even when budgets are small.

Building a small sync catalogue

Rather than waiting for one big placement, many independent composers build a small catalogue over time. Aim for variety within a clear identity: a few gentle solo piano cues, a few lightly rhythmic pieces with soft percussion and a few short stings that can open or close a video. Keep a simple spreadsheet listing each track, its moods, tempo, length and available versions. When a request arrives, you can then reply within hours instead of days, which matters in a field where editors often need music the same week.

What to check in an agreement

Scope. Where will the music appear, for how long and in which countries?

Exclusivity. Can you license the same track to others?

Fee and royalties. Is there an upfront payment, and will you receive performance royalties when the work is broadcast?

Credit. Will your name appear in the credits or description?

Edits. May the client cut or change the music?

If anything is unclear, ask questions or seek advice from someone experienced in music contracts before signing. A good deal is one both sides understand.

FAQ

Q: Do I need a publisher to do sync licensing?

A: No. Many independent composers license their own music directly, though a publisher can help find opportunities.

Q: What is a one-stop track?

A: A track where one person or company controls both the composition and the master, so a single agreement covers everything.

Q: Should I offer my music for free to get exposure?

A: Sometimes it can build a portfolio, but think carefully. Set clear limits on how and where free music is used.

Q: What kind of piano music gets licensed?

A: Calm, emotional or simple rhythmic pieces with clear structure are often useful for video.
