The Dorian Mode on Piano: Minor With a Brighter Sixth

Music theory · 2026-11-11

The Dorian mode is a seven-note scale that sounds minor but contains a raised sixth step compared with the natural minor scale. The easiest way to find it on the piano is to play only white keys, starting on D and climbing to the D an octave higher. The flat third gives it a minor character, while the natural B, a major sixth above D, adds a lighter, slightly bittersweet colour that many listeners associate with folk tunes, jazz and funk grooves.

Comparing Dorian with natural minor

Play D natural minor first: D, E, F, G, A, B-flat, C, D. Then play D Dorian, which changes only the sixth note from B-flat to B. The two scales share six notes, yet the change makes a clear difference in mood.

  • Natural minor sounds darker and more settled into sadness.
  • Dorian sounds minor but less heavy, sometimes hopeful, sometimes cool and detached.
  • The characteristic interval is the major sixth between the tonic and the sixth step.

When you improvise, giving that sixth step a little time is what tells the ear the music is Dorian rather than ordinary minor.

Finding Dorian in any key

Dorian is the second mode of the major scale. Any major scale played from its second note produces a Dorian scale.

Using a parent major key

To find A Dorian, ask which major scale has A as its second note. The answer is G major, so A Dorian uses the notes of G major: A, B, C, D, E, F-sharp, G, A.

Using the minor scale shortcut

Alternatively, start from the natural minor scale on your tonic and raise the sixth step. E natural minor contains C, so E Dorian uses C-sharp instead.

Both methods give the same answer. Some players find the parent-key method faster for reading, and the minor-scale method faster for improvising.

The chords that make a Dorian sound

In D Dorian, the tonic chord is D minor. The chord that most clearly shows off the mode is G major, the fourth chord, because it contains B, the raised sixth. Moving back and forth between D minor and G major quickly sets up a Dorian atmosphere.

Other useful chords include C major, the flat seventh chord, and E minor. Try a loop of D minor, G major, D minor, C major, and you will hear a sound common in folk dances and in modal jazz.

Improvising with the mode

Start small. Hold a D minor chord in the left hand, or play a repeating D and A as a drone, and improvise with the right hand using only white keys.

  • Begin and end phrases on D so the ear knows where home is.
  • Land on B on strong beats now and then to highlight the Dorian colour.
  • Use short rhythmic motifs and repeat them with small changes rather than running up and down the scale.
  • Leave silence between phrases; rests make improvisation sound more confident.

Hearing Dorian in different styles

Many traditional melodies from the British Isles use Dorian or something very close to it. Jazz musicians often treat a minor seventh chord as a Dorian sound, especially in modal pieces built on long stretches of a single chord. Funk and soul players frequently use the minor seventh chord with an added sixth, which is another way of outlining the mode.

Writing a short Dorian piece

Try composing eight bars. Use D minor and G major as your only chords for the first four bars, then add C major in the next four. Write a melody that touches B at least twice and finishes on D. Keep the left hand simple so the colour of the mode stays clear.

FAQ

Q: Is Dorian a major or minor mode?

A: It is minor, because the third step is lowered, but its raised sixth makes it brighter than natural minor.

Q: Which white key starts the Dorian mode?

A: D. Playing only white keys from D to D gives D Dorian.

Q: How is Dorian different from melodic minor?

A: Ascending melodic minor raises both the sixth and the seventh steps. Dorian raises only the sixth, so its seventh step remains flat.

Q: What chord should I use to practise Dorian?

A: Alternate the tonic minor chord with the major chord built on the fourth step, such as D minor and G major.
