Beginner Boogie-Woogie Piano: Left-Hand Patterns and a 12-Bar Plan

Songs · 2026-10-31

Boogie-woogie is a blues piano style built on a left hand that never stops: eight bass notes in every bar, usually swung, repeated over the chords of a 12-bar blues. To start, learn one left-hand pattern on the C chord, move it to F and G, add a simple right-hand riff, and keep the groove steady before you think about speed.

This article is a practical starter kit. It gives you a short history, four left-hand patterns from easiest to hardest, a right-hand vocabulary, and a one-week plan for putting everything together.

Where boogie-woogie comes from

Boogie-woogie grew out of the blues in the American South in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, often linked to Texas and to pianists who played for working communities. It spread to cities such as Chicago, and in 1928 Pinetop Smith recorded "Pine Top's Boogie Woogie", one of the recordings that helped give the style its name.

The style reached a much larger audience after the "From Spirituals to Swing" concert at Carnegie Hall in New York in December 1938, where pianists Albert Ammons, Meade Lux Lewis and Pete Johnson performed. Its driving bass later fed into rhythm and blues and early rock and roll.

The 12-bar map you need first

Almost every boogie-woogie piece uses the 12-bar blues. In C, the chords run like this:

  • Bars 1–4: C, C, C, C
  • Bars 5–6: F, F
  • Bars 7–8: C, C
  • Bar 9: G
  • Bar 10: F
  • Bars 11–12: C, then C or G as a turnaround

Say the chord names aloud while tapping a steady beat before you play a note. If you know where the chord changes fall, your left hand will not panic when it has to jump.

Four left-hand patterns, easy to hard

Pattern A: root and fifth

Play C and G together, then C and A together, alternating on each beat. This "rocking" shape is gentle on the hand and already sounds like boogie.

Pattern B: the broken sixth

Play single notes C, E, G, A, B♭, A, G, E as eight even notes across the bar. This climbing and falling line is the classic sound most people imagine.

Pattern C: walking octaves

Play broken octaves that climb through the chord: low C to high C, then E, G, A and back. Each note is played as an octave split into two quick notes. It is powerful but tiring, so keep the wrist loose.

Pattern D: the shuffle with a blue note

Like Pattern B, but slide from E♭ to E on the way up. That small crush gives the bass a darker blues edge.

Learn each pattern on C first. Then move the same shape to F and G without changing the fingering. Most of the work is the jump at bar 5 and bar 9.

Swing: the long-short feel

Boogie-woogie is usually played with swung eighth notes. Each pair of eighths is split long-short, roughly like the first and third notes of a triplet. If you play the eight notes perfectly evenly, the pattern sounds mechanical. Try saying "dooo-ba, dooo-ba" while you play, and let the hand copy the voice.

Some players keep the left hand slightly straighter while the right hand swings harder. Listen to recordings and decide what you like, but stay consistent inside one piece.

A right-hand vocabulary

Keep the right hand simple at first. Your job is to sit on top of the bass, not compete with it.

  • Off-beat chords: strike a C7 shape on beats two and four, short and light, like a hand clap.
  • The crushed note: play E♭ and slide it to E with the same finger. This "crush" is the signature blues sound.
  • Repeated riffs: choose a two-bar riff and repeat it over the changing chords. Repetition is part of the style.
  • Tremolo: rock quickly between two notes of a chord, such as G and C, for a shimmering build.
  • Turnaround lick: in bar 12, walk down chromatically from C to G to lead back to the top.

A one-week practice plan

Day 1: tap the 12-bar form, then play Pattern A through the whole form, slowly.

Day 2: learn Pattern B on C only, until the little finger lands without looking.

Day 3: move Pattern B to F and G; practise only the jumps.

Day 4: add the right-hand off-beat chords, hands together at half speed.

Day 5: add one crushed-note riff in bars 1–4.

Day 6: record one full chorus and listen for rushing at the chord changes.

Day 7: play two choruses in a row, keeping the same tempo.

Ten to fifteen focused minutes a day is enough. When the left hand starts to feel automatic, you can raise the tempo a little at a time.

Why this style is worth learning

Boogie-woogie is one of the clearest workouts for hand independence. One hand must keep a pattern running while the other plays riffs and rhythms against it. That skill carries over to pop accompaniment, jazz comping and classical pieces with busy left hands. It is also a cheerful way to learn piano, which matters: a groove you enjoy is a groove you practise.

If you learn piano online, the left-hand loop is ideal for recording, because it is short and repetitive. An online piano lessons app that listens to your timing can show where the notes bunch up, usually just before a chord change, so you know exactly what to slow down.

Frequently asked questions

Q: Is boogie-woogie hard for beginners?

A: The simplest left-hand pattern is manageable for early players. Start slowly, learn one bar at a time and keep the tempo steady.

Q: Should I play boogie-woogie swung or straight?

A: Most boogie-woogie is swung. Some modern players use straighter eighths, but the swung long-short feel is the traditional sound.

Q: What is the difference between boogie-woogie and the blues?

A: Boogie-woogie is a piano style within the blues tradition. It usually uses the 12-bar blues form, with a repeating eight-to-the-bar left hand.

Q: My left hand gets tired. What should I do?

A: Lower the tempo, relax the wrist and choose Pattern A or B instead of octaves. Take short breaks and avoid forcing speed.

Q: Can an app help me with the left-hand groove?

A: Yes. An app that shows your timing note by note makes uneven spots visible, which helps you practise them on purpose.
