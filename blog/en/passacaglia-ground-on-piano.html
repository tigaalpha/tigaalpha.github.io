The Passacaglia Ground on Piano: A Repeating Bass Under Changing Lines

Music theory · 2026-11-12

A passacaglia on the piano is organised around a repeating bass, a ground that returns while the upper parts change. The ground may be a short melody in the bass as well as a harmonic cycle, and it is the thread the ear follows from variation to variation. If that thread becomes uneven, inaudible, or reshaped without a written reason, the form is harder to hear. The practical job is therefore different from simply learning a long string of pages: first the cycle must be secure, then each change of upper line must be practised without knocking the bass off its path. This is related to, but not the same as, practising a chaconne; here the repeating bass line itself is the object you keep in focus.

Learn the ground as a bass line you can close and restart

Extract the repeating bass, including any characteristic chromatic step or cadence, and play it until the close of the cycle leads naturally into the next start. Count its length, mark each cycle in the score, and sing or hum the bass if you can. A ground that exists only as a remembered pattern under thick figuration will fail in later, busier variations. If an early variation already ornaments the bass, learn the plain line first, then the ornamented form as a variant of the same cycle.

Add upper lines in small groups of cycles

Practise one variation, or a small group that shares a texture, with the bass always present. Do not spend so long on a right-hand figure alone that the ground is added later as a guess. When a variation is difficult, take the entire cycle under a slower pulse, bass included, instead of allowing the upper writing to set an unsteady speed. Join the last bar of one cycle to the first bar of the next, because that join is where the form either continues or breaks.

Keep the ground recognisable as later lines grow busier

Later variations may be fuller or quicker, but the bass should keep a familiar walking shape unless the written notes themselves change it. Voice the low notes on purpose, and be careful with pedal that can smear the steps of the ground into one wash. If an upper line is very active, thin its tone slightly so the cycle can still be followed.

  • Work the repeating bass by itself until finishing one cycle and starting the next feels smooth.
  • Mark the start of each cycle, or of each variation, in the copy.
  • Work the upper lines with the bass present, rather than treating them as isolated right-hand studies.
  • Slow the whole cycle when a variation is difficult, including the bass.
  • Voice the low notes so the ground remains audible as the texture grows.

Memory and stamina across many repeats of the same bass

Because the ground is short and often repeated many times, later cycles are easy to leave under-practised. Begin some sittings in the second half of the piece. For memory, name variations by texture rather than only by page number, and practise starting from those points. Endurance here means keeping cycles even, rather than saving everything for one late high point. The passacaglia then remains what it is: changing lines above a known walk.

FAQ

Q: How is this different from a chaconne?

A: Each form uses a repeating foundation. In this piece, keep the repeating bass line itself in focus, and stay with the notes on the stand rather than lingering over the historical name.

Q: What if the bass is transferred to an upper voice for a few cycles?

A: Treat that as a written change of the ground's register. The cycle should still be clear, now in its new place, then prepared for its return to the bass if that is what follows.

Q: Should every variation use the same tempo?

A: Follow the score. If none is marked, choose a pulse that can also carry the most elaborate variation, and keep early cycles from running ahead.

Q: Can I practise a hard upper line without the bass for a moment?

A: Yes, briefly, to solve a fingering. Return the ground at once; the variation has not been learned until it rests on that cycle.

Q: How do I keep so many similar cycles distinct?

A: Name them by texture, mark starts, and practise beginning from several points, including late variations.
