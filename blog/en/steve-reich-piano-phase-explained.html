Steve Reich's Piano Phase Explained for Curious Pianists

Songs · 2026-10-31

Piano Phase is a 1967 piece by the American composer Steve Reich for two pianos, or two marimbas, in which both players repeat the same short pattern of twelve notes. After a while, one pianist speeds up very slightly until the two parts slide out of alignment and then lock in again, one note apart. This gradual process continues until the pattern has cycled through every possible offset. The result is a shimmering, constantly changing texture created from almost no material, and it is a fascinating study in listening, pulse and concentration for any pianist.

This explainer covers where the idea came from, how the score is organised, what you hear as the parts drift, and a gentle drill that borrows the technique for your own practice.

From tape loops to two pianos

Reich discovered phasing in the mid-1960s while working with tape recorders. In pieces such as It's Gonna Rain, two copies of the same spoken recording played on machines running at fractionally different speeds. As the copies drifted apart, the words broke into echoes, rhythms and new melodic shapes that nobody had recorded directly.

The composer then wondered whether human performers could create the same effect. Piano Phase was one answer. Instead of machines, two musicians would play the loop, and one would control the drift by ear and feel. That decision turned a studio technique into a demanding piece of live chamber music.

How the score is organised

The core material is a single bar of twelve fast notes using only five pitches: E, F sharp, B, C sharp and D. The right hand plays one shape while the left hand plays another, and together they form a rocking figure that repeats.

The phasing process

  • Both pianists begin in unison, playing the pattern together.
  • The second pianist gradually and slightly increases speed.
  • After a short transition, the second part sits exactly one note ahead.
  • The players hold that new alignment for a number of repetitions.
  • The process repeats until the parts return to unison.

Later sections shorten the pattern to eight and then four notes, introducing new shapes while keeping the same principle.

What you hear as the patterns drift

When the parts are aligned, the music sounds like a single piano playing a busy pattern. During a transition, the texture blurs, and notes seem to double and smear. Once the new alignment settles, fresh melodies appear. Your ear picks out pairs of notes that happen to coincide, and these resulting patterns can sound like tunes nobody wrote.

Reich has described this as a musical process that the listener can follow from start to finish. There is no hidden structure. The pleasure lies in hearing small changes accumulate and noticing how your attention shifts from one emerging line to another.

A phasing drill for one pianist

You do not need a second piano or a concert-level technique to explore phasing. Try this slower version.

Prepare a short loop

Choose four notes in a comfortable position, for example E, F sharp, B and C sharp, and play them as even quavers. Repeat the loop until it feels automatic.

Record a steady track

Set your metronome to a slow tempo, record one minute of the loop, then switch the metronome off.

Play against the recording

Start in unison with the playback. After several repetitions, aim to place every note slightly early. Keep nudging until your part sits one note ahead of the recording.

Hold, then return

Stay in the new alignment for a few loops, listening for new patterns. Then ease back to unison.

Expect this to feel strange at first. Your instinct will be to snap back into line with the recording. Resisting that pull is exactly the skill the piece develops.

Skills Piano Phase builds

  • Inner pulse that does not depend on an external click.
  • Independent listening, hearing your part and another at once.
  • Endurance and relaxation during long stretches of repetition.
  • Sensitivity to tiny differences in timing, which improves evenness in all repertoire.

Pianists preparing Bach, Czerny studies or fast accompaniment figures often find that phasing practice makes their passagework more even, because it forces attention onto the exact placement of each note.

Listening suggestions

Before trying the drill, listen to a full performance of Piano Phase with the score or a visual guide. Then listen to Clapping Music, another Reich piece built on a similar shifting idea for two pairs of hands. Comparing the two shows how the same principle works with pitch and without it.

FAQ

Q: How long is Piano Phase?

A: Performances usually last around fifteen to twenty minutes, depending on how many repetitions the players choose.

Q: Is Piano Phase suitable for intermediate pianists?

A: The full piece is demanding, but the simplified drill above is accessible to anyone who can play a steady four-note pattern.

Q: Why does phasing feel tiring?

A: It asks you to resist the natural urge to synchronise. Short sessions of a few minutes are enough at first.

Q: Can children try phasing?

A: Yes, as a clapping game with a slow recorded beat. Keep it brief and playful.
