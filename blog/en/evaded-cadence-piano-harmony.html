Evaded Cadences on Piano: Delaying a Close So the Phrase Can Continue

Music theory · 2026-11-11

On the piano, an evaded cadence is prepared as if a close were coming, then avoided by a small change in the final chord, the bass, or the melody, so the phrase can continue. Unlike a full stop on the tonic, and unlike a deceptive landing that often sits on vi as a new place of rest, an evaded close feels like a cadence that was started and then sidestepped. The music usually remains in motion, often by turning the would-be tonic into an inversion, a seventh chord, or a new dominant.

How the dodge is prepared

The bars before the evasion should still look and sound like an approach to a close: a pre-dominant, a dominant, and a melody that could have ended. If those habits are missing, the later sidestep will not read as an evaded cadence. It will simply be the next event in a busy phrase.

Play a normal perfect cadence in C major. Then play the same approach, but let the final C chord arrive in first inversion, or with an added B-flat that turns it into a C7 heading toward F. The ear feels the almost-ending, then the new door. That is the useful contrast to practise first, before rarer textbook examples.

A short test at the keyboard:

  • Approach a close in the usual way, with a clear dominant.
  • On the landing, change one element: bass, inversion, or an added seventh.
  • Continue the phrase for at least two more bars.
  • Compare that version with a true tonic close in the same spot.

How this differs from a deceptive cadence

A deceptive cadence usually lands on a different root, often vi, and can feel like a full, if unexpected, place to breathe. An evaded cadence is more like a cadence that missed its footing and kept walking. The tonic may even appear, but not in a form that can end the phrase: too unstable a bass, a seventh that demands another move, or a melody that refuses to settle.

The labels overlap in some books, and arguing about them is less useful than hearing the difference. If the landing feels like a new quiet chord, you may be in deceptive territory. If it feels like the close was snatched away and the line must continue, evaded is the better everyday word.

A few practical shapes on the keys

Name the element that did the evading

One common shape is a dominant that resolves to a tonic with the third in the bass, then moves on. Another is a dominant that resolves to a tonic seventh, which immediately becomes a new dominant to a later key or to a later phrase in the same key. A third is a melody that leaps away from the expected tonic pitch at the last moment, while the accompaniment almost cadences underneath.

Practise each shape in one key only at first. Name aloud which element did the evading: bass, inner chord, or melody. Then recast the same shape in a neighbouring key, reading the accidentals.

Letting the phrase continue after the dodge

The bars after an evaded cadence should justify the delay. A short sequence, a repeated motif, or a new approach to a later, real close will do. If nothing follows but a sudden stop, the evasion will feel accidental. Write or improvise two extra bars, then a firm cadence, so the story has a second chance to end.

Marks in a score that often hint at an evasion

Look for a cadence formula whose final bass is not the tonic in root position, or for a tonic chord that carries a seventh on a strong beat where an ending might have occurred. A slur or a phrase mark that continues through the would-be close is another clue. None of these marks proves the label on its own, so confirm by playing the arrival and asking whether a root-position tonic would have ended the sentence.

FAQ

Q: Is every inverted tonic at a cadence evaded?

A: Not automatically. Some phrases simply end on an inversion for colour. The evaded type keeps the music moving and usually needs a later, firmer close.

Q: How is this different from a deceptive cadence?

A: A deceptive cadence often rests on vi or a similar chord. An evaded cadence usually feels like a close that was started and then pulled aside so the phrase can continue.

Q: Should the dominant still be clear?

A: Yes. If the approach is vague, the later dodge is hard to hear. Keep the dominant honest, then change the landing.

Q: Can a melody evade the cadence on its own?

A: Yes, if it refuses the expected tonic pitch while the rest of the texture almost closes. The continuation should still make sense.

Q: How do I practise this without getting lost?

A: Practise a normal close, then one changed landing, then two bars of continuation and a final close. Keep that whole story in one key until it is easy.
