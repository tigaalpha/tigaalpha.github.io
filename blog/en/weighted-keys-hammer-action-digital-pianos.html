Weighted Keys Explained: Hammer Action on Digital Pianos

News · 2026-11-04

Weighted keys are keys on a digital piano that resist the finger in a way meant to resemble an acoustic piano. Fully weighted instruments usually use a small hammer mechanism inside each key, which is why they are often called hammer action. Graded hammer action makes low keys a little heavier and high keys a little lighter, as on an acoustic grand. Semi-weighted keys use springs with some added weight, and unweighted keys feel like a light keyboard. For learners who plan to play acoustic pianos, a fully weighted, graded action makes the move between instruments easier.

Why acoustic keys feel heavy

On an acoustic piano, each key is a lever. When you press it, it throws a felt hammer up against the strings. The hammer has mass, and the low strings use larger hammers than the high strings. That is why the bass feels heavier and why players learn to control speed and weight together. The feel of an acoustic key is a result of its mechanism, not an added feature.

How digital pianos imitate it

Digital pianos do not need hammers to make sound; sensors measure how fast the key moves and the instrument chooses a sample or model at a matching volume. To make the touch familiar, makers add mechanisms that copy the resistance.

  • Hammer action: a small weighted arm inside each key that moves when you play.
  • Graded hammer action: hammers that are heavier in the bass and lighter in the treble.
  • Semi-weighted: springs plus some weight, common on controllers and portable keyboards.
  • Synth action: light, spring-loaded keys without weight, used mostly for synthesiser playing.

Some instruments also include extra sensors that help detect fast repeated notes, and some use wooden keys or longer key pivots to feel closer to a grand.

What "touch sensitive" means

Touch sensitivity is about sound, not weight. A touch-sensitive keyboard plays louder when you press faster. A keyboard can be touch sensitive but still unweighted.

Does key feel matter for learners?

For the first weeks, any keyboard can teach note names and simple rhythms. Over time, key weight starts to matter. Players who practise only on light keys can find acoustic pianos tiring, and their dynamics may jump suddenly from soft to loud. Practising on a weighted action builds finger control and helps soft playing feel natural.

Testing in a shop

If you can, play slow scales at a very soft level, then repeat notes quickly. Notice whether the soft notes speak evenly and whether repeated notes feel controllable. Try the same passage on an acoustic piano if one is available nearby.

Caring for a digital action

Digital actions need less care than acoustic ones, but some habits help: keep the instrument away from direct sun and damp areas, cover it to keep dust out, and avoid placing drinks on top. If certain keys start to click loudly or feel uneven, a service technician can usually inspect the action.

Moving between instruments

Even with a good action, every piano feels slightly different. When you visit a new instrument, play a slow warm-up for a minute or two to learn its weight and response before attempting your pieces. This habit is useful for exams, concerts and lessons in a new room.

Choosing for a home and a budget

Before buying, think about space, volume and how the instrument will be used. A compact model with a full 88-key weighted action suits most learners who practise at home with headphones. If several family members will play, check that the bench height and pedal unit work for everyone. Look at the number of pedals, the headphone outputs and whether the instrument can connect to a tablet for learning apps. Read the manual for the touch settings too: many digital pianos let you choose a lighter or heavier response curve, which can help a young child or an adult with a strong touch find a comfortable level.

FAQ

Q: Is a weighted keyboard necessary for beginners?

A: Not on day one, but a weighted action is helpful for anyone planning to continue with classical or acoustic piano repertoire.

Q: What is the difference between weighted and graded?

A: Weighted means the keys have added resistance. Graded means that resistance changes from heavier bass to lighter treble.

Q: Can I learn on a semi-weighted controller?

A: Yes, especially for pop and production work, but expect an adjustment period on acoustic pianos.

Q: Does heavier always mean better?

A: No. A good action should feel controllable and even across the range, not just heavy.
