Tango Rhythm on Piano: Marcato, Síncopa and the 3-3-2 Pulse

Music theory · 2026-10-31

Tango piano rests on three rhythmic ideas: marcato, a firm chord on each of the four beats; síncopa, an accent pushed just ahead of the beat; and the 3-3-2 grouping of eight eighth notes. Learn to play each one cleanly in the left hand, then combine them, and almost any tango arrangement becomes readable instead of mysterious.

Tango grew up in the late nineteenth century around the Río de la Plata, in Buenos Aires and Montevideo. In the classic dance orchestras, the piano was a rhythm instrument as much as a melodic one, sharing the job of driving the dancers with the double bass. That is why a pianist who wants to sound like tango has to think like a drummer first. This article breaks the style into small rhythmic tools you can practise one at a time.

The three rhythm tools in brief

  • Marcato in four: four short, equal, accented chords per bar. It is the walking heartbeat of many tangos.
  • Marcato in two: accents on beats one and three only, giving a lighter, more relaxed feel.
  • Síncopa: an accent placed on the last eighth note before a strong beat, then tied or released, so the music seems to lean forward.
  • 3-3-2: eight eighth notes grouped as three, three and two, with accents on the first note of each group.
  • Arrastre: a quick "drag" into a beat, often a slide or grace-like run in the bass, which adds a dramatic pull.

Marcato: the steady stamp

Start with a single chord, such as A minor in close position in the left hand. Play it on every beat of a 4/4 bar at a moderate tempo. Each chord should be short and firm, with the hand lifting slightly between beats.

Common mistake: chords that ring too long

If you hold every chord, marcato turns into a wash. Release each one so there is a small gap before the next. The silence between chords is what makes the rhythm sound crisp.

Variation: accent beats one and three

Keep four chords, but make beats one and three a little stronger. This creates the swaying "two" feel heard in many older recordings.

Síncopa: leaning into the next beat

Count eighth notes out loud, using "one-and, two-and, three-and, four-and". In a basic síncopa figure, you play an accent on the "and" of beat 2 and hold it into beat 3, then do the same on the "and" of beat 4 into the next bar's beat 1. The chord arrives early, and the ear feels the music tipping forward.

Practise this slowly with a metronome clicking on every beat. Say the counts aloud, and make the early accent clearly louder than the surrounding notes. When it feels natural, alternate one bar of marcato with one bar of síncopa. That contrast is at the heart of the style.

The 3-3-2 pattern

Group eight eighth notes as "1-2-3, 1-2-3, 1-2". Accent the first of each group. Look closely and you will see that the three accents sit on the downbeat, halfway through the second beat, and on the fourth beat. The pattern appears in tango and in many other styles, and the later tango composer Astor Piazzolla used it widely.

How to learn it

  • Tap the pattern on the closed fallboard with both hands before you play any notes.
  • Play only the three accented notes in the left hand, as single bass notes.
  • Fill in the unaccented eighth notes softly in the right hand.
  • Finally move the whole pattern into chords, keeping the accent shape clear.

Combining the tools into a short exercise

Use a simple progression: A minor for two bars, E7 for two bars, A minor for two bars, E7 for one bar, then A minor. Play it four times, changing the rhythm tool each time:

  • First time: marcato in four.
  • Second time: marcato in two.
  • Third time: síncopa on every bar.
  • Fourth time: 3-3-2 throughout.

Record the four passes. When you listen back, ask whether each version sounds clearly different. If they blur together, the accents are not strong enough or the releases are not short enough.

Touch and expression

Tango is often described as dramatic, but the drama comes from contrast rather than constant loudness. Keep your wrist flexible so short chords do not tire your arm. Let some phrases fade to almost nothing before a sudden accent. Use the sustain pedal sparingly, mostly in lyrical passages, because too much pedal destroys the crisp marcato.

Frequently asked questions

Q: Do I need to read music to play tango rhythms?

A: Reading helps, but these patterns can be learned by counting and tapping. Many players learn the rhythm first and the notation later.

Q: Which tool should I learn first?

A: Start with marcato, because it trains short, even chords. Síncopa and 3-3-2 are much easier once marcato is steady.

Q: Is tango too advanced for adult beginners?

A: Not if you begin with one chord and one rhythm at a time. Adults who learn piano later often enjoy tango because the patterns are clear and satisfying.

Q: Can I learn piano online and still get the tango feel?

A: Yes. Listen to recordings, slow them down and play along. An online piano lessons app helps you keep time while you build accuracy.
