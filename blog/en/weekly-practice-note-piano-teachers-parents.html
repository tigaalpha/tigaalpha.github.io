Weekly Practice Notes: How Piano Teachers and Parents Stay in Sync

Stories · 2026-11-02

A weekly practice note is a short written message from a piano teacher to a student and their family after each lesson, explaining what was covered, what to practise and how to practise it. It connects the lesson to the six days of home practice that follow. For parents without musical training, the note answers the common question "What should my child be doing at the piano this week?" For the teacher, it creates a record of goals and progress that makes the next lesson more focused.

Notes do not need to be long. Three or four clear points are usually more helpful than a full page.

What to include in a practice note

  • The pieces or exercises to practise, with bar numbers.
  • One specific goal for each, such as "hands together at a slow tempo".
  • How to practise, for example "left hand alone three times, then hands together".
  • One encouraging comment about something that went well in the lesson.
  • Any reminders, such as bringing a book or a change in lesson time.

Write in plain language. Instead of "work on articulation in the B section", try "make the notes in bars 9–12 short and bouncy".

Formats that families actually read

Some teachers write in a paper notebook that travels with the student. Others use a shared document, an email or a messaging app chosen by the family. The right format is simply the one families check regularly, so ask rather than assume. Ask parents at the start of the year which method suits them, and keep the same format so notes are easy to find.

Keep personal information minimal

Use first names only, avoid sharing other students' details, and agree on how messages will be stored. Respecting privacy builds trust.

How parents can use the note

Parents can read the note with their child after the lesson and again before practice. They do not need to judge the playing; they can simply ask, "Which goal are you working on today?" and help the child keep track of completed steps. If the note says "three times correctly", parents can count with the child. If something is unclear, a short question to the teacher is always better than guessing.

When practice does not go to plan

If the week was busy or the child struggled, parents can add a line to the note, such as "Bars 5–8 were very hard this week". This helps the teacher adjust the next lesson instead of assuming no practice happened.

Writing notes quickly as a teacher

Writing a note takes time, especially with many students. A few habits keep it efficient:

  • Write during the last two minutes of the lesson, with the student watching.
  • Use a simple template with the same headings every week.
  • Let older students write the note themselves while the teacher checks it.
  • Keep a small bank of phrases for common goals.

Involving the student in writing the note also improves understanding, since they must put the goals into their own words.

Tone matters

Notes should be encouraging and specific. Start with something positive, then give clear goals. Avoid comments that sound like criticism of the child or the family. If there are concerns, such as repeated lack of practice, discuss them in a calm conversation rather than in writing.

Building independence over time

For young children, parents may read the note together every day. As students grow, they gradually take responsibility for reading it and planning their own practice. Eventually, teenagers and adults may write their own weekly plans, with the teacher only reviewing them. The weekly note thus becomes a tool for teaching self-directed learning, a skill that serves music students long after their first piano lessons end.

FAQ

Q: How long should a weekly practice note be?

A: Usually a few lines: pieces, goals, practice method and one positive comment.

Q: Should the note be paper or digital?

A: Either works. Choose the format the family checks most reliably.

Q: What if parents do not read music?

A: Write in plain language and use bar numbers and simple instructions anyone can follow.

Q: Can students write their own notes?

A: Yes. Older students benefit from writing goals in their own words, with the teacher checking.
