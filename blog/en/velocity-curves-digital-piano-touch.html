Velocity Curves on Digital Pianos: Matching the Keys to Your Touch

News · 2026-11-11

A velocity curve is the setting on a digital piano or MIDI keyboard that translates how fast you press a key into how loud and bright the resulting note sounds. Because every player's touch differs, and because keyboards vary in weight and sensors, most instruments let you choose between several curves, often labelled light, normal and heavy, or allow a custom shape. Choosing the right curve can make practice more comfortable and help your playing transfer smoothly to acoustic pianos.

How a digital key measures touch

Most digital keyboards have two or three sensors under each key. When you press, the key passes one sensor and then the next. The time between them indicates how fast the key was moving, which the instrument converts into a velocity number, usually from one to one hundred and twenty-seven in the MIDI standard.

The sound engine then uses that number to select a louder or softer sample, or to adjust a modelled sound, so faster key movement produces a fuller, brighter tone, much like a hammer striking a string harder on an acoustic piano.

What the curve actually changes

The velocity curve is a mapping between how fast you press and the velocity number sent to the sound engine.

  • A linear curve maps key speed evenly, so each extra bit of force adds a similar amount of volume.
  • A light curve gives higher numbers for gentler presses, making loud sounds easier to reach.
  • A heavy curve requires faster key movement for the same volume, giving more control over soft dynamics.
  • A fixed setting sends the same velocity for every note, which can be useful for some organ or synth sounds but removes expression.

The physical weight of the keys does not change; only the response of the sound does.

Signs your curve may not suit you

Several clues suggest the current setting does not match your playing.

Everything sounds loud

If gentle playing still produces a strong sound, the curve may be too light, and you will struggle to practise quiet passages.

Soft notes disappear

If quiet notes barely sound or drop out entirely, the curve may be too heavy for your touch.

Tiredness after practice

If you find yourself pressing much harder than you would on an acoustic piano to get a full sound, adjust the curve rather than forcing the keys.

Choosing a curve step by step

A short test helps you find a sensible setting.

  • Play a single note repeatedly, from as soft as you can control to as loud as feels natural.
  • Listen for a smooth increase in volume and tone with no sudden jumps.
  • Try the same exercise on each curve setting, then play a short piece with both quiet and loud sections.
  • Choose the curve where your natural range of touch produces the full range of sound.

If you also play an acoustic piano, try to match the feel of that instrument, so your touch habits transfer in both directions.

Why it matters for learning

Dynamics are a central part of musical expression, and students need to hear the results of their touch accurately. A poorly matched curve can teach habits that do not work on other instruments, such as hitting keys too hard. Teachers often check a student's digital piano settings early on for this reason.

  • Too light: students learn to play with very little weight.
  • Too heavy: students learn to force the keys.
  • Well matched: touch habits carry over to acoustic instruments.

Apps that listen to playing are also affected. When a digital piano connects directly to a learning app, the velocity numbers travel with each note. Beyond simple curves

Some instruments and software offer detailed velocity editing, letting users adjust specific parts of the curve or apply different settings to different key ranges. Musicians who record with virtual instruments sometimes fine-tune curves for each sound library, because sample sets respond differently. For most learners, though, a few minutes spent choosing among the standard presets is enough.

FAQ

Q: Does a velocity curve change how heavy the keys feel?

A: No. It changes how the sound responds, not the physical resistance of the keys.

Q: Which curve should beginners use?

A: Usually the normal or medium setting, adjusted if soft or loud playing feels difficult to control.

Q: Why do my recordings sound different from what I hear while playing?

A: Recording software may apply its own velocity response or volume processing. Check both the instrument and the software settings.

Q: Should I change the curve for different pieces?

A: Most players keep one setting for consistency, but some adjust for different sounds or rooms.
