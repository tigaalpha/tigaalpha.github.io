Markov-Chain Melodies: How Simple Probability Writes Tunes

News · 2026-11-11

One of the earliest computer methods for generating melodies is also one of the simplest. A Markov chain is a model that chooses the next event based only on the current event, or on a few recent ones, using probabilities learned from examples. In music, it can be trained on a set of melodies and then asked to produce new ones that sound similar in their note-to-note movement. Understanding Markov chains helps learners see how pattern-based generation works and why more advanced systems were developed.

Counting what comes next

Imagine analysing a collection of simple folk tunes. For each note, you count which notes follow it. You might find that after C, the next note is D forty percent of the time in this collection, E thirty percent, G twenty percent and C again ten percent. These counts become a table of probabilities, known as a transition table.

To generate a new melody:

  • start on a chosen note, such as C
  • look up the probabilities for what follows C
  • choose the next note randomly according to those probabilities
  • repeat from the new note

The result is a sequence that reflects the step patterns of the training melodies.

Orders of memory

A first-order Markov chain looks only at the current note. A second-order chain looks at the last two notes, a third-order chain at the last three, and so on. Higher orders capture longer patterns, producing melodies that sound more like the training examples. But they also need more data, and if the order is too high, the model may simply copy long passages from the originals rather than creating something new.

Rhythm and other features

Early experiments often generated pitch and rhythm separately, or combined them as pairs. More detailed models include note duration, position within the bar or even chord context. Each added feature makes the output more musical, but also increases the amount of data needed.

Strengths of Markov melodies

Local smoothness

Because each note follows patterns from real music, short fragments often sound natural.

Style imitation

A model trained on one style, such as simple folk songs, produces melodies with similar intervals and contours.

Transparency

The transition table can be inspected and explained, making the model easy to understand and teach.

Weaknesses

The main weakness is lack of long-term structure. A Markov chain has no idea of phrases, repetition or a destination. Its melodies tend to wander, rarely returning to a theme or building to a climax. They may also end at random rather than on a satisfying cadence. Human music relies heavily on repetition and development across many bars, which a short memory cannot capture.

How later systems addressed the problem

Researchers tried many solutions: adding rules for phrase endings, combining Markov models with grammars, or constraining generation to fit a chosen harmony. Later, neural networks with longer memory and attention mechanisms were developed to capture structure across larger spans. Even so, Markov chains remain useful as teaching tools and as building blocks in some interactive music software.

A classroom experiment

Teachers can demonstrate the idea without a computer. Take a short melody that students know and count note-to-note transitions on a whiteboard. Then use numbered cards drawn from a bag to generate a new melody from the table. Play it on the piano and discuss: does it sound like the original style? Where does it lose direction? This activity connects probability, listening and composition.

Why this matters for learners

Understanding how simple models generate music helps students approach AI tools critically. They can recognise that a generated melody may be locally convincing but structurally weak, and that composers still shape form, repetition and meaning. This knowledge also helps learners use generative tools creatively, as a source of ideas rather than a finished product.

FAQ

Q: What is a Markov chain in music?

A: A model that chooses each next note based on probabilities learned from how notes follow each other in examples.

Q: Why do Markov melodies wander?

A: They remember only a few recent notes, so they lack phrase structure and a sense of direction.

Q: What does a higher order mean?

A: The model considers more previous notes, capturing longer patterns but needing more data.

Q: Are Markov chains still used?

A: Yes, especially as teaching tools and in some interactive or rule-based music systems.
