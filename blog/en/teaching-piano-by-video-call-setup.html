Teaching Piano by Video Call: Camera, Sound and Lesson Flow

Practice tips · 2026-11-02

Teaching piano by video call means giving live lessons through a video platform, with teacher and student at their own instruments in different places. It works well when three things are in place: a camera angle that shows the hands and keyboard clearly, audio settings that let the piano sound natural, and a lesson flow that accepts the small delay of internet connections. With these basics, online lessons can cover reading, technique, repertoire and musicianship effectively, and they save travel time for families.

Teachers who move online often discover that their in-person habits, such as playing along with a student, need adjusting. A little preparation makes lessons smooth.

Camera angles that work

Side view

A camera placed to the side at about shoulder height shows hands, wrists and posture. This is useful for technique.

Overhead view

A camera above the keyboard shows fingering and hand positions clearly. A phone on a simple stand can provide this angle.

Two cameras

Some teachers use a laptop camera for the face and a phone for the hands. Students can do the same if a second device is available.

Make sure lighting comes from the front or side, not from a bright window behind the player.

Sound settings for piano

Many video platforms reduce background noise and adjust volume automatically, which can make the piano sound thin or cut off soft notes. Look for settings that preserve original sound or turn off noise suppression. Place the microphone a little away from the instrument to capture a balanced tone. Wired internet or a strong signal reduces dropouts.

  • Test sound with a slow scale and a loud chord before the first lesson.
  • Ask students to mute other devices in the room.
  • Use headphones on the teacher side to avoid echo.

Planning around delay

Internet delay means teacher and student cannot play together in strict time. Instead of duets, use turn-taking: the teacher demonstrates, then the student plays. For rhythm work, the student can play with a metronome or backing track at their end while the teacher listens. Keep instructions short and clear, and use bar numbers so both sides know the exact place in the music.

Shared materials

Send scores before the lesson as files both can open. Annotating a shared screen or sending a photo of marked music helps students remember feedback.

A sample online lesson flow

  • Greeting and quick sound check (2 minutes).
  • Warm-up: scales or technique shown from the overhead camera (5 minutes).
  • Repertoire: student plays, teacher gives focused feedback, student tries again (20 minutes).
  • Reading or theory activity using the shared screen (5 minutes).
  • Summary of practice goals, written in the chat or a shared notebook (3 minutes).

Teaching young children online

Children need more variety and movement online. Short activities, games and visual aids keep attention. A parent nearby can help with camera position and page turning, especially in early lessons. Clear routines, such as the same greeting and closing song, make lessons feel safe. Keep screen time reasonable by shortening lessons for very young students.

Recordings and privacy

Some teachers record lessons so students can review them. Always ask permission first, explain how recordings will be stored, and delete them when no longer needed. Students can also send short practice recordings between lessons, giving teachers a clearer view of progress and saving lesson time.

When the connection fails

Prepare a backup plan before it is needed. Agree that if the call drops, the student continues practising the current passage while the teacher reconnects. Keep a phone number or chat channel for quick messages, and have a short offline task ready, such as recording a piece and sending it after the lesson. Students then know that a technical problem does not mean a lost lesson, and teachers stay calm rather than rushed.

FAQ

Q: Can students and teachers play duets online?

A: Real-time duets are difficult because of delay. Turn-taking or playing with a local track works better.

Q: What equipment does a student need?

A: A piano or keyboard, a device with a camera and microphone, and a stable internet connection.

Q: Are online lessons suitable for beginners?

A: Yes, especially with a clear camera view and a parent helping young children.

Q: How can I make sure the piano sounds good?

A: Turn off automatic noise reduction if possible and place the microphone at a balanced distance.
