First-Week Orientation for New Piano Learners and Families

News · 2026-11-12

The first week of piano lessons is often crowded: a new room, a new bench, a new teacher, and a first piece of homework that may not yet have a place in the family week. A short orientation, given on the first visit or in a brief welcome note plus a walk-through, can keep that week musical rather than confusing. The aim is not a long lecture. It is to show where the learner sits, how practice is meant to happen at home, and how scores, questions, and any recordings move between the lesson and the household.

Walk through the teaching room and the sitting place

Show the learner where to put a bag, where the waiting seat is, and how to approach the piano without rushing the bench. Check height and distance, and show how to leave the fallboard or cover at the end if that is the studio rule. A child may also need to know who will wait, and where a parent sits if they stay. These are small facts, but they prevent a first lesson from starting with a hunt for a chair.

Leave enough of the first visit for playing, not only for information

Explain the first practice plan in writing

Send home a short, written first assignment: which bars, how often, and what "done" looks like for this week. Say whether a book stays at home, travels, or exists in two copies. If a click, a notebook, or a particular pencil mark is expected, show an example in the lesson. Families should leave with something they can put on a fridge or in a diary, not only with a memory of spoken instructions.

  • Show the room, the waiting place, and how to sit at the piano.
  • Send home a short written first assignment, including which scores travel.
  • Explain how questions, absences, and any recordings should be sent.
  • Demonstrate any tool the studio expects, using a real phrase.
  • Keep enough of the first visit for playing, not only for information.

Show how questions and takes travel back to the teacher

Tell families whether to write a question in the notebook, send a message, or bring it next week. If the studio uses practice recordings, explain the naming pattern and who may hear the takes. A first-week demo of a short take in TIGA ALPHA, when the studio uses it, can be part of this explanation, so the tool is attached to a real phrase rather than to a technical tour.

Also say how to report a missed lesson, and how to arrive if the learner is late, so the family does not have to guess the studio's habit.

Leave time in the first lesson for playing, not only for talking

Orientation should be short enough that the learner still plays. A first sound at this piano, even a simple pattern, belongs in the first visit. Teachers can split the welcome: a five-minute room walk, a written plan, then work at the keys, with any remaining household questions at the end or in a note. The first week then has a musical centre, which is why the family enrolled.

If two members of the family attend the first visit, agree before leaving who will sit with the learner at home for the first assignment, so the written plan is not left on a table with no one assigned to open the book.

FAQ

Q: Should a parent stay in the room for the first lesson?

A: Follow the studio's usual rule, and say it in advance. Some first visits include a parent for the orientation and then a short time at the keys with the learner.

Q: What if the family already knows how to practise?

A: Still give the written first assignment and the studio's own habits for scores and messages. Familiar skills still need this room's routine.

Q: How long should orientation take?

A: Short enough that playing still happens. A written note can carry details that do not need to be said in full at the bench.

Q: Do we need a separate orientation day?

A: Not always. A planned first lesson can include the walk-through. A group welcome can help if many learners start in the same week.

Q: What should we bring on the first day?

A: The items the studio listed: a score if one was named, a notebook, and any form that is still outstanding. Ask in advance rather than arriving with a large bag of possibilities.
