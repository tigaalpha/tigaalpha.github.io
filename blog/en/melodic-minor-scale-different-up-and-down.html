The Melodic Minor Scale: Different Going Up and Down

Music theory · 2026-11-22

The melodic minor scale is a minor scale that changes shape depending on direction. Going up, the sixth and seventh notes are raised by a semitone; coming down, those notes return to their natural minor forms. The change exists to make the melody sound smooth, with no awkward gap near the top of the scale.

Starting from natural minor

Take the natural minor scale first. In A minor it uses only the white keys from A to A. The seventh note, G, sits a whole tone below the top A, so the step up to the tonic feels rather soft and open.

Many composers wanted a stronger pull toward the tonic. Raising the seventh note, to G sharp, creates that pull. The trouble is that raising only the seventh leaves a larger gap between F and G sharp in the harmonic minor scale, which can sound a little angular in a melody.

How the melodic form solves the gap

The melodic minor scale raises both the sixth and the seventh notes on the way up. In A minor that means F sharp and G sharp. The result is a line that moves almost entirely by small steps and arrives at the top with a clear lift.

On the way down, the music no longer needs the pull toward the tonic, so both notes return to natural: G and F. The descent follows the natural minor scale, which feels relaxed and settled.

The shape in numbers of steps

Reading the scale as a pattern of whole tones and semitones helps memory. Going up it begins like a minor scale for the first four notes, then continues like a major scale for the last four. Players sometimes describe it as a minor beginning with a major ending.

Practising it at the piano

Step by step

Begin with A minor, because the white keys are familiar. Play the natural minor scale slowly, one hand, to hear its character. Then play the same scale with F sharp and G sharp on the way up, and return with natural notes on the way down.

Say the direction aloud as you play: "raise on the way up, release on the way down." Many learners forget one of the two changes, and speaking helps the fingers remember.

When one hand is secure, add the other hand in octaves, slowly. After that, try other keys, one at a time, writing down the raised notes for each key beforehand.

Hearing it in real music

You can hear melodic minor in many melodies that rise to a high note and then fall softly. Because the raised notes only appear when the line goes up, a tune can feel hopeful in the ascent and reflective in the descent. Listening for that colour can be as useful as memorising the scale.

If a piece has accidentals that appear going up and disappear coming down, there is a good chance it uses melodic minor. A teacher can help you decide, bar by bar, which form fits.

A daily routine

Keep it brief

  • One key per day.
  • Two rounds, hands separate.
  • One round, hands together.

Spend a few minutes a day on one key. Play the scale up and down twice, hands separately, then hands together at a gentle speed. A piano teaching app or a simple recording on a phone can help you check that the raised notes are in the right place. Stop when your hands are still relaxed.

The melodic minor scale is not difficult once the pattern is clear: raise on the way up, release on the way down, and let the ear confirm it.

FAQ

Q: Why does the melodic minor scale change when going down?

A: Going up, the raised notes lead to the tonic. Coming down, that pull is not needed, so the notes return to natural minor.

Q: Is melodic minor harder than natural minor?

A: It has two extra changes to remember, but they follow a clear rule: raise them going up and release them going down.

Q: Which key should I try first?

A: A minor, because the natural form uses only white keys and the two raised notes are easy to find.

Q: Do I have to memorise every key?

A: Not at first. Learn one or two keys well, then add more when the idea feels natural.
