Isorhythm: The Medieval Trick of Repeating Patterns

Music theory · 2026-11-08

Isorhythm is a composition technique from medieval Europe in which a repeating rhythm pattern is combined with a repeating pitch pattern of a different length. Because the two patterns do not line up, each time the pitches return they meet a new rhythm. Composers of the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries used this to build long, structured pieces from small units. The idea is still useful for learners who want to understand how patterns create variety.

Two patterns with two names

Medieval theorists gave the two layers names. The talea is the repeating rhythm pattern. The color is the repeating series of pitches. A composer might choose a talea of five rhythmic values and a color of seven pitches. When they run together, the pitches and rhythms gradually shift against each other.

How long until they realign?

With five rhythms and seven pitches, the two patterns only start together again after thirty-five notes, because thirty-five is the first number that both five and seven divide into evenly. This is simple arithmetic, but it produces a long passage that never sounds exactly the same.

Where isorhythm appeared

Isorhythm was used mostly in motets, which were sacred and secular vocal works with several lines of text. Composers such as Philippe de Vitry and Guillaume de Machaut are often named in discussions of the technique. Usually, the lowest voice, called the tenor, carried the isorhythmic pattern, while the upper voices sang freer lines above it.

In later works, composers sometimes applied isorhythm to upper voices as well, creating a dense web of patterns. Listeners would not necessarily hear the scheme consciously, but it gave the music a strong sense of order.

Hearing the effect

When you listen to an isorhythmic motet, try to focus on the lowest voice. You may notice long notes that move in a regular but not obvious rhythm. The repetition is easier to see in a score than to hear, yet the result feels calm and architectural.

Modern relatives of the idea

The same principle of patterns with different lengths appears in many later styles. Minimalist composers layer loops of different lengths. Some electronic music uses step sequences where a melody pattern of one length runs over a drum pattern of another length. Drummers sometimes play a repeating figure that crosses the bar line until it comes back around.

  • Medieval isorhythm uses talea and color in long vocal works.
  • Minimalism layers short loops that drift in and out of alignment.
  • Electronic sequencers run patterns of uneven lengths.
  • Many folk traditions repeat melodic cycles over steady rhythms.

Try it at the piano

You can explore isorhythm with a very simple exercise.

  • Choose a rhythm of three values, such as long, short, short.
  • Choose a pitch series of four notes, such as C, E, G, A.
  • Play the pitches in order while repeating the rhythm pattern.
  • Keep going until the first pitch and the first rhythm meet again.

You will find that it takes twelve notes, because twelve is the first number divisible by both three and four. Listen to how each pitch appears with different note lengths along the way. Then try other combinations, like a five-note rhythm with a three-note pitch series.

Why the technique still matters

It also shows that early music was not simple or primitive. Composers of the period worked with careful plans, numbers and proportions, much like an architect designing a building. Recognising this planning can change how you approach older repertoire. Instead of hearing slow, unfamiliar sounds, you start to listen for the hidden framework that holds the piece together, and you notice how calm repetition can still produce constant small surprises over a long span of time.

Isorhythm teaches a useful lesson about structure. Small materials, combined thoughtfully, can generate long and varied music. For learners, it encourages careful counting, close listening and a new respect for the planning behind early music.

FAQ

Q: Is isorhythm the same as a repeated rhythm?

A: Not exactly. It combines a repeated rhythm with a repeated pitch series of a different length.

Q: Which voice usually carries the isorhythm?

A: In many motets, the tenor, which is the lowest structural voice.

Q: Can you hear isorhythm easily?

A: It is often clearer in a score, though careful listening to the lowest line helps.

Q: Is the technique used today?

A: The idea of overlapping loops appears in minimalism and electronic sequencing.
