The Whole-Tone Scale on Piano: Six Notes, No Clear Home

Music theory · 2026-11-11

The whole-tone scale is a six-note scale in which every step is a whole tone, two semitones apart. Starting on C, it runs C, D, E, F-sharp, G-sharp, A-sharp and back to C. Because all the steps are equal, no note feels like a natural resting point, so the scale sounds floating, dreamy or mysterious. It is closely linked with early twentieth-century music, especially French piano pieces, and with film and cartoon moments that suggest dreams or magic.

Only two whole-tone scales exist

Since the scale divides the octave into six equal parts, starting it on D gives the same set of notes as starting on C. In fact, there are only two different whole-tone collections.

  • Collection one: C, D, E, F-sharp, G-sharp, A-sharp.
  • Collection two: C-sharp, D-sharp, F, G, A, B.
  • Together the two collections contain all twelve notes, with no overlap.

That symmetry is unusual. Most scales sound different when started from a different note, but a whole-tone scale is the same pattern from any of its members.

Why it sounds unresolved

Traditional harmony depends on unequal steps. The half step between the seventh note and the tonic in a major scale creates a pull towards home. The whole-tone scale has no half steps at all, so that pull disappears.

It also contains no perfect fifths, which are the backbone of ordinary chords. The chords that can be built from the scale are augmented triads, made of stacked major thirds, which themselves sound open and unsettled.

Fingering ideas on the keyboard

Because the scale mixes black and white keys in an unusual way, standard major-scale fingerings do not apply. A practical approach is to group the notes by colour.

Collection starting on C

The white keys C, D, E are followed by the black keys F-sharp, G-sharp, A-sharp. Try one, two, three on the whites and two, three, four on the blacks, with the thumb returning to C.

Collection starting on C-sharp

The black keys C-sharp and D-sharp are followed by the white keys F, G, A, B. Try two, three on the blacks and one, two, three, four on the whites.

Experiment and write down what feels comfortable for your hand, since different hand sizes favour different patterns.

Using the scale creatively

The whole-tone sound is strong, so a little goes a long way. Composers often use it briefly for colour rather than for an entire piece.

  • Play a quick whole-tone run before a cadence to blur the harmony for a moment.
  • Hold an augmented chord with the pedal and let a melody drift across the scale above it.
  • Use the scale over a dominant seventh chord with a raised fifth, a common jazz colour.
  • Create a dream-sequence effect by sliding both hands up the scale in parallel.

Listening for whole-tone passages

Many French piano pieces from around the turn of the twentieth century contain passages built mostly from the whole-tone scale, often combined with pedal and soft dynamics to create shimmering textures. Jazz pianists also use whole-tone patterns over altered dominant chords, where the scale supplies raised fifths and ninths. In film music, a whole-tone run is a familiar sign that a scene is moving into memory or fantasy.

A short practice plan

Spend a few minutes each day for a week on this scale. Play both collections hands separately, then hands together in contrary motion, which often feels easier at first. Then improvise for one minute over a held augmented chord. FAQ

Q: Why are there only two whole-tone scales?

A: The scale divides the octave into six equal steps, so starting on any of its notes reproduces the same set. Only one other set fills the remaining notes.

Q: Which chords fit the whole-tone scale?

A: Augmented triads and dominant seventh chords with a raised fifth fit naturally.

Q: Is the whole-tone scale useful for beginners?

A: Yes, for improvising. Because there is no clear home note, it is hard to play a wrong-sounding note within the scale.

Q: How is it different from the chromatic scale?

A: The chromatic scale uses all twelve semitones. The whole-tone scale uses every other one, so it has six notes.
