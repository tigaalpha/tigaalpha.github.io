Kodály Hand Signs: Linking Solfa, Singing and Piano Keys

Practice tips · 2026-10-31

Kodály hand signs are a set of seven hand shapes, one for each degree of the scale, that let singers show pitch with their hands while they sing solfa syllables such as do, re and mi. The signs were devised by the English teacher John Curwen in the nineteenth century and later became a core tool in the teaching method associated with Zoltán Kodály, the composer from Hungary. Because each sign also sits at a different height, the body becomes a visible staircase of pitch. For pianists, combining the signs with the keyboard is a simple way to strengthen the link between what you hear, what you sing and where your fingers go.

This article introduces the seven signs, explains why movement helps memory, and offers practical games that pair signing with playing.

The seven signs at a glance

The signs follow movable do, which means do is always the home note of the current key, whatever its letter name.

  • Do: a closed fist, held at waist level. Solid and stable.
  • Re: a flat hand angled upward, as if pointing toward the next step.
  • Mi: a flat hand held level, palm down, calm and settled.
  • Fa: a fist with the thumb pointing downward, showing its pull toward mi.
  • So: an open palm facing the body, level, at chest height.
  • La: a relaxed hand hanging gently from the wrist.
  • Ti: the index finger pointing up toward the high do.

Each sign is placed slightly higher than the previous one, so a rising scale makes the hand climb and a falling scale makes it descend.

Why the body helps the ear

Learning to recognise intervals by sound alone can feel abstract. The signs give each scale degree a physical identity. The tension of fa, for example, is mirrored by a downward-pointing thumb, while the open, upward ti suggests movement to the next do. When students repeatedly feel these shapes while singing, they start to associate the sensation with the sound. Many teachers find that this multisensory approach helps young learners and adults alike sing more accurately.

The height of the hand adds a second layer of information. A leap from do to so is visibly larger than a step from do to re, so the size of an interval becomes something you can see.

Mapping signs to the keyboard

Start in C major

Place your right thumb on C. Sing do and show the fist, then press C. Move up the scale, adding each sign and key together, until you reach high C.

Move to another key

Shift to G major. Do is now G, and the signs stay exactly the same. This shows that solfa describes function in the key, not fixed letter names, which is a powerful idea for transposing.

Say, sign, then play

For any short melody, first sing it with solfa and signs, then play it. If you can sign it confidently, finding it on the keys becomes much faster.

Games for home and the classroom

Mirror melody

One person shows a sequence of signs silently. The other sings it, then plays it on the piano. Swap roles after each turn.

Missing note

Play a five-note phrase but leave out one note. The listener shows the sign for the missing note.

Hand-sign dictation

Play a short tune slowly. The listener follows along with signs instead of writing notes. This trains real-time listening without the pressure of notation.

Two-hand harmony

Advanced learners can show one sign with each hand, creating intervals. Sing both notes with a partner, then play the pair together on the piano.

Tips for using the signs well

  • Keep the movements relaxed and small. Precision matters more than size.
  • Use a consistent height for do so the scale stays readable.
  • Practise with simple songs first, such as folk tunes in a five-note range.
  • Add chromatic notes only after the seven basic signs are secure.

FAQ

Q: Did Kodály invent the hand signs?

A: No. John Curwen developed them. The Kodály approach adopted and adapted them, which is why they are often called Kodály hand signs.

Q: Are hand signs only for children?

A: They work for any age. Adults who struggle with sight-singing often find the signs make intervals more concrete.

Q: Do I need to sing well to use hand signs?

A: No. Gentle, accurate humming is enough. The goal is to connect pitch and movement, not to perform.

Q: How long should a hand-sign session last?

A: Five to ten minutes at the start of practice is plenty to warm up the ear.
