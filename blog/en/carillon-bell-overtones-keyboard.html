The Carillon: Bell Overtones and the Keyboard Played With Fists

Music theory · 2026-11-03

A carillon is a musical instrument made of at least 23 tuned bronze bells, usually hung in a tower; the musician plays it from a console of wooden levers called batons. The player, a carillonneur, strikes the batons with loosely closed hands and presses pedals with the feet, and wires connect each key to a clapper inside a bell. Carillons developed in the Low Countries, in what are now Belgium and the Netherlands, from around the sixteenth century. The instrument is fascinating for pianists because its bells produce overtones very different from piano strings, which changes how harmony sounds.

How a carillon is played

The baton keyboard looks like a large, widely spaced version of a piano keyboard, with the "black keys" raised above the "white keys". Because each baton pulls a clapper against a heavy bell, the player uses the side of a loose fist rather than fingertips. Pedals for the lower, heavier bells allow the feet to play bass lines.

  • Batons: wooden levers arranged like a keyboard.
  • Pedalboard: plays the largest bells, similar to an organ pedalboard.
  • Transmission: wires and cranks link each key to its clapper.
  • Dynamics: striking harder makes a louder sound, so touch still matters.

Practice instruments

Because the bells can be heard across a whole town, carillonneurs often prepare on a practice console with small metal bars or electronic sound, then perform on the tower instrument.

Why bells sound different from strings

When a piano string vibrates, its overtones follow the harmonic series, a set of frequencies that fit neatly with the fundamental. Bells behave differently. A well-tuned carillon bell typically contains a strong minor third above the main tone, along with other partials such as the octave below, called the hum note. This minor-third partial gives bells their slightly melancholy colour.

The result affects harmony. A major chord played on bells can sound unclear, because each bell already adds its own minor third. Composers and arrangers for carillon often choose open voicings, minor harmonies or widely spaced chords to avoid clashing overtones.

Long decay and musical spacing

Large bells ring for a long time after they are struck. Unlike the piano, there is no damper to stop the sound. Carillon music therefore needs careful spacing: fast passages are often placed in the higher, smaller bells, while bass notes move slowly. Tremolos, rapid alternations between two notes, help sustain melody notes on the lighter bells.

Lessons for pianists

Thinking like a carillonneur can improve your piano playing.

  • Listen to decay: hold a chord with the pedal and notice how each note fades at a different rate.
  • Space chords widely in the bass so low notes do not blur together.
  • Use the sustain pedal with care in low registers, changing it more often.
  • Try playing a melody with only the side of your hand on a single finger, feeling how arm weight produces sound.

These exercises connect sound physics with practical decisions about pedalling and voicing.

Hearing a carillon

Many cities in Europe, North America and other regions have carillons that give regular recitals, often in the open air. Listening from different distances is part of the experience: close to the tower the sound is bright and detailed, while further away the bells blend into a soft glow. If you cannot attend in person, recordings and videos still reveal the special overtones and the long ringing tails of the bells.

Writing and arranging for bells

Arrangers who adapt piano music for carillon often make specific changes. They move fast figures to the upper bells, thin out thick chords, avoid long major-third intervals in the bass and add repeated notes or tremolos to keep melodies alive. Trying a similar reduction on the piano is a helpful arranging exercise: take a familiar chorale or hymn-like piece, remove notes that clash or blur, and keep only the clearest outline. You will learn which notes really carry the harmony and which are simply filling space.

FAQ

Q: How many bells does a carillon have?

A: At least 23 tuned bells, though many instruments have far more.

Q: How is a carillon played?

A: By striking wooden batons with loosely closed hands and pressing pedals with the feet.

Q: Why do bells sound slightly melancholy?

A: A tuned bell usually contains a strong minor-third overtone above the main pitch.

Q: What can pianists learn from the carillon?

A: How overtones and decay affect harmony, voicing and pedalling decisions.
