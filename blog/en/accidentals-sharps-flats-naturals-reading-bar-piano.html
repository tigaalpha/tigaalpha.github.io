Sharps, Flats and Naturals: Reading Accidentals in a Bar

Music theory · 2026-11-20

An accidental is a small sign placed before a note that changes its pitch: a sharp raises it by a semitone, a flat lowers it by a semitone, and a natural cancels an earlier sharp or flat. On the piano, a semitone is the distance from one key to the very next key, whether black or white. The change lasts until the end of the bar, so one sign can affect several notes of the same letter name. Knowing this removes much of the confusion beginners feel when they see extra signs in a score.

The three signs

Sharp

The sharp sign looks like a small hash. It tells you to play the next key to the right. For F sharp you play the black key just above F.

Flat

The flat sign looks like a small lowercase b. It tells you to play the next key to the left. B flat is the black key just below B.

Natural

The natural sign cancels. If a sharp or flat is in the key signature or appeared earlier in the bar, the natural means play the plain white key again.

Not every sharp is a black key

Because a semitone is simply the nearest key, a sharp or flat can land on a white key. E sharp is the same key as F, and C flat is the same key as B. These cases are less common, but they appear in some keys. When you meet one, trust the rule: move one key in the direction of the sign.

How long an accidental lasts

An accidental applies to every note with the same letter name and the same octave until the next bar line. If the next bar begins with the same note, it returns to normal unless the key signature says otherwise.

  • The sign affects the note it stands before.
  • It also affects repeats of that note in the same bar and octave.
  • A bar line ends its effect.
  • A tied note carries it over into the next bar.

Accidentals and key signatures

A key signature puts sharps or flats at the start of each line and applies them throughout the piece. An accidental is a temporary change on top of that. For example, in a piece with F sharp in the key signature, an F natural sign means one F is played plainly for the rest of that bar. Remember that the key signature still returns in the next bar.

Reminder accidentals

Sometimes a composer or editor adds a natural or sharp that is technically not needed, just to remind the player. These are called courtesy or reminder accidentals, and they are often placed in brackets. They are easy to ignore if you know the rule, but they are a helpful hint in busy music.

Practising accidentals

Pick a short passage with a few accidentals and mark them with a pencil circle. Play slowly and say the note names aloud, including "sharp" or "flat". After that, play the same bar again without speaking, trusting your hands. Finally, look at the next bar and check whether the accidental is still in effect. This simple routine builds reading accuracy without any rushing.

A common slip

Many learners forget that the effect stops at the bar line and carry the sharp into the next bar. Others forget that it continues inside the bar and play a plain note after a sharp. Circling the sign and drawing a short line to the last affected note can make the idea visible on the page.

Spotting accidentals before you play

Take thirty seconds before every new piece to scan the score for sharps, flats and naturals. Circle each one with a pencil and say the note name. This habit prevents most surprises and teaches your eyes where to look. It is especially helpful in pieces that change key signature, where accidentals cluster. A short scan is much quicker than stopping repeatedly to correct a wrong note, and it makes your first read-through calmer and more musical.

FAQ

Q: What is a semitone?

A: It is the smallest step on the piano, from one key to the next nearest key.

Q: Does an accidental affect other octaves?

A: No. It applies to the same note name in the same octave only.

Q: What is a double sharp?

A: A sign that raises a note by two semitones. It is rare in beginner music.

Q: Why are flats and sharps both needed?

A: They help the score show the correct key and keep note names clear.
