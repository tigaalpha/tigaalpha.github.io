Teaching Intervals by Distance on the Keys: Seconds, Thirds, Fifths

Music theory · 2026-11-16

An interval is the distance between two notes. It sounds like a technical term, but learners can discover it quickly with their hands and ears. When teachers present intervals as distances on the keyboard, the idea becomes concrete. This article describes a gentle way to teach seconds, thirds and fifths to beginners.

Intervals in plain words

Every pair of notes has a distance. Notes next to each other in the musical alphabet form a second. Skipping one note forms a third. Skipping two forms a fourth, and so on. The number counts the letter names, including the first and last.

  • C to D is a second.
  • C to E is a third.
  • C to G is a fifth.

On the piano, the learner can see these distances by looking at white keys, finding the starting note and counting up.

Start with the hand

Place the hand in a relaxed shape over five white keys. The thumb is on the first note and the little finger on the fifth. Now the fingers are numbered and the distances are easy to feel.

Seconds: neighbouring fingers

Play finger one then finger two. This is a second, a small step.

Thirds: skip one finger

Play finger one then finger three. This is a third, a slightly bigger jump.

Fifths: from thumb to little finger

Play finger one then finger five. This is a fifth, a wide span.

Using the keys as a map

Ask the learner to point to a starting key and then find the note a second, a third or a fifth above it. Let them check by counting aloud: "one, two, three" for a third. Do this from several different starting notes, so that the learner sees the pattern repeats all over the keyboard.

  • Start with white keys only.
  • Later include black keys and talk about how they change the size slightly.
  • Use a sticker or a small marker to show the starting note if needed.

Training the ear

Once the learner can find intervals by sight, add listening. Play a second, then a third, then a fifth, and ask the learner to describe what they hear. Words like "close", "open" and "wide" are good. Many learners connect each interval with a short tune they know. The teacher can suggest well-known children's songs that start with a certain interval, but should check that the learner knows the song well.

  • Play the two notes one after another, then together.
  • Ask the learner to sing the top note after hearing both.
  • Let the learner close their eyes while listening.

Games that make it fun

  • Interval hop: Place paper notes on the floor and hop a second, a third or a fifth.
  • Mystery distance: The teacher plays two notes, and the learner finds the same distance somewhere else.
  • Draw it: The learner draws a staircase with steps of different sizes for the intervals.

Reading intervals in the score

On the staff, a second connects a line to a neighbouring space. A third goes from line to line or space to space. Show these on a simple score and let the learner colour them in. After a while, the eye starts to see the shapes without counting.

Why intervals matter

Intervals are the building blocks of melodies and chords. A learner who recognises thirds and fifths will find chords much easier later. They also help with sight-reading, because the eye can jump to the right key without counting each step.

Reminders for teachers and parents

  • Keep each session short and focus on one interval at a time.
  • Use real melodies as examples as soon as the learner is ready.
  • Celebrate careful listening, not only correct answers.

FAQ

Q: Should beginners learn all intervals at once?

A: No. Start with seconds, thirds and fifths, then add others gradually.

Q: Do black keys change the interval name?

A: The name stays based on letter names, but the size may change slightly. Introduce this later.

Q: Is ear training necessary?

A: It is helpful. A trained ear makes reading and playing easier.

Q: Can young children learn intervals?

A: Yes, through games and movement. Keep it playful.
