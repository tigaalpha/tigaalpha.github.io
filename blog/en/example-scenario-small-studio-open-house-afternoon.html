Example Scenario: A Studio Plans an Open House Afternoon

Stories · 2026-11-19

An open house afternoon lets prospective families visit a music studio, meet teachers and try an instrument in a relaxed setting. It works best when the studio plans a simple flow, keeps activities short, explains fees and schedules clearly and follows up gently afterwards. The fictional example below shows how one small studio might approach it, step by step, without hard selling.

About this fictional studio

This is an invented scenario used to illustrate ideas. It does not describe any real business. Imagine a small piano studio with three teachers, two teaching rooms and a modest waiting area. Most new students arrive through word of mouth, and the owner wants to give curious families an easy way to look around before deciding anything.

Setting a clear goal

The team begins by agreeing on what the afternoon is for. They decide the goal is understanding, not sign-ups. Visitors should leave knowing how lessons work, what the rooms feel like and whom to contact with questions.

  • Show the teaching rooms and instruments.
  • Let children and adults try a few notes.
  • Answer questions about schedules and fees honestly.

Keeping the goal modest shapes every later choice, from the invitation wording to the follow-up message.

Writing the invitation

The invitation is short and specific. It gives the date, the time window, the address and a note that visitors may drop in at any point. It avoids words such as "limited" or "last chance."

Invitation text in the example

Open studio afternoon, Saturday from 1 to 4 pm. Come and see our rooms, meet the teachers and try the piano. No booking needed. Children are welcome with an adult.

The studio shares the invitation with current families, on a notice at the entrance and on its own website, rather than buying advertising.

Planning the room flow

With limited space, the team plans how people will move. One room becomes the "try a piano" room, with a teacher guiding short, five-minute tries. The second room shows a typical lesson setup with books, a small whiteboard and a tablet for practice examples. The waiting area holds printed information and water.

  • Mark each room with a simple sign.
  • Keep tries short so visitors do not queue long.
  • Leave a quiet corner for parents with questions.

What children try

For children, the teacher in the try room uses a playful activity: spotting the paired and triple black keys, after which each child copies a short pattern by ear. Each child leaves with a small card showing the pattern so they can try it again at home on any keyboard.

Adults get a slightly different taste: a simple chord shape and a melody note on top, enough to feel the instrument respond. Nobody is tested or judged.

Talking about fees and schedules

The studio prints a single page listing lesson lengths, prices, available weekly slots and the cancellation policy. Teachers hand it out when asked and avoid discussing discounts or deadlines. If a family wants to start, the teacher notes their contact details only with permission and explains how the data will be used.

This transparency matters. Families comparing options appreciate straightforward facts more than persuasive language, and clear information reduces misunderstandings later.

Following up kindly

Two days later, the studio sends one short message to visitors who agreed to share contact details. It thanks them for coming, attaches the information page and invites questions. It sends no further reminders unless the family replies.

  • One message only, sent at a reasonable hour.
  • An easy way to say no thanks.
  • Contact details removed for anyone who asks.

What the team learns

After the afternoon, the teachers meet briefly. In this example, they notice that parents asked many questions about practice at home. They decide to add a short section on home practice to the information page, including how a practice app such as TIGA ALPHA can give feedback between lessons while the teacher guides overall progress.

They also note practical points: the try room needed a second chair, and the signs should be larger. Small adjustments like these make the next open house smoother.

FAQ

Q: Is this a real studio?

A: No. It is a fictional example created to explain one possible approach.

Q: How long should an open house last?

A: Two to three hours usually gives families flexibility without tiring the teachers.

Q: Should a studio offer special prices at an open house?

A: Many prefer not to, so visitors can decide calmly based on regular, clearly stated fees.

Q: What if very few people come?

A: A quiet afternoon still allows deeper conversations with each visitor, which can be just as valuable.
