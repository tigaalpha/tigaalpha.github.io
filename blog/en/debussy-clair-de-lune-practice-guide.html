How to Play Debussy’s Clair de Lune: A Four-Zone Practice Guide

Songs · 2026-10-31

Claude Debussy's "Clair de lune", the third of the four pieces in his Suite bergamasque, is a slow, dreamy work in D-flat major and 9/8 time. It is playable for late-intermediate pianists who are comfortable with five flats, careful pedalling and flexible timing. The key to learning it is to master the counting of the opening, keep the sound very soft and blended, and treat the flowing middle section as a wave of arpeggios rather than separate notes.

This guide breaks the piece into four zones, explains the main technical challenges in each, and offers a practice route from first reading to a full performance.

The piece at a glance

  • Composer: Claude Debussy (1862–1918).
  • Collection: Suite bergamasque, a set of four pieces begun around 1890 and published in 1905.
  • Title: borrowed from Paul Verlaine's poem "Clair de lune" ("Moonlight") from his collection Fêtes galantes.
  • Key and metre: D-flat major, 9/8, with a tempo marking of Andante très expressif.
  • Opening dynamic: pianissimo, with the instruction con sordina, meaning "with the soft pedal".
  • Typical length: around four to five minutes in many recordings.

Before you start: five flats and nine beats

The key signature

D-flat major has five flats: B, E, A, D and G. Spend a few minutes playing the D-flat major scale and its main chords so that the black keys feel like home. Most wrong notes in early readings come from forgetting one flat.

Counting 9/8

The bar has nine eighth notes, grouped in three sets of three. Many of the opening notes are tied across beats, which makes the rhythm hard to read. Count all nine eighths aloud slowly at first ("1-2-3, 4-5-6, 7-8-9"), and only later feel the music in three large beats.

Zone 1: the opening chords

The piece begins with soft, two-voice chords in the right hand that drift over tied notes. Nothing should sound like a beat being struck.

  • Play with the pads of the fingers close to the keys, using arm weight rather than finger force.
  • Count strictly while learning, so that the ties are held for their full value.
  • Listen for the top note of each chord. It is the melody and should be just a little clearer than the lower voice.
  • Use the soft pedal as marked, and change the sustain pedal with each harmony to avoid blur.

Zone 2: the first rise

As the harmony begins to move, the music grows gradually. Keep the growth gentle; Debussy saves the big moment for later. Aim for a smooth curve of sound across each phrase, and let the left hand support it with deep, quiet bass notes.

Zone 3: the flowing middle

The middle section changes character. The left hand plays wide, rippling arpeggios while the right hand sings a broad melody, often in octaves or chords.

Learn the arpeggios as shapes

Block each arpeggio into a chord first, so your hand knows the shape. Then unfold it slowly, keeping the wrist loose and letting it travel sideways with the notes.

Keep the left hand in the background

The arpeggios have many more notes than the melody, so they can easily become too loud. Practise them alone at a whisper.

Shape the climax

The music builds to the loudest passage of the piece. Even here, the sound should be round and warm, never harsh.

Zone 4: the return and the ending

The opening material returns, now with a gentle rippling accompaniment. The ending fades into silence. Hold the final chords for their full length and lift the pedal slowly, letting the sound disappear rather than stopping it.

Flexible timing without losing the pulse

Debussy marks moments of tempo rubato, a flexible approach to time. A helpful rule: let the music slow slightly at the ends of phrases and move forward a little as phrases grow, but return to the basic pulse. If you record yourself and the piece sounds like it is constantly stopping, the rubato is too strong.

A practice route

  • Stage 1: play the D-flat scale and chords; count the opening bars aloud without playing.
  • Stage 2: learn the four zones hands separately, starting with the middle section because it takes the longest.
  • Stage 3: put hands together slowly with strict counting and simple pedalling.
  • Stage 4: add dynamics, voicing and gentle rubato.
  • Stage 5: play the whole piece through once a day and record it once a week.

Expect the piece to take several weeks or months depending on your level. Clair de lune is a common goal piece in a piano course, and steady practice with clear targets matters more than speed.

Frequently asked questions

Q: What grade is Clair de lune?

A: It is generally considered late-intermediate to early-advanced, mainly because of its key, counting and pedalling.

Q: Why is the opening so hard to count?

A: The notes are tied across beats in 9/8, so you rarely play on the main beats. Counting all nine eighth notes aloud solves most problems.

Q: Do I need the soft pedal?

A: Debussy marks con sordina at the start. It helps create the muted colour, though the touch itself matters most.

Q: How can I keep the middle section from sounding messy?

A: Learn each arpeggio as a chord shape first, keep the left hand very soft, and give every new harmony a fresh, clean pedal.

Q: Can I learn this piece if I learn piano online?

A: Yes, especially if you divide it into sections, record your progress and get feedback on timing and balance.
