Trills on Piano: How to Play Them Evenly and Musically

Practice tips · 2026-11-11

A trill is an ornament in which a player alternates rapidly between a written note and the note directly above it. It is usually marked with the letters tr above the note, sometimes followed by a wavy line showing how long it should continue. Trills add sparkle, tension or excitement to a melody, but uneven trills can sound nervous or clumsy. With the right fingering and focused practice, most learners can develop trills that are smooth and controlled.

Which notes to play

The upper note of a trill is normally the next note of the scale in the current key. If the composer wants a different upper note, an accidental is written above the trill sign. For example, in C major a trill on E alternates between E and F, but a sharp above the sign would mean E and F-sharp.

Style also affects how the trill begins.

  • In much Baroque and early classical music, trills often start on the upper note.
  • In later music, trills usually start on the written main note.
  • Many trills end with a short turn, two notes that lead neatly into the next melody note.

When unsure, consult a teacher or a reliable edition, since conventions vary.

Choosing fingers

Different finger pairs suit different hands and passages. Common choices include two and three, one and three, and three and four. Many pianists find two and three the most comfortable and even, but the surrounding notes may make another pair more practical.

Test several pairs

Play a slow, even trill with each pair and listen for which produces the steadiest sound with the least effort. Write the chosen fingering in the score.

Building evenness first

A trill does not need to be fast to sound good. An even trill at moderate speed sounds far more musical than a fast, irregular one.

Count the notes

Begin by playing the trill as a measured rhythm, for example four or eight notes per beat. Use a metronome and keep the notes perfectly even.

Keep the motion small

Fingers should stay close to the keys, moving only as much as needed. Large lifting movements waste energy and slow the trill.

Let the hand help

A gentle rotation of the forearm, rocking slightly between the two fingers, can make the trill easier and more consistent.

Gradually adding speed

Increase speed in small steps. Play a measured trill at a comfortable speed, then add a little faster note group for one beat before returning. Over days and weeks, the faster group can extend until the whole trill moves at the desired speed.

Avoid pressing hard or tensing the arm to go faster. If the trill becomes tight, return to a slower speed and focus on lightness.

Trills in context

Practising trills alone is useful, but they must also fit into the music. Pay attention to how a trill starts and ends.

  • Practise the note before the trill and the first notes of the trill together.
  • Practise the ending turn and the note that follows, so the exit is smooth.
  • Decide how many notes the trill will contain in slower passages, since a measured trill can be more stable.
  • Shape the dynamic of the trill to match the phrase, often with a slight crescendo towards the resolution.

Trills against another part

Sometimes one hand trills while the other plays a melody or accompaniment. Practise slowly, counting how many trill notes fall on each beat of the other hand. Gradually make the trill freer once coordination is secure.

FAQ

Q: Should a trill start on the upper or main note?

A: It depends on the style and period. Baroque trills often start on the upper note, while later trills usually start on the main note.

Q: Which fingers should I use for trills?

A: There is no single answer. Many players use two and three, but test several pairs to find the steadiest for you.

Q: How fast should a trill be?

A: Fast enough to sound like an ornament, but slow enough to remain even and controlled.

Q: Why does my trill get stuck or uneven?

A: Tension is the usual cause. Practise slowly with small, relaxed motions and increase speed gradually.
