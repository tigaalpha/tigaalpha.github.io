The Deceptive Cadence on Piano: When the Dominant Avoids the Tonic

Music theory · 2026-11-11

A deceptive cadence on the piano is a close that seems headed for the tonic and then lands somewhere else, most often on the submediant. In C major that means a G or G7 chord followed by A minor rather than C major. The ear has been primed for rest, and the new chord is close enough to the tonic to feel related, yet the phrase is not finished. The pattern is a way to extend a paragraph of music without writing a full stop.

The usual dominant-to-vi move

A close neighbour of the tonic

In a major key, vi shares two notes with the tonic triad. In C major, C major is C–E–G and A minor is A–C–E. After G7 (G–B–D–F), the F often still falls to E, the B can rise to C, and the bass moves to A instead of C. The upper voices may look almost like a tonic resolution; only the bass (and the missing G of the tonic) tells the truth.

Play this slowly in C major, then in F major and in G major. Keep the right-hand resolution small. The surprise is stronger when the upper voices behave as they would in a real perfect cadence and only the bass takes the turn.

A short keyboard check:

  • Play a plain dominant-to-tonic cadence and remember the sound of rest.
  • Repeat the dominant, but land on vi with the same upper-voice habits.
  • Hold the vi chord long enough to hear that the phrase can continue.
  • Return to a real tonic cadence a bar or two later so the delay has a destination.

Other landing chords besides vi

Vi is the textbook case, not the only one. A dominant may move to a iv chord in minor, to a vi in first inversion, or to a chord that borrows colour from the parallel minor, such as a lowered sixth in the bass. The shared idea is that the expected tonic root does not arrive on time, while some upper notes still resolve as if it might have.

When you meet an unexpected landing in a score, name the two chords first, then see which voices resolved as they would toward the tonic and which did not. That is more useful than memorising a long list of rare labels.

Part writing that keeps the surprise clean

The seventh of the dominant still usually falls. The tendency tone in the raised-seventh position — the third of the dominant — still often rises. If both of those habits are dropped at once, the move can sound merely disconnected rather than deceptive. Keep at least one familiar resolution in the upper voices so the ear feels the missed tonic rather than a random jump.

The bass should be easy to hear. A weak, hidden A in an inner voice will not do the work of a clear bass on vi. Give the left hand a simple A, or a short arpeggio around it, and let the right hand stay close to its cadence shape.

Writing a delayed close into a four-bar model

A useful composition drill is: phrase on the tonic, move to a dominant, take a deceptive landing on vi, then continue with a real cadence in a fifth bar or a second four-bar group. The extra space after vi can be a short melody, a repeated motif, or a simple passing chord back toward the dominant. Do not rush to correct the surprise in the very next beat, or the delay will not register.

After the model works in C major, recast it in A minor, where the usual deceptive landing is often on F major (VI). Listen for the same sense of a postponed close, now in a minor colour.

When the word "deceptive" is a poor fit

Not every unexpected chord after a dominant is a cadence. If the phrase is mid-sentence and the dominant was only a passing harmony, the next chord is just the next chord. Reserve the cadence label for a moment that could have closed and chose not to. The listening test is simple: would a tonic at that spot have felt like an ending? If not, use a plainer description in your notes.

FAQ

Q: Is a deceptive cadence the same as an interrupted cadence?

A: Many teachers use the two names for the same dominant-to-vi (or similar) move. Follow the term your score or exercise uses, and listen for the postponed tonic.

Q: Must the second chord be vi?

A: Vi is the most common landing in major, but other related chords can create the same sense of a delayed close.

Q: Should I accent the surprising chord?

A: A little space and a clear bass are usually enough. A sudden harsh accent can make the move sound like a joke rather than a continuation.

Q: How do I resolve the seventh?

A: In most textbook cases it still steps down, even though the bass did not go to the tonic. That familiar step helps the ear recognise the cadence type.

Q: Can I use this in a short piece of my own?

A: Yes. Write a phrase that could have ended, land on vi, then add a real close a little later so the delay has a purpose.
