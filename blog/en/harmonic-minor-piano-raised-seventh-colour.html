Harmonic Minor on Piano: The Raised Seventh and Its Pull

Music theory · 2026-11-05

Harmonic minor is a natural minor scale with a raised seventh degree. That single change creates a strong pull toward the tonic and gives the dominant chord a major quality that natural minor lacks. On piano, the scale also produces an unusual three-semitone step between the sixth and seventh degrees, which gives melodies a distinctive twist. Learning the pattern in a few keys helps you recognise classical cadences, film cues and song bridges that lean on this colour.

From natural minor to harmonic minor

Natural minor in A is A, B, C, D, E, F, G, A. Raise G to G sharp and you have A harmonic minor. The raised note is called the raised seventh because it sits a half step below the tonic and wants to resolve upward.

The interval between F and G sharp is three half steps. That gap is why some harmonic-minor runs feel exotic or dramatic compared with smooth major scales. Practise slowly so the jump does not become a smear.

Chord consequence

In A minor, the chord on E becomes E–G sharp–B when you use the raised seventh. That E major (or E7) chord pulls firmly to A minor. Many pieces rely on that V–i motion.

Fingerings and hand shapes

Use a standard minor-scale fingering and adjust only where the raised note sits. In the right hand for A harmonic minor, a common approach mirrors A minor with the G sharp under whatever finger usually plays G, keeping the thumb on A and E as usual. Consistency matters more than one perfect published fingering.

  • Ascend and descend two octaves with a metronome on every beat.
  • Pause on the raised seventh and resolve to the tonic deliberately.
  • Then play the scale in broken thirds to hear the colour inside intervals.

Left-hand scales deserve equal time. Cadences often place the raised seventh in an inner voice or in the bass approach to the tonic.

Melodies that use the raised seventh

Compose eight-bar phrases that climb to the raised seventh and resolve. Avoid treating the note as a random sharp; give it a job. Common jobs include:

  • Ending a rising run by stepping to the tonic.
  • Decorating a dominant chord before a final i chord.
  • Approaching the tonic from the raised seventh as a true raised seventh.

Try an ostinato on i while the right hand leans on the raised seventh every fourth bar. The ear quickly learns the magnetic pull.

Practice plan across five keys

Spend one day each on A, E, D, G and C harmonic minor. For each key:

  • Scale, two octaves, hands separately.
  • V–i cadence in root position and first inversion.
  • A four-bar melody that must include the raised seventh twice.
  • A recorded take of the melody with a simple left-hand waltz or broken-chord pattern.

By the end of the week you will have a small book of your own phrases and a clearer ear for the colour in repertoire.

Where the colour shows up

Classical keyboard music uses harmonic minor for strong minor-key cadences. Folk and song arrangements borrow it when a bridge needs more urgency than natural minor offers. Film-style piano writing often climbs the scale and lands on the raised seventh before a cut to a new scene. You do not need a catalogue of titles; you need the habit of naming the raised note when you hear that sudden bright pull inside a minor context.

Compare the same melody in natural minor and harmonic minor. The comparison teaches faster than memorising rules alone.

FAQ

Q: Is harmonic minor harder than natural minor?

A: Only the raised note and the three-semitone gap are new. The rest of the scale matches natural minor.

Q: Should I always raise the seventh in minor keys?

A: No. Melodies and chords choose natural, harmonic or melodic minor for different effects. Learn all three over time.

Q: What about the melodic minor scale?

A: Melodic minor raises the sixth and seventh ascending and often returns to natural minor descending. Study it after harmonic minor feels secure.

Q: Can beginners start with harmonic minor?

A: Yes, after they know one natural minor scale well enough to change a single note on purpose.

Comparing natural and harmonic endings

Play the same four-bar minor melody twice: once with a natural seventh at the cadence, once with the raised seventh resolving to tonic. Record both. Most listeners describe the raised version as more conclusive. Knowing that difference helps you choose endings for sketches and helps you recognise why a classical piece suddenly brightens inside a minor key right before it rests.
