Barcarolle: How to Play the Piano Boat Song

Music theory · 2026-10-31

A barcarolle is a piece that imitates the songs of Venetian gondoliers, the boatmen who steer long, narrow boats through the canals of Venice. Its music rides on a lilting compound metre, most often 6/8 or 12/8, with a left hand that rises and falls like an oar pulling through water and a melody that glides on top. Pianists meet the barcarolle in pieces ranging from Mendelssohn's Venetian Gondola Songs to Chopin's Barcarolle in F sharp major, Op. 60. Understanding how the sway works makes these pieces far easier to learn and far more convincing to hear.

Below you will find the musical signature of the form, a short listening list, a counting method for 12/8 and a practice sequence for the left hand.

A boat song in a nutshell

Think of the movement of a gondola. The oar enters the water, pushes, lifts out and swings forward again, in a regular cycle. A barcarolle turns that cycle into rhythm. Each main beat contains three smaller pulses, and the accompaniment often spans a wide range, travelling up from a low bass note and back down. The melody tends to move in pairs of notes, sometimes in thirds or sixths, as if two voices were singing together across the water.

Compared with a cradle song, which rocks in place, a barcarolle travels. The phrases are longer, the bass covers more ground, and the overall mood is flowing rather than still.

Pieces to hear first

  • Mendelssohn, Venetian Gondola Songs. Three short pieces from the Songs Without Words, ideal for intermediate players.
  • Tchaikovsky, June from The Seasons. A wistful barcarolle in G minor, widely studied by students.
  • Offenbach, Barcarolle from The Tales of Hoffmann. An operatic duet that many pianists know through arrangements.
  • Chopin, Barcarolle Op. 60. A large-scale concert work from 1846, rich in harmony and demanding in technique.
  • Fauré, thirteen barcarolles. A series spanning decades of the composer's career, showing how far the form can stretch.

Listening to two or three of these back to back makes the shared rhythm unmistakable.

Counting 12/8 without getting lost

A bar of 12/8 contains twelve quavers grouped into four beats of three. Counting all twelve quickly becomes tiring, so try a layered approach.

Layer one: the big beats

Tap your foot four times per bar. These are the oar strokes.

Layer two: the inner triplets

Say "one-and-a, two-and-a, three-and-a, four-and-a" while tapping. Each syllable is one quaver.

Layer three: the long line

Once the triplets are steady, stop saying them aloud and think only of the four big beats. Feel the triplets inside your body rather than in your mouth. This is the level at which a barcarolle starts to flow.

Left-hand practice sequence

The accompaniment carries the motion of the piece, so it deserves dedicated attention.

Block the shapes

Play each left-hand group as a single chord. This shows the hand positions and reveals where the stretches are.

Rotate gently

Open the chord into its written pattern, letting the forearm rotate slightly rather than reaching with individual fingers.

Shade the curve

Make the lowest note a little fuller and let the upper notes taper, like water falling away from the oar.

Pedal through the stroke

Catch each bass note with the pedal and change cleanly when the harmony changes, so the low note keeps ringing beneath the pattern.

Join the melody last

Add the right hand only when the left hand can run for several bars without effort.

Common traps and how to avoid them

  • Accenting every triplet. This turns the lilt into a jog. Lean only on the main beats.
  • Rushing the top of the arc. Players often hurry the highest notes of the left hand. Keep the turnaround unhurried.
  • Thick pedal. In lower registers, long pedals blur quickly. Listen and change sooner if the bass turns muddy.
  • Melody too loud. The tune should sing, not shout. Let it float a little above the accompaniment level.

FAQ

Q: Is a barcarolle always in a minor key?

A: No. Many are in major keys. The defining element is the rocking compound metre and flowing accompaniment, not the mode.

Q: What is a good first barcarolle for an intermediate student?

A: Tchaikovsky's June or one of the Mendelssohn gondola songs are common choices before attempting larger works.

Q: How is a barcarolle different from a waltz?

A: A waltz has three beats per bar with a strong first beat. A barcarolle has larger beats each divided into three, producing a smoother sway.

Q: Should beginners practise 12/8 counting?

A: Yes. Clapping the four big beats with triplets inside is a useful rhythm exercise even before playing any barcarolle.
