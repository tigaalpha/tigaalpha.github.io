Giving One Clear Listening Task Between Piano Lessons

News · 2026-11-12

Listening is part of learning the piano, but a vague request to "listen to something this week" is easy to forget and hard to check. One clear listening task, set at the end of a lesson, works better: a named piece or a named movement, one thing to listen for, and a way for the learner to report back. The task should be short enough to complete, and it should connect to what is being practised, so the ear and the hands are working on related ideas. This is not a general culture assignment. It is a musical homework with a piano purpose.

Name the music and the one thing to hear

Write the composer, the title, and, if needed, the track or the movement, plus one question: where the main tune returns, how a bass pattern repeats, whether a close is major or minor, or how a dance pulse sits in the left hand. One question is enough. A list of six things to notice usually means none of them is noticed with care. Choose music the learner can actually obtain, through a library, a legal stream, or a recording the studio may play in the room.

Set one listening question, not a list of six

Keep the task short and attached to this week's playing

Five or ten concentrated minutes, once or twice in the week, is often enough. If the learner is practising a rondo, the task can be to hear how a returning tune is prepared. If the learner is working on a soft chord repeat, the task can be to hear how a recorded pianist matches tone. The link should be obvious in the written homework, so a parent can see why this track was chosen.

Ask for a short report, not a long essay

At the next lesson, take one or two minutes for the report: a spoken sentence, a mark on a short listening slip, or a hummed fragment. If the learner heard something unexpected, that is useful too. Do not turn the start of the lesson into a test that punishes a missed listening; find out what happened and, if needed, repeat a shorter task.

A teacher may also invite the learner to compare a short home take in TIGA ALPHA with the one feature heard in the recording, so the listening has a path back to the learner's own keys.

Choose recordings legally and at a comfortable volume

Use sources the family is entitled to use, and keep the volume comfortable in a shared house. The studio can keep a short list of suggested recordings for common teaching topics, with notes on where they can be found legally. The point is still one task at a time. A long recommended-listening list, offered without a question, is not a substitute for this week's clear job.

If a week includes a holiday, the same single task can still stand, with a slightly later report at the first lesson back, rather than a pile of extra tracks that no one will hear with care.

  • Write the title and one listening question, not a list of six.
  • Attach the task to this week's playing so the link is obvious.
  • Keep the listening short enough to complete in a busy week.
  • Ask for a brief report at the next lesson.
  • Point families to a legal source, and keep the volume considerate at home.

FAQ

Q: What if the family cannot find the recording?

A: Give a second legal source, or play the extract once in the lesson and ask the learner to remember one feature during the week.

Q: Should every lesson have a listening task?

A: Not necessarily. Use the task when it serves the current playing. A week of heavy technical work may need no extra assignment.

Q: Can the learner choose the piece?

A: Sometimes, if the teacher still supplies the one question to listen for, so the task remains specific.

Q: Is watching a video allowed, or only audio?

A: Either can work. If a video is used, the question should still be musical — a return, a bass, a close — not only a comment on the setting.

Q: How do I help a very young learner report back?

A: Ask for a demonstration: clap the pulse, sing the opening, or point to a returning tune if the teacher plays a short extract again.
