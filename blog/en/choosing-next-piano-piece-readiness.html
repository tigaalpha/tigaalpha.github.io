Choosing the Next Piano Piece: Signs a Learner Is Ready to Move On

Practice tips · 2026-11-12

Choosing the next piano piece is a teaching decision, not a race through a list. A learner is ready to move on when the present piece can be played with a stable pulse, planned fingering, and a musical shape that no longer collapses under a modest run, and when the next page adds only a limited amount of new work. A new piece chosen too early becomes a pile of unsolved corners. A new piece chosen too late can stall curiosity. The signs below are meant to be observed in the room, not guessed from a week-count.

Signs the current piece is actually finished

Start from the middle as well as from bar one

A finished piece, at this level, need not be a concert performance. It should be continuous at an agreed tempo, with the marked fingering mostly in place, and with dynamics or a simple character that can be repeated, not only produced once by luck. If every run still needs a rescue in the same four bars, the piece is not finished, even if the first page feels easy.

Ask the learner to start from the middle, and from a later section, not only from bar one. Readiness is clearer when the whole piece can be found, not only the opening.

What the next piece should add

The useful next piece stretches one main skill — a new rhythm, a wider left-hand pattern, a modest new key, or a slightly longer form — while keeping other demands close to what the learner already handles. If the new page is harder in rhythm, texture, key and length at once, the studio week will be spent surviving, not learning.

Name the one new skill in a sentence

Write that sentence at the top of the new score, for both teacher and learner: "this piece is for left-hand jumps," or "this piece is for dotted rhythms." If you cannot name the one new skill, the choice may be too broad, or the reason for the choice may be only that the title looked appealing.

Involving the learner without losing the frame

A late beginner can help choose among two or three suitable pieces, after the teacher has already filtered for readiness. A free choice from a large stack often leads to a page that looks friendly and is technically unkind, or to a piece that repeats a skill already secured and adds nothing. Offer a short, honest reason for each option, play a little of each, and then decide.

If a learner is very attached to an overly hard piece, it can sometimes sit as a slow, long-term side project, while the official next piece remains at a fair level. The official piece still needs to be finished.

Practical checks in the first week of a new piece

  • The first read can be honest at a slow pulse.
  • The new skill you named actually appears, and can be isolated.
  • The learner can find the starting hand position without a long search.
  • A tiny fragment of the new skill can be practised in the first lesson.
  • The previous piece is not immediately abandoned; a short review keeps it available.

When to wait a little longer

Wait if reading is still guesswork, if hands-together work on the current piece is not yet stable, or if a break in attendance has left the last page shaky. A short review period, or a slightly easier bridging piece, is a fair decision. Moving on to protect a sense of progress, while the last piece is still broken, usually creates a longer delay later.

FAQ

Q: How finished does a piece need to be before a new one starts?

A: Finished enough to be continuous at an agreed tempo, with planned fingering and a repeatable shape. It does not have to be a formal recital performance.

Q: Can two pieces be in progress at once?

A: Yes, if one is being finished and the other is a careful first read, or if a harder piece is a slow side project. Two new, equally hard pieces at once are harder to teach well.

Q: What if the learner is bored of the current piece?

A: Check whether it is actually finished. If it is, move on. If it is not, shorten the remaining work, change the practice task, or add a small new side piece, rather than leaving a pile of unfixed bars.

Q: Should the next piece always be in a new key?

A: Not always. A new key can be the one new skill, but it should not arrive together with several other large demands.

Q: Who should choose the piece?

A: The teacher should filter for readiness. The learner can then help choose among a short, suitable list.
