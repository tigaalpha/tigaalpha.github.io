Portato: The Gentle In-Between of Legato and Staccato

Music theory · 2026-11-22

Portato is a way of playing notes that sit between smooth legato and short staccato. Each note is gently released before the next one begins, but the gap is small and the line keeps singing. In printed music it usually appears as dots under a slur, or as short dashes, and it asks for a touch that is soft, shaped and a little breathing.

What the markings look like

Legato is shown with a curved line over several notes, and staccato with a small dot above or below a note. Portato combines the two: a slur is drawn over the notes and each note also carries a dot or a short line. The slur says the phrase belongs together, while the dots say each note has its own gentle start and finish.

Some editions write the same idea with the word "portato" or with the abbreviation "mezzo-staccato". Because publishers differ, it helps to read the surrounding bars and decide how long each note should sound before it is lifted.

How it sounds and feels

Imagine speaking a sentence with a small pause between syllables, but without cutting any of them off. That is close to the feeling of portato. The notes are not clipped, and they are not blended into one another. Each one is a soft step along the melody.

On the piano the touch comes from the arm and fingers together. The key is pressed with a calm, rounded weight, and then released a little earlier than in legato. The pedal, if used at all, should be light, so the small gaps stay audible.

A simple way to practise

Choose a short passage of five or six notes, such as a slow scale fragment.

Try it three ways

  • Play it legato, holding each note until the next one begins.
  • Play it staccato, releasing each key very quickly.
  • Play it portato, releasing each key about halfway between those two ideas.

Listen to the difference in length, not only in volume. Repeat the portato version slowly, and ask whether the line still feels connected when the notes are separated.

Where portato appears

Portato turns up in slow songs, in gentle accompaniments and in passages that need a calm, walking quality. A left-hand bass line may use it to feel steady without sounding heavy. In a melody it can make a repeated note sound more thoughtful. In string and wind playing the same idea is produced with the bow or the breath, and pianists often borrow that picture when they shape a phrase.

If a score has no printed marking but the character of the music feels like a gentle walk, it is fine to ask a teacher whether a portato touch would fit.

Common questions about the touch

Two easy mistakes

New players sometimes make portato too short and it turns into staccato, or too long and it becomes legato again. A helpful check is to record a short take on a phone and listen for small, even gaps. If the gaps are uneven, slow the tempo and try again with a relaxed wrist.

Another useful habit is to sing the notes quietly while playing. A voice cannot cut a note off abruptly, so singing can remind the hands to keep the line soft and shaped.

Putting it into a practice session

Spend two or three minutes at the start of a session on touch exercises, then move to a real piece. Mark the portato bars lightly in pencil, play them hands separately, and then hands together. A learner who likes extra structure could try a piano teaching app for short guided drills on touch, then return to the printed music at the instrument.

Portato is learned by listening. When the small gaps sound even and the phrase still moves forward, the touch is working.

FAQ

Q: Is portato the same as staccato?

A: No. Staccato is short and detached, while portato is only lightly separated and keeps a singing line.

Q: How do I recognise portato in a score?

A: Look for dots or short dashes under a slur. Some editions also write the word portato or mezzo-staccato above the notes.

Q: Do I need the pedal for portato?

A: Usually only a very light touch of pedal, if any. Too much pedal blurs the small gaps that make the effect.

Q: Can beginners practise portato?

A: Yes. Start with a slow five-note passage and compare legato, staccato and portato side by side.
