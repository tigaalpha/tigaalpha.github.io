Example Scenario: A Small Piano Studio Plans Its First Open House

Stories · 2026-11-11

This is an illustrative example, not a report about a real business. It describes how a small, independent piano studio might plan an open house: a free afternoon when families and adult learners can visit, touch the instruments, meet teachers and ask questions without committing to anything. The aim is to show the planning decisions involved, so other studios can adapt the idea to their own space and audience.

The situation in the example

Imagine a studio with three teaching rooms, two part-time teachers and a mix of children and adult students. Most new enquiries arrive through messages asking similar questions: what age is suitable, whether a piano is needed at home, and how lessons are structured. The owner notices that many people ask, but fewer book a first lesson, perhaps because the process feels unfamiliar.

The open house is meant to lower that barrier by letting people see the studio before deciding.

Setting a clear goal

Before choosing a date, the team writes one sentence describing success: visitors should understand what a lesson feels like and leave with answers to their main questions. Sign-ups are welcome, but they are not the measure of the day.

That goal shapes everything else. It rules out a hard sales table and encourages short, hands-on activities.

Designing the afternoon

The team plans a relaxed flow so visitors can drop in at any time during a three-hour window.

  • A welcome desk with a simple one-page guide to lesson types and timetables.
  • A try-the-keys room where children and adults can play a few notes with a teacher's help.
  • A mini lesson every half hour, lasting ten minutes, showing a typical warm-up and a short piece.
  • A question corner where parents can talk about practice routines, instruments and schedules.
  • A quiet listening area playing recordings made by current students, shared only with their consent.

Thinking about accessibility

The team checks that the entrance is easy to reach, that signs are readable, and that information exists in the languages local families use.

Inviting people honestly

Invitations go out through the studio's mailing list, a notice at the local library and posts on the studio's own social pages. The wording is plain: what will happen, when, where, and that there is no cost and no obligation. The team avoids phrases that pressure people or suggest limited places when space is not actually limited.

Current students are invited to bring a friend, and a few volunteer to play short pieces. Their parents' consent is recorded before any photos or recordings are shared.

Showing technology without overpromising

In the example, the studio also uses an AI practice app between lessons. One teacher sets up a keyboard where visitors can try TIGA ALPHA and see how it responds to their playing. The teacher explains what the app does, such as listening for notes and timing, and what it does not do, such as replace a teacher's guidance on musical expression. Visitors appreciate the honest explanation more than a polished demonstration.

Following up afterwards

At the end of the day, visitors who wish to can leave an email address to receive the timetable. The team sends one message within a few days, thanking them, answering questions raised on the day and linking to the booking page. There are no repeated reminders.

What other studios can take from it

The key lessons are simple and transferable. Decide what success means before planning. Let people experience the activity rather than only hearing about it. Keep invitations factual and calm. Respect consent for photos, recordings and contact details. Finally, write down the questions visitors asked, because those questions are a valuable guide to improving the website and the enquiry process.

FAQ

Q: Did this open house actually take place?

A: No. It is a composite example intended to illustrate planning decisions.

Q: How long should an open house last?

A: A drop-in window of two to three hours lets visitors come when they can without a fixed start time.

Q: Should studios offer discounts at the event?

A: That is a business decision. If offers are made, they should be clear and not framed with false urgency.

Q: What if very few people attend?

A: Use the time to record short videos of the mini lesson, with consent, and review how the invitation was shared for next time.
