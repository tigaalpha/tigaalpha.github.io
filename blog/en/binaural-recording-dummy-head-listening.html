Binaural Recording: Hearing Music Through a Dummy Head

News · 2026-11-08

Binaural recording is a technique that uses two microphones placed inside the ears of a model human head, often called a dummy head, to capture sound the way our own ears hear it. When you listen to the recording on headphones, sounds can seem to come from in front, behind, above or beside you. This creates a strong feeling of being in the room. It is used for concerts, sound art, radio drama and research into how people hear.

How our ears locate sound

We know where a sound comes from because of small differences between our two ears. A sound from the left reaches the left ear slightly earlier and slightly louder than the right ear. The shape of the outer ear, the head and the shoulders also changes the tone colour of sounds depending on direction.

  • Timing differences tell us about left and right.
  • Level differences add more detail at higher frequencies.
  • The outer ear filters sound, which helps with height and front or back.

A dummy head copies these physical features, so the recording carries the same clues our brain uses every day.

The dummy head setup

A typical binaural setup uses a head-shaped model made from materials that reflect and absorb sound much like a real head. Small microphones sit inside the ear canals. Some designs include shoulders or a torso for extra realism. Recordists place the head where a listener would sit, such as in a good seat at a concert hall.

Headphones are essential

Binaural recordings are designed for headphones. On loudspeakers, the sound from each speaker reaches both ears, and the spatial effect is greatly reduced.

Listening to piano in binaural

A piano recorded binaurally can sound surprisingly present. You may hear the low notes slightly to one side, the sound of the room and small details like the movement of the pedal. Listening this way can help learners understand how a piano fills a space.

Try comparing a regular stereo recording with a binaural one. Notice how the sense of distance and room changes. This kind of comparison trains careful listening, which is valuable for anyone who plays.

Uses beyond music

The idea is older than many people expect. Experiments with listening through two separate microphones go back to the late nineteenth century, and dummy heads were refined during the twentieth century for broadcasting and acoustic research.

Binaural techniques appear in many fields. Radio dramas use them to place characters around the listener. Researchers use dummy heads to measure noise in cars and buildings. Virtual reality and gaming use related methods to create sound that moves as you turn your head.

Recording your own playing

You do not need a professional dummy head to experiment. Small in-ear microphones worn by a person can create a simple binaural effect. If you record yourself at the piano this way, the result will sound like your own position at the keyboard. Listening back may reveal balance issues, such as a left hand that is louder than you thought.

Tips for a cleaner recording

Keep head movements small, because moving your head shifts the entire sound image. Set levels so that loud passages do not distort, and record a short test before the full piece.

Limits to keep in mind

Binaural recordings do not work equally well for everyone, because ear shapes differ. Some listeners hear sounds as inside the head rather than in front of them. Researchers continue to study personalised methods that adjust recordings to individual ears. Even with these limits, binaural sound remains one of the most direct ways to share the experience of being in a room with music.

Playback volume matters too. Keep headphones at a comfortable level, since detailed recordings can tempt you to turn the sound up.

FAQ

Q: Can I hear binaural effects on speakers?

A: Only weakly. The effect is designed for headphones.

Q: What is a dummy head?

A: A model head with microphones in the ears that captures sound like human hearing.

Q: Why do some sounds seem to come from behind?

A: The outer ear changes the tone of sounds from different directions, and the recording keeps these clues.

Q: Is binaural useful for piano practice?

A: It can help you hear your balance and the room more like a listener would.
