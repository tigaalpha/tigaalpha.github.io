Sonatina Form Explained: Exposition, Development and Recapitulation

Music theory · 2026-10-31

Sonatina form is a small version of sonata form. A first theme in the home key is followed by a second theme in a related key, usually the dominant; there may be a very short development or none at all; and then the opening material returns with the second theme now in the home key. If you can spot these three parts in a sonatina movement, you will learn it faster, memorise it more securely and play it with a clearer sense of direction.

This article shows how to recognise each part, works through a well-known example, and gives you an analysis routine you can use on any sonatina before you start practising it.

The three parts in brief

  • Exposition: presents the first theme (home key), a short transition, the second theme (new key) and a closing phrase. It is often repeated.
  • Development: takes pieces of the themes and moves them through different keys. In sonatinas this part is usually short, sometimes only a few bars, and some sonatinas skip it.
  • Recapitulation: brings back the first theme in the home key, then the second theme, also in the home key, so the piece ends where it began.

The big idea is a journey: home, away, a little wandering, and home again with everything resolved.

Why the keys matter

The most important event in the form is the change of key in the exposition. In a major-key sonatina, the second theme usually appears in the dominant: in C major, that means G major, with F♯ appearing in the music. In minor keys, the second theme often moves to the relative major.

When the recapitulation comes, the second theme returns in the home key. That small change, the same tune but in a new key, is what makes the ending feel settled. Hearing it is one of the most satisfying moments in classical piano music.

A worked example: Clementi's Sonatina in C, Op. 36/1

Muzio Clementi's Six Sonatinas, Op. 36, are widely used teaching pieces for piano. The first movement of the first sonatina, in C major, is a compact model of the form.

First theme

It opens with a bold rising C major arpeggio and a falling scale answer. The music is clearly in C.

Second theme

After a short transition, the music settles in G major, with F♯ now appearing. The character becomes lighter and more playful.

Development

A brief passage moves away from the home key using ideas from the exposition. It is short enough to learn in a few sessions.

Recapitulation

The opening arpeggio returns in C major, and the second theme follows, now also in C. Compare it with the exposition and notice which notes changed to stay in the home key.

A famous twist: Mozart's "easy" sonata

Mozart's Piano Sonata in C major, K. 545, is often called the "Sonata facile". Its first movement follows the same plan as a sonatina, with one memorable surprise: the recapitulation begins in F major rather than C major. Mozart then adjusts the music so that the second theme arrives in C as expected. Knowing the "rule" lets you enjoy how a composer bends it.

An analysis routine before you practise

Step 1: find the home key

Look at the key signature and the first and last chords.

Step 2: find the second theme

Look for the place where a new accidental appears regularly and the mood changes. Write "Theme 2" and the new key above it.

Step 3: mark the boundaries

Draw a line at the end of the exposition, often marked by a repeat sign. Then find where the first theme returns; that is the start of the recapitulation.

Step 4: compare the two halves

Put the exposition and recapitulation side by side. Circle every bar that is different. Those circled bars are where memory slips usually happen, so practise them on purpose.

Step 5: name the character

Give each theme a one-word description, such as "bold" or "gentle". Plan your dynamics and touch to show the contrast.

This routine takes about fifteen minutes and makes later practice far more efficient.

Practice tips for sonatina movements

  • Learn the recap differences first. The bars that change between exposition and recapitulation deserve the most attention.
  • Play the themes in contrast. If both themes sound the same, the form disappears.
  • Keep Alberti basses light. Many sonatinas use broken-chord accompaniments. Let them murmur under the melody.
  • Practise transitions alone. The bridges between themes are often where tempo wobbles.
  • Memorise by section, not by page. Knowing "this is the second theme in G" is more reliable than remembering where the page turn was.

Frequently asked questions

Q: What is the difference between sonata form and sonatina form?

A: Sonatina form follows the same basic plan but is shorter, with simpler themes and a brief or missing development.

Q: Which composers wrote popular sonatinas?

A: Muzio Clementi, Friedrich Kuhlau and Anton Diabelli wrote sonatinas that are widely used in piano study.

Q: Why does the second theme change key in the recapitulation?

A: Returning in the home key resolves the tension created in the exposition, so the movement can end at home.

Q: Is analysis really useful for beginners?

A: Yes. Knowing the structure helps you divide practice, remember the piece and shape your performance.

Q: Can an app help me learn a sonatina?

A: An app that listens to your playing can help you check accuracy and timing in each section, while the analysis guides your practice plan.
