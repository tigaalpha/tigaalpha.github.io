Physical Modelling: How Software Simulates Piano Strings

News · 2026-11-13

Physical modelling is a way of making a piano sound in software by simulating the instrument's moving parts, rather than by playing back recordings of a real piano. The program uses mathematical descriptions of a string's vibration, a hammer's contact, a damper's arrival and a soundboard's radiation, then calculates how those parts interact in real time as keys and pedals are used. When the model is convincing, a soft note, a hard attack or a slowly lifted damper behaves like a related physical event, not like a different recording being selected from a shelf.

What a model is trying to recreate

A piano tone is the result of several coupled systems. The hammer felt compresses against one or more strings. The strings vibrate with a slightly inharmonic set of partials. Energy travels into the bridge and the soundboard, which move air. Dampers can stop the strings, and undamped strings can respond in sympathy. A useful model does not need to copy every screw in the case, but it does need these main energy paths.

Strings as vibrating objects

The string part of the model tracks how a displacement travels back and forth, how stiffness makes higher partials run a little sharp, and how energy fades. If the player repeats a note, the new hammer hit meets a string that may already be moving, which a simple one-shot sample cannot show as naturally.

Hammers, dampers and the board

The hammer model covers contact time and stiffness, which change with velocity. The damper model covers how quickly a felt can silence a string, including half-pedal states. A soundboard model spreads the vibration so the same string does not sound like a thin laboratory wire.

How this differs from sample playback

Sample-based instruments record many real notes at several dynamic levels and then switch or crossfade between those recordings. They can sound remarkably faithful because the raw tone is a genuine piano. Physical modelling starts from rules instead of recordings, which leads to a different set of strengths:

  • Notes can interact, so one loud attack can disturb a string that is already sounding.
  • Pedal behaviour can vary continuously instead of jumping between a few recorded pedal states.
  • The same model can be retuned, slightly resized, or given a different hammer hardness without a new recording session.
  • Long resonances do not have to end when a recorded loop or decay file ends.

Neither approach is a moral improvement on the other. They are different tools. Sample libraries often win on the instantly recognised character of a particular instrument. Modelling often wins when the player asks the sound to respond in between the recorded points.

Where a simulated piano still needs judgement

A model is only as musical as its simplifications.

  • Some designs underplay the messy, woody character of a real soundboard or the noise of the action.
  • Extreme playing, such as a bare whisper or a cluster struck from a height, may fall outside what the equations were tuned to do.
  • Headphones and small loudspeakers can hide or exaggerate resonance that would be obvious in a room.
  • A model will not automatically sit in a piece with the same care that a recording engineer gives a chosen instrument.

If you use a modelled piano for daily practice, spend a little time listening to real acoustic instruments as well, so the ear keeps a live reference.

What players can notice at the keyboard

You can test a modelled sound in a thoughtful way without any laboratory equipment.

Repeat a soft note

Play the same key several times at a low level and listen for whether later repetitions meet a string that still has life, or whether each note starts as if from silence.

Explore half pedal

Lower the sustain pedal only part of the way and move a short melody through it. A flexible model should change the blur gradually.

Leave some notes open

Hold a low chord silently, then play a short figure above it and listen for sympathetic response when you release the figure.

Listen to a long decay

Play a mid-range chord, lift the fingers, and keep the sustain pedal down. Notice whether the virtual soundboard continues in a natural way or cuts off too soon.

FAQ

Q: Is physical modelling the same as digital synthesis from oscillators?

A: It is a form of synthesis, but it aims to simulate the moving parts of an instrument, not to build a tone from abstract waveforms alone.

Q: Why do some modelled pianos feel more responsive than recordings?

A: Because the sound is calculated from the present state of the virtual instrument, including strings that are already vibrating and pedals that are only partly down.

Q: Can a model replace an acoustic piano?

A: It can be a flexible practice and writing tool. It does not copy every detail of a particular acoustic instrument in a real room, so both experiences remain useful.

Q: Do I need to understand the equations to use one?

A: No. The useful skill is listening: changing touch and pedal, then noticing how the sound responds.
