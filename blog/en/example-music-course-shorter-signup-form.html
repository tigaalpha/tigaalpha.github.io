Example Scenario: An Online Music Course Shortens Its Sign-Up Form

Stories · 2026-11-10

This is an illustrative example scenario, not a report about a real business. It imagines a small online music course whose sign-up form had grown long and confusing, and walks through how the team might simplify it. The point is to show a practical marketing and product process: look at the form from the learner's side, remove what is not needed, explain what remains and respect personal data from the first click.

The starting situation

Imagine a two-person team running an online beginner keyboard course. Over time, each new idea added a field to the sign-up form. The marketing side wanted to know how people found the course. The teaching side wanted the learner's experience level, goals and preferred music styles. Someone added a birthday field for greetings, and another person added a phone number in case support was needed.

The team notices a pattern in their messages: visitors write to ask why so much information is required before they can even try a lesson. Some say they stopped halfway. The team does not have precise figures, and in this scenario they deliberately avoid guessing. They simply decide that the form deserves a careful review.

Step one: list every field and its purpose

The team copies every field into a simple table and writes one line next to each: why do we need this, and when?

  • Name and email: needed to create an account.
  • Password: needed, unless a sign-in link by email is used instead.
  • Experience level: useful, but only once lessons begin.
  • Goals and music styles: useful for recommendations later.
  • How did you hear about us: interesting for marketing, never required.
  • Birthday: not needed for learning at all.
  • Phone number: rarely used; email already covers support.

Seeing purposes side by side makes the decision clearer. Only a few fields are truly needed at the moment of sign-up.

Step two: move questions to the right moment

Rather than deleting useful questions, the team moves them. Experience level and goals become a short, friendly welcome step after the account exists, presented as "help us suggest your first lesson". Learners can skip it. The marketing question about how people found the course moves to an optional one-line survey a week later.

This separation follows a broader principle in data protection laws such as Thailand's PDPA: collect only what is necessary for a stated purpose, and explain that purpose. It also feels more natural, because people answer questions more willingly once they understand how the answers help them.

Step three: rewrite the words around the form

Small wording changes make a large difference to how a form feels.

Headline

Instead of a bare "Register", the page explains what happens next: create an account and choose a first lesson.

Field labels

Plain labels such as "Your email" replace technical ones. A short line under the email field says it is used for account access and lesson reminders only.

Consent

Marketing emails get a separate, unticked checkbox, so learners actively choose them. The privacy notice link sits right next to it.

Buttons

The button says what it does, for example "Create my account", rather than a vague "Submit".

Parents and children

For learners under the local age of consent, the form asks for a parent or guardian to complete sign-up and explains why.

Step four: check the experience on a phone

Most visitors in this scenario arrive from mobile devices. The team opens the new form on several phones and checks that fields are large enough to tap, the keyboard type matches each field, error messages appear next to the right field and the page does not jump while loading.

Step five: measure gently

After launch, the team watches a few simple signals over the following weeks: how many visitors start and finish the form, how many support messages mention sign-up problems and how many learners complete the optional welcome step. They compare against their own earlier experience rather than against industry claims, and they avoid announcing results they cannot support.

Where an AI product fits the story

The same thinking applies to app onboarding. A learning app such as TIGA ALPHA could ask for very little at first, let the learner play a short passage and then suggest a starting level based on what it hears. Letting the instrument answer the experience question, instead of a dropdown menu, is a small example of how AI can make onboarding feel modern and less like paperwork.

Lessons from the scenario

  • Every field should have a purpose and a moment.
  • Optional questions work better after trust is built.
  • Clear words around a form matter as much as the fields themselves.
  • Consent should be active, separate and easy to understand.

FAQ

Q: Is this based on a real company?

A: No. It is a generic example scenario designed to illustrate a process any music course or app could follow.

Q: Should a sign-up form ever ask for a phone number?

A: Only if the service genuinely needs it, such as for lesson reminders by text that the learner has chosen.

Q: Can we still learn how people found us?

A: Yes. An optional question after sign-up, or analytics that respect consent settings, can provide that information.

Q: How short is short enough?

A: Short enough that each remaining field clearly helps the learner start. For many courses that is an email and a password, or a sign-in link.
