The Gruppetto Turn on Piano: Four Notes Around a Main Pitch

Practice tips · 2026-11-11

A turn, or gruppetto, on the piano is a four-note ornament around a principal pitch: the upper neighbour, the written note, the lower neighbour, and the written note again, in the most common modern teaching shape. Some periods begin differently, so a preface still matters, but the useful first skill is to play four even notes that circle the main pitch and arrive on time. The turn should feel like a curl in the line, not like a new bar squeezed into the old one.

The basic four-note shape

Upper, written, lower, written

In C major, a turn on E is often F, E, D, E. Play those four notes slowly, with the last E being the one that continues the melody. If an accidental is printed above or below the sign, it applies to the upper or lower neighbour. A sharp above the sign, for example, would raise the upper neighbour.

A slow construction:

  • Find the written note and both neighbours in the current scale, plus any marked accidentals.
  • Play the four-note group in even values, slower than the piece.
  • Place the last of the four notes on a part of the beat you have chosen in advance.
  • Shorten the first three notes together as you gain control, without moving the arrival.

Where the turn sits in the beat

Turns may be written directly over a note, or between two notes. Over a note, the curl often starts at the beginning of that note, or slightly after a first short sound of the main pitch, depending on style and tempo. Between two notes, the turn usually occupies the end of the first note's time and leads into the second. Decide the placement before you go fast, and mark it with a small bracket if the page is crowded.

A common mistake is to let the four notes push the next melody note late. Practise the arrival first: hold a rest for the first part of the beat if needed, then place the last note of the turn exactly where the written pitch must continue.

Fingering a clean curl

Choose a fingering that can play both neighbours without a large shift. For a right-hand turn on a white key with white-key neighbours, a compact 3–2–1–2 or 4–3–2–3 pattern is often workable; test both and keep the one that stays closest to the keys. When a black key is involved, let the hand stay over the black-key area so the neighbours are not a long reach.

Do not change the fingering of the surrounding passage just to make a showy turn. The ornament should be possible inside the fingering you already need for the phrase.

A quieter last note

The last note of the turn is the main pitch returning to the line. It can take a little more length than the inner notes, especially in a slow tempo, so the melody is easy to hear again. The inner notes should remain even with one another.

Turns after a dotted rhythm

In some slow movements a turn is written after a dotted note and before a short one. The four notes then live in the last part of the long value. Count the dotted note first, leave a clearly felt remainder, and place the turn inside that remainder so the short following note is still on time. If the turn steals the short note's place, the rhythm of the bar collapses.

When not to add a turn

If the score has no sign, do not invent a gruppetto for decoration during a first learning stage. Added ornaments belong to a later stylistic choice, and only when the plain line is already secure. One written turn, played clearly, is more useful than several guessed curls in the same phrase.

FAQ

Q: How many notes are in a turn?

A: The common teaching shape has four notes around the principal pitch. Some historical versions differ, so check the edition if a piece is early or heavily marked.

Q: Which neighbour is first?

A: In the shape described here, the upper neighbour comes first. If a special sign or preface asks for an inverted turn, the lower neighbour starts instead.

Q: My turn makes the next beat late. What should I change?

A: Shorten the first three notes as a group and protect the arrival of the last note. Practise the arrival with a click or a spoken count.

Q: Can both neighbours be chromatic?

A: Yes, when accidentals in the sign or the key require it. Spell them carefully so the hand does not treat them as random extra keys.

Q: Should a turn be loud?

A: It should be clear, not heavier than the line it decorates. The returning main note is usually the one that carries the phrase.
