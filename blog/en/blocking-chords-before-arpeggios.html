Blocking Chords Before Practising Arpeggio Figures

Practice tips · 2026-11-12

Many piano passages are written as arpeggios or broken chords: the notes of a harmony arrive one after another instead of together. If you begin by rolling the figure at speed, the hand may never learn the shape of the chord, and the ear may never hear where the harmony changes. Blocking the same notes as a single chord, in the fingering you will later use, gives both the hand and the ear a map. The rolled figure then has somewhere to go.

Name the harmony of each blocked shape before you roll it

Build the blocked chord with the fingering of the figure

Look at one group of the arpeggio and put every note down together, using the fingers that will play the rolling version. If a stretch is uncomfortable as a block, the rolling fingering may also be a poor fit; adjust it now and write it in. Play the blocked chord and listen for a clear, balanced sound, not only for whether all the notes speak. Move to the next harmony and do the same, so you have a chain of shapes rather than one isolated grip.

Hear where the harmony changes in the blocked chain

Play the blocked chords in order, slowly, as if they were a chorale. Notice which note is new, which note is held in common, and where a bass note turns the harmony. Mark those changes in the score. When you later roll the figure, you will already know which group belongs to which chord, and you will be less likely to pedal through a change or to accent a passing group as if it were a new arrival.

Roll the figure only after the blocks are secure

Return to the written arpeggio at a slow tempo, with the same fingering, and keep the blocked sound in mind. The first note of each group should still suggest the chord you practised as a block. If the figure smears, go back to one blocked chord and one rolled group, then join two groups. Speed comes after the shapes are clear, not as a way of hiding them.

  • Block each arpeggio group with the fingering you will use when it rolls.
  • Play the blocked chords in order and mark the harmony changes in the score.
  • Balance the blocked sound so an inner note is not lost.
  • Roll one group at a time, then join two groups at the same slow tempo.
  • Add pedal only after the harmonic change is clear in the blocked chain.

A short check in TIGA ALPHA can confirm that the rolled groups still outline the same harmonies you blocked, provided you judge the take against the marks in the score.

When the figure crosses hands or changes direction

Arpeggios that pass from one hand to the other, or that turn back on themselves, still benefit from a blocked map of each harmony. Practise the hand-off as a blocked chord shared between the hands, then as a slow roll through the same shape. If the direction changes in the middle of a harmony, keep that harmony in the ear while the fingers turn. The block is the reference; the roll is the surface.

FAQ

Q: Should every broken-chord accompaniment be blocked first?

A: It is most useful when the harmony changes often, or when the fingering is still unstable. A very familiar pattern may need only a brief blocked reminder.

Q: What if the stretch is too wide to block all the notes at once?

A: Block a comfortable inner group, then add the outer note, or divide the chord between the hands as the later figure will be divided. Write that plan down.

Q: Does blocking slow down learning the written rhythm of the arpeggio?

A: Blocking is a first step, not a substitute. Once the shapes are clear, you still practise the written rhythm of the roll, slowly, with a click if the pulse slips.

Q: How do I use the pedal with blocked practice?

A: Change the pedal with the blocked harmony, then keep those change points when you roll. Do not hold one pedal through two blocked chords that disagree.

Q: The arpeggio is in both hands at once. Do I block each hand separately?

A: Yes, first, so each hand knows its shape. Then block both hands together to hear the full harmony before you roll.
