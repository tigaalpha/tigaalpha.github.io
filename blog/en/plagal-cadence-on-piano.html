The Plagal Cadence on Piano: Approaching the Tonic From IV

Music theory · 2026-11-12

A plagal cadence approaches the tonic from the subdominant, a move often described as IV to I, and sometimes heard as a soft or amen-like close. At the piano it is easy to underplay, so the last two chords pass without feeling like an ending, or to force it, as if it were a weaker substitute for a perfect cadence that must be made dramatic. The useful work is to find the close in the score, voice the two chords so the bass and the inner parts are clear, and let the tonic arrive and hold as a finished thought with its own colour.

Find the subdominant-to-tonic pair and name the bass

Look for a phrase ending in which the harmony moves from IV, or from a related subdominant colour such as iv in the minor, to I. Write the pair of low notes and label each harmony. Play the bass alone, then the full spacing. If the close is decorated with passing notes, find the two essential chords first. Do not confuse a passing IV in the middle of a phrase with a true plagal close at the end of one.

Voice the two chords so the close has weight without force

The subdominant should lead into the tonic, and the tonic should be held for its written value. Balance the top note if it is a melody arrival, and keep inner voices from covering the bass. A sudden accent on I often makes the cadence sound like an imitation of a perfect close. Practise the pair slowly, then in the last phrase, and listen for a finish that is calm and complete rather than stamped.

  • Mark the two chords and name the bass of IV, or iv, moving to I.
  • Play the bass pair alone, then the full spacing, and hold the tonic.
  • Keep the close free of an unwritten accent on the last chord.
  • Practise the cadence at the end of its phrase, not only as two isolated harmonies.
  • If a coda repeats the close, plan the length of each arrival.

Place the cadence at the end of the phrase that earns it

A plagal close belongs to the line before it. Practise from several bars earlier, arrive, and hold. If a coda or a repeated amen-like gesture follows, practise the length of each close so the piece does not fade too soon or go on without a plan. Pedal may join the two chords, but it should not hide a sloppy change of harmony.

Teaching a plagal close beside a perfect cadence

A learner can play a perfect close and a plagal close on the same tonic, and say what is different in the bass and in the colour. Teachers may then ask for the written cadence in the piece, with the last phrase attached. The label is useful once the sound is known. The aim is not to rank one cadence above the other, but to play the one that is written, with a finish that matches its colour.

FAQ

Q: Is a plagal cadence weaker than a perfect cadence?

A: It has a different colour, not a lower rank. Play the written close, and let that colour finish the phrase.

Q: Does it only appear at the end of a piece?

A: It often ends a piece or a section, but a plagal move can also close a phrase inside a movement. Judge by the writing and by whether the tonic feels like an arrival.

Q: What if the subdominant is inverted or decorated?

A: Find the essential IV-to-I, or iv-to-I, then add the written decoration. The bass still needs a clear path to the tonic.

Q: Should I slow down into the last two chords?

A: Only as the phrase or the score suggests. A vague slowing is not a substitute for voicing and for holding the tonic.

Q: How is this different from a perfect cadence?

A: A perfect cadence typically approaches I from V. A plagal cadence approaches I from IV. Compare the two at the piano on the same tonic to hear the difference.
